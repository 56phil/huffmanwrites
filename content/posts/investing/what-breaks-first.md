On the last Thursday in October, more than a trillion dollars of technology market value disappeared in a single session. The Nasdaq 100 fell 3.4 percent, the S&P 500 lost 1.8 percent, the technology sector fell 4.2 percent, and Intel dropped 29 percent, its worst day since the 1980s ([NBC News, 30 October 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/markets/tech-stocks-nasdaq-ai-boom-fears-deepen-rcna606512)). Alphabet lost roughly half a trillion dollars of value in its worst session ever. Six days later, on the Thursday after the midterm elections, the decline became a rout: the Nasdaq 100 fell 6.3 percent and shed $2.3 trillion, the S&P 500 fell 4.4 percent, the Philadelphia semiconductor index fell 12.3 percent in its worst day since March 2020, and Nvidia slid 9.2 percent, turning negative for the year ([NBC News, 5 November 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/markets/tech-stocks-plunge-ai-midterms-rcna607650)). The Nasdaq ended that session 11.9 percent below its record ([AP, 5 November 2026](https://apnews.com/article/stocks-markets-ai-midterm-oil-trump-7ee9c212805aa5e70cf34d25aab7a36e)). By Friday morning the selling had reached Asia, where the Kospi fell 8 percent at the open and triggered a circuit breaker ([BBC News, 6 November 2026](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cmn04705rd8jo)).

Whether that constitutes the bubble bursting is a question about equity prices, and equity prices are the least interesting layer of this structure. A bubble in the ordinary sense is a mispricing that corrects. This one is a payment schedule: five companies committing more than $800 billion of capital expenditure in a single year, an unknown but large share of it borrowed, against revenue that arrives later than the debt does. A correction marks that schedule down. A cascade breaks it, and what breaks first is never the thing everyone is watching.

The useful question, then, is not whether the artificial intelligence trade unwinds. It is what a full unwind would take down, in what order, and which event starts it. That question has an answer built from dates and contracts rather than sentiment, and the calendar is already printed.

## Four conditions, and which of them are already in place

Not every expensive market produces a cascade. Four conditions have to hold at once, and it is worth scoring this one against each.

**Leverage, at a scale that has to be refinanced.** The Bank for International Settlements put the capital expenditure of the five largest United States hyperscalers above $800 billion by the end of 2026, around 2.5 percent of American gross domestic product, with gross bond issuance of $222 billion by September and roughly $75 billion to $100 billion of bonds and asset-backed securities issued through off-balance-sheet vehicles ([BIS Bulletin No. 140, 27 October 2026](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-140-building-ai-credit-structures-and-spillovers.pdf)). The same bulletin cites Moody's estimate that the largest hyperscalers had $1.2 trillion of data-centre lease commitments by mid-2026, of which about $820 billion were for leases that had not yet started. Those are disclosed obligations that are not recorded as debt. The bulletin's warning about what that hides is the one worth carrying: "An insurer that holds a hyperscaler's bonds and the debt of several vehicles leased to it, for example, could be more exposed to that hyperscaler than its bond holdings alone suggest."

**Opacity, so that the loss is discovered rather than priced.** Much of the financing sits in private credit, where loans are marked quarterly by their managers rather than by a market. The OECD reckons artificial intelligence deals were 34 percent of global private credit transactions in 2025, twice the five-year average, and the Bank of England estimates that roughly $700 billion of data-centre spending over 2026 to 2028 will be financed that way, inside a market the Financial Stability Board sized at $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion ([Altaf Kassam, Reuters](https://www.devdiscourse.com/article/international/4006108-roi-private-credits-huge-ai-footprint-obscures-growing-risks-altafkassam)). Blue Owl Capital, one of the largest lenders to data centres, twice capped redemptions from its private credit funds at 5 percent this year.

**Circularity, so that a single disappointment propagates.** Nvidia is supplier, shareholder, and guarantor to the same customers. Its July filing disclosed $36 billion of off-balance-sheet backstops for neoclouds, a guarantee of up to $105 billion on the OpenAI campus in Pike County, Ohio, and $20 billion of future leases signed as a tenant ([CNBC, 24 October 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/24/nvidias-role-as-ai-financier-faces-first-big-test-as-neoclouds-take-market-hit.html)). Total off-balance-sheet commitments exceeded $500 billion, most maturing by fiscal 2029.

**Forced sellers, so that the markdown becomes a sale.** This is the condition that turns a bad quarter into a spiral, and it is the one that has changed most in the past year. In the dot-com bust the marginal buyer was a day trader with a brokerage account. Here the marginal holder of the least transparent debt may be a pension saver in a default fund, and 59 percent of participants in Vanguard-administered workplace plans held their entire account in a single target-date fund in 2024, according to the Financial Stability Board figures Kassam cites.

Three of the four are in place. The fourth, forced selling at scale, is not yet, and that is the whole difference between the November selloff and a cascade. It is also why the bond market matters more than the stock market from here.

## Five triggers, ranked by how soon they can fire

A cascade needs an initiating event that is visible, dated, and large enough to move a mark. Here are the candidates, in order of proximity.

**1. The Anthropic listing, 18 and 19 November.** This is the most consequential of the five, and the first in which the public gets a price. Anthropic launched its roadshow on 10 November at $70 to $78 per share, aiming to price on 18 November and trade on 19 November under the symbol ANT. At the top of the range the company would be worth up to $1.405 trillion, up from the $965 billion valuation it announced in May ([CNBC, 10 November 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/10/anthropic-sets-price-range-of-70-to-78-in-upcoming-ipo.html)). Its amended filing discloses approximately $900.9 billion of future spending commitments, largely on computing infrastructure, of which about $451.6 billion falls due by the end of 2030 and about $70 billion in the next year ([TechTarget, 12 November 2026](https://www.techtarget.com/news/anthropic-ipo-tests-wall-streets-appetite-for-standalone-frontier-ai/874588)). Roughly half of the company's computing commitments sit inside a single agreement worth up to $120.5 billion through 2029 ([CNBC, 11 November 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/11/anthropic-ipo-profit-computing-commitments.html)).

Read the ratio. Anthropic reported run-rate revenue of about $81 billion at the end of October and $33.24 billion of revenue for the nine months to September. Against $900.9 billion of commitments, that is a company selling a decade of capacity obligation against roughly one year of current sales, and it is asking the public market to underwrite the difference at a trillion-dollar valuation. It is also bringing $26 billion of concurrent private placement from its own suppliers, including about $10 billion from Nvidia, into the same deal.

That last detail is the one to hold. If the listing prices below the range, or trades down on day one, then the first public mark on a standalone frontier laboratory is a mark below the private round, and every private valuation in the sector is measured against it: OpenAI's last round, CoreWeave's equity, the data-centre joint ventures whose collateral is a contract with a company now worth less than the model said. If the listing prices well and holds, the funding window stays open through the winter and the entire schedule moves right.

**2. Nvidia's third quarter, 17 November.** One day before the Anthropic pricing, Nvidia reports against guidance of $108 billion in revenue at roughly 74 percent gross margin, followed by a call at 2 p.m. Pacific ([Nvidia investor relations, 28 October 2026](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Sets-Conference-Call-for-Third-Quarter-Financial-Results/default.aspx)). What makes this quarter different from every other is that Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive on 12 November and Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, is running the company as interim chief executive ([Nvidia, 12 November 2026](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-announces-ceo-transition)). Kress built the financing book. She is now the person who has to defend it in her own voice, with no permanent successor named.

Goldman Sachs wrote the day after the resignation that while previously announced commitments would be honored, "it may be more difficult for the company to negotiate new multi-party investments among ecosystem partners at the same scale it has done up to this point" ([CNBC, 13 November 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/13/nvidia-jensen-huang-resignation-ai-financing.html)). Translated: the guarantees stop growing. For a neocloud whose business plan assumes a backstop, that is the difference between a customer and a former customer.

**3. A single refinancing that fails.** CoreWeave carries roughly $51.6 billion of total debt, including about $35 billion in notes, bonds, and loans. Its five-year credit default swaps traded above 1,000 basis points after the resignation, down more than 17 percent on the week but still in territory that ordinarily describes distress, and its 9.75 percent note due in 2031 yielded 14.79 percent (CNBC, 13 November 2026). Oracle's $18 billion of loans on the Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico were quoted at 89 to 91 cents on the dollar in September, after the company sent its landlord a force majeure notice ([Reuters, 18 September 2026](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/oracles-18-billion-data-center-debt-under-pressure-ft-reports-2026-09-18/)). None of these is a default. Each is a borrower paying a rate at which no arithmetic works, and the companies that cannot refinance do not announce it. They stop ordering.

**4. The political channel, which has already fired.** This is the trigger that is no longer hypothetical, and it is the reason the November selloff was about demand rather than about accounting. On 3 November, Democrats who campaigned on data-centre moratoriums won governorships in Texas, Ohio, Georgia, and New Mexico, and at least 42 jurisdictions approved measures to ban or restrict data-centre development (["Data Center Moratoriums and Their Impacts,"](https://octus.com/resources/articles/democrats-backing-data-center-moratoriums-or-tighter-regulations-win-key-gubernatorial-races-voters-in-13-states-reject-data-centers-in-ballot-measures/) Octus, 4 November 2026). Roughly 41 percent of the national planned pipeline sits in states with an existing or proposed moratorium ([NewsNation, 6 November 2026](https://www.newsnationnow.com/business/tech/whats-next-data-centers-texas-political-shift/)). Texas alone accounts for about a quarter of planned United States capacity, and its governor-elect takes office on 19 January 2027.

A moratorium does not destroy the demand for computing. It destroys the schedule. A project that cannot get a permit cannot be leased, a lease that cannot be signed cannot be securitized, and a bond that was priced against a 2028 opening is now priced against nothing. The market figured this out in a day, which is why the independent power producers fell with the chipmakers: Vistra lost about 15 percent and NRG about 12 percent on 4 November (Octus).

**5. The 2027 wall, which is where the arithmetic resolves.** This is the trigger I mapped in October, and it has not moved: capacity stops being scarce when Vera Rubin ships in volume, depreciation on the previous generation arrives on the income statement, and the credit channel prices first. The first honest test of Nvidia's 70 percent growth claim is the quarter reported in May 2027. If the four earlier triggers all fail to fire, that is where the schedule collides with the revenue.

## The cascade, in order

A cascade is not a single crash. It is a sequence in which each step makes the next one cheaper to trigger, and the order is set by which party has to mark a loss first. Here is the sequence I would expect, with the tell that says it has started.

| Step | What breaks | The tell |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Equity in the financers, not the founders | Neocloud and data-centre developer stock falls while hyperscalers hold |
| 2 | Spreads on securitized leases and neocloud debt | CoreWeave CDS through 1,000 and staying there; ABS spreads widen |
| 3 | The guarantee book | A new chief executive restates or caps Nvidia's backstops and residual-value commitments |
| 4 | Equipment orders | A hyperscaler guides 2027 capital spending flat instead of up |
| 5 | Private credit marks | A second manager gates redemptions; a loan is marked below 90 |
| 6 | The physical layer | Data-centre construction spending turns; electrical and mechanical backlogs shrink |
| 7 | The bill for the grid | A state refuses to let ratepayers absorb the capacity cost of projects that never opened |
| 8 | The household | Index concentration transmits the loss to retirement accounts, and consumption follows |

Two steps in that table deserve more than a row.

**The guarantee book is the hinge.** Nvidia's $500 billion of off-balance-sheet commitments and its partnership with six asset managers on a $500 billion financing push, including up to $125 billion of residual-value guarantees on GPU financings, exist because a supplier with $70 billion of half-year free cash flow can lower its customers' cost of capital. That is the mechanism by which a chip company became the banking system of its own industry. A new chief executive with no founder's authority and an open investigation has every incentive to shrink it rather than extend it, and the first public sign will be a guarantee renewed at a smaller size rather than at the same one.

**The grid bill is the step that turns a market event into a political one.** The PJM Interconnection market monitor reported on 12 November that its December capacity auction will again clear at the price cap, adding $5 billion to $10 billion of data-centre costs to electricity bills across a region serving 67 million people, and that capacity cost rose more than 80 percent in the first nine months of 2026 compared with the same period last year ([Pennsylvania Capital-Star, 12 November 2026](https://penncapital-star.com/energy-environment/electricity-grid-watchdog-warns-of-higher-prices-after-delayed-pjm-market-reforms/)). Those costs are being collected now, from ratepayers, for projects that may not be built. That is the arrangement a newly elected governor was sent to end, and the fight over who absorbs a stranded substation is precisely the fight that makes a buildout stop.

## What the economy absorbs

The honest answer is that the macroeconomic damage depends on how much of the spending stops, and the estimates are narrower than the rhetoric.

The Congressional Research Service summarized the mechanism on 12 November: artificial intelligence investment "has likely also put upward pressure on inflation, interest rates, and imports, slowing other parts of the economy," and "if AI investment proves to be a bubble that subsequently bursts, it could be destabilizing for the economy" ([CRS In Focus IF13346](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-11-12_IF13346_698515681624c3b64c5acc0e8377e2c4926f08f3.pdf)). Its own arithmetic is sobering in the other direction: AI-related investment accounted for about 39 percent of real gross domestic product growth in the first nine months of 2025, more than at the peak of the dot-com boom, and the four largest hyperscalers are absorbing nearly all of their operating cash flow to fund it ([New Capital, September 2026](https://www.newcapital.com/doc/jcr:5f974afa-cf65-479a-bc28-e313c7c82f1b/InFocus_Hyperscalers_NC.pdf/lang:en/InFocus_Hyperscalers_NC.pdf)).

That same analysis runs the counterfactual. A 30 percent cut in hyperscaler capital spending, about $219 billion, subtracts 0.27 to 0.40 percentage points from growth directly, depending on how much of the spending is domestic value added, and 0.5 to 1 percentage point once employment, construction, and the market effect are included. That is enough to bring growth close to stagnation if it coincides with another shock, and not enough to produce a depression on its own. Which is the point: the technology is not the risk. The concentration of growth in one industry is.

## What does not happen

The comparison that keeps being drawn is 2000, and the useful half of that comparison is not the crash. It is the decade after it.

In 2002, defaults on corporate bonds worldwide reached $163 billion, of which 56.4 percent were in the telecommunications sector, including investment-grade companies, after WorldCom restated $3.8 billion and Global Crossing filed for bankruptcy. The OECD's assessment was that the sector's restructuring had "severe upstream effects on equipment suppliers and technology firms" while its impact on the overall economy was "relatively small" ([OECD Working Paper No. 361, 2003](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2003/06/after-the-telecommunications-bubble_g17a1480/311813664474.pdf)). The fibre built in that mania was not wasted. It was used, at a price that destroyed the people who financed it.

Two differences are worth stating because they cut in opposite directions.

The first makes a sudden collapse less likely. The dot-com boom was financed by equity and by young companies with no revenue. This one is financed largely by the most profitable companies in the world, whose own cash flow covers most of the spending, which is why the CRS can describe the risk as destabilizing rather than systemic.

The second makes a slow grind more likely. The losses are held in instruments that do not trade, by institutions that mark them quarterly, and the holders include pension funds and insurers who will not see the loss as it happens. Kassam's line is the one to remember: "If this one ends badly, the late money could arrive through pensions, at prices set by models."

## What to watch, by date

This is a schedule, not a forecast. Each date is a place where a mark has to be made.

| Date | Event | What it tests |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 17 November | Nvidia's third quarter, $108 billion guidance | Whether an interim chief executive restates the financing book |
| 18 to 19 November | Anthropic prices and trades as ANT | The first public mark on a standalone frontier lab, and on $900.9 billion of commitments |
| 8 to 9 December | Federal Reserve decision | A hike priced near 60 percent, against a market that just lost $2.8 trillion |
| 11 December | The continuing resolution expires | A funding cliff inside the same week as the rate decision |
| January 2027 | The new Congress is seated; Texas changes governors on 19 January | Whether moratoriums become permits policy |
| February 2027 | Hyperscaler fourth quarters; Nvidia's fiscal year-end | The 2027 capital spending number, in writing |
| May 2027 | Nvidia's first quarter of fiscal 2028 | Whether 70 percent growth was supply or demand |

The tells are more useful than the dates, because the dates will be reported and the tells have to be read. A hyperscaler guiding 2027 capital spending flat rather than up. CoreWeave's five-year swaps holding above 1,000 into a refinancing window. A private credit fund gating redemptions for a second consecutive quarter. A guarantee renewed at a smaller size. A rating agency treating an unstarted lease as debt.

The bond price moves before the headline, and it moves before the layoff. Anyone who wants to know whether this is a correction or the beginning of something longer should stop reading the stock market and start reading the credit market, because that is where the schedule is written and where the first broken promise will appear.

## What a careful reader does with this

I hold all of the above as a scenario, not a prediction, and the difference matters to what follows.

The premise is not that artificial intelligence is worthless. It is that the industry has financed a five-year build against a revenue curve that arrives later than the debt does, and it has now added a political constraint on the schedule. The technology will be there on the other side at a lower price, the way the fibre was. The question is who pays for the interval.

Three things follow, and none of them requires a view on the date.

Know what you own. If you hold an index fund, you hold the seven largest technology companies at roughly a third of the index by weight, and you hold them whether or not you chose them. That is not an argument for selling. It is an argument for knowing the number.

Treat the concentrated position as a position. Size whatever you own in the trade so that a gap of 20 percent on a single Tuesday does not change your plan, because that gap has now happened twice in six weeks.

Read the credit market, and keep some cash earning. The unwind, if it comes, will not announce itself with a headline about a bubble. It will announce itself with a spread, a yield, or a gate, and a reader who is watching the right instrument will have days rather than minutes to think. That is the only advantage available, and it is enough.

The collapse of a bubble is not a single event. It is an order of operations, and the first item on the list is always the same: the cheapest claim on the promise gets repriced, and then the repricing has to be admitted by whoever is holding it.
