## Ninety Days From Today

Count it out on a calendar. Ninety days from this morning is **February 22**, a Monday, which closes this window on the far side of a new year and a new Congress. Between here and there sit a Thanksgiving on **November 26**; an OPEC+ meeting on 2027 output policy on **November 29**; two Federal Reserve meetings, on **December 8 and 9** with a fresh set of projections and again on **January 26 and 27** without one; November employment on **December 4**; November inflation on **December 10**; a government funding deadline on **December 11**; a White House hearing on the removal of Governor Lisa Cook on **December 14**; a Christmas on **December 25**; a new Congress seated on **January 3**; December employment on **January 8** and December inflation on **January 13**; and the Treasury's next quarterly refunding in early February ([BLS release schedule](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/2026/12_sched_list.htm); [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/6500); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/04/trump-federal-reserve-lisa-cook-hearing.html)).

The premise is unchanged: no improvement in the war, no breakthrough, no formal reopening of the strait. What changed in November is that the premise held and the price did not.

Brent settled October 30 at **$117.64**. It settled Friday at **$92.57** and traded this morning at **$88.62**, its lowest since August 27, with WTI at **$79.53**, its weakest since August 10 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/20/oil-wti-brent-hormuz.html); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/24/oil-wti-brent-hormuz-supply.html)). Hormuz is still closed. Iran's army chief has ordered it "closed further day by day" until Tehran's seven conditions are met, the Revolutionary Guard is still claiming that tankers struck mines in the strait, and President Trump has said strikes could resume after the New Year ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/24/iran-orders-hormuz-closed-further-day-by-day-as-qatar-seeks-to-revive-peace-talks)). Qatar's prime minister flew to Washington to try to restart talks and came away with a ten-minute meeting and no deal.

So the war did not improve. The oil market simply found enough supply elsewhere to stop paying the war premium. Crude supply has been rising, Libya's Sharara pipeline has reopened, China and India have been buying record volumes of discounted Russian barrels, and Deutsche Bank estimated a global oversupply of about **1.7 million barrels a day** in October ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/24/oil-wti-brent-hormuz-supply.html)). A closed strait and a falling oil price can both be true at once. That is the first fact of this window, and it is the one the previous installment did not anticipate.

The second fact is that the Fed is going to hike anyway.

## Where the Market Stands Today

The numbers as of the November 23 close, with the October 30 close beside them for the month's move.

- The **10-year Treasury** closed at **4.92%**, down **two basis points** from **4.94%**. It rose as high as **5.15%** on November 13 and gave all of that back in seven sessions ([U.S. Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?type=daily_treasury_yield_curve&field_tdr_date_value_month=202611)).
- The **30-year** closed at **5.29%**, down **two basis points** from **5.31%**. Its November high was **5.47%** on November 13.
- The **2-year** closed at **4.63%**, up **seven basis points** from **4.56%**. The front end is the only part of the curve that ended the month higher.
- The **5-year** rose **three basis points** to **4.74%** and the **7-year** one to **4.81%**.
- The **2s10s spread** closed at **29 basis points**, down from **38** at the end of October. The curve flattened by nine basis points, and it did so because the front end rose while the long end did not.
- The **10s30s spread** closed at **37 basis points**, exactly where it began the month.
- The **20-year still sits above the 30-year**, at **5.33%** against **5.29%**. That inversion has now persisted for months.
- The **federal funds target range is 3.75% to 4%**. The market prices a quarter-point hike at the December 8 and 9 meeting at roughly **98%**, per CME FedWatch; right after the October inflation report it put about a **13.6%** chance on a half-point move ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/treasury-yields-bonds-fed.html); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/10/cpi-inflation-report-october-2026.html)).
- **October payrolls rose 165,000** against an estimate near 59,000, and unemployment fell to **4.1%**. Average hourly earnings rose **3.1%** on the year, and the prior two months were revised up by a combined **84,000** ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/06/jobs-report-october-2026.html)).
- **October consumer prices rose 0.4%**, with core up **0.3%** for the third straight month. The BLS could not publish a twelve-month figure because last October's data were never collected during the shutdown; independent estimates put the annual rate near **4%** ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/10/cpi-inflation-report-october-2026.html); [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/10/inflation-cpi-interest-rates)).
- The average **30-year mortgage is 7.28%**, up from **7.11%** the prior week and **6.26%** a year ago ([Freddie Mac](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms)).
- **Brent is $88.62** and **WTI $79.53** this morning, down roughly a quarter from the October 30 settlement.

Read the first, third, and tenth bullets together. The ten-year ended the month two basis points lower. The two-year ended it seven basis points higher. The ten-year spent the middle of the month at 5.15% and the last week falling. A market that can do all of that in four weeks and arrive almost exactly where it started is not a market with a view. It is a market with two views that cancel.

## The Round Trip

The ten-year's path this month is worth walking through, because the sequence explains more than the level does.

On **November 4** the Treasury refunded. On **November 5** the ten-year touched **4.84%**, its low for the month. On **November 6** the October jobs report came in at 165,000 and the ten-year rose two basis points. On **November 10** the October CPI printed at 0.4% and 0.3%, and the ten-year rose ten basis points to 4.98%. By **November 13** it had reached **5.15%**, its highest since the spring, as the market moved to fully price a December hike and began to price a second one in January. The two-year rose from 4.46% on November 5 to 4.88% on November 13, a move of forty-two basis points over the next five sessions.

Then the equity market broke. The S&P 500 fell for seven straight sessions through **November 20**, its worst week since early 2020, and on that Friday the ten-year fell **twelve basis points** in a day, from 5.04% to 4.92%. On **November 23** the index had its best day since April 2025, rising 4.73%, and the ten-year did not move at all. The long end stayed exactly where it was.

That last sentence is the most important one in this article. On the best equity day in nineteen months, with a December hike all but certain, the ten-year closed at 4.92% and the thirty-year at 5.29%. The long end refused to sell off into a rally. It also refused to rally into a crash, at least beyond a single session.

What does that tell you? It tells you the long end has found a floor and a ceiling, and neither one is set by the war. The floor is the yield at which buyers who remember 2007 are willing to own duration. The ceiling is the yield at which the same buyers decide that a four percent inflation rate and a Treasury funding a war in its tenth month are not worth the coupon. For now those two numbers are roughly 4.8% and 5.2% on the ten-year, and the market spent the month discovering them the hard way.

The other thing the round trip tells you is that the bond market has become an equity hedge. In October the ten-year rallied seventeen basis points in a session when AI stocks fell. In November it rallied twelve basis points in a session when AI stocks fell, then held still when they rebounded. The sign of the stock-bond correlation has flipped from the wartime positive, where both priced the same inflation shock, to the peacetime negative, where bonds are the thing you buy when the equity trade breaks. That is a healthier relationship in the abstract. It is also a fragile one, because it makes the long end's yield a function of the equity market's mood rather than of the government's credit.

## The Curve Flattened, and the Long End Did Not Care

The previous installment set a test for the 2s10s and the market answered it, though not in the direction the test expected.

The test was whether the curve would keep steepening, which would mean the market expected the Fed to stop before it won, or flatten back toward 27 basis points, which would mean the market expected the Fed to keep going. It flattened, from **38 to 29**. By the letter of the test, the market expects the Fed to keep going, and the November data support that: a blowout payroll report, a third straight 0.3% core CPI, and a Fed chair who has promised to end rising prices.

But the composition matters. This was a **bear flattening** for the first half of the month, with the front end rising on the data, and a **bull-ish flattening** in the last week, with the long end falling on the equity crash. The net is a curve that is nine basis points flatter and a market that is no more confident than it was. The two-year at 4.63% is pricing roughly two hikes. The ten-year at 4.92% is pricing a Fed that hikes twice and then stops, an inflation rate that peaks near four percent, and an oil price that keeps falling. Those are not incompatible. They are just a lot of assumptions for a two-basis-point monthly move.

The 10s30s is the part of the curve that did nothing, and its stillness is now the most informative thing about it. At the end of September it was 35 basis points, at the end of October 37, and today 37. In between, the Treasury dropped the phrase "at least" from its auction-size guidance, extended its buyback program, and watched the equity market lose and regain a trillion dollars of technology value. None of it moved the spread. The gap between the ten and the thirty is not a term-premium signal right now, because a term premium that could move would have moved. It is a number that reflects a market pricing one inflation regime across every maturity long enough to be hurt by it.

The 20-year above the 30-year remains the quietest signal on the board. The market charges **four basis points more** for twenty years than for thirty. That is not a normal curve, and it is not a recession signal. It is what a buyer does when the far end of the curve has stopped being about inflation and started being about who will hold the paper. The answer, increasingly, is the Treasury itself.

## The Buyback Scorecard, Fifth Entry

This section exists to hold one claim accountable: buying your own bonds does not lower the rate at which the market will lend to you for thirty years.

November gave the claim its cleanest test yet, and the result was a draw.

On **November 4** the Treasury kept its coupon auction sizes unchanged at **$58 billion** of three-year notes, **$42 billion** of ten-year notes, and **$25 billion** of thirty-year bonds, refunding about **$97 billion** and raising about **$28 billion** in new cash. It also extended its buyback capacity to **$70 billion** for liquidity support and **$25 billion** for cash management in the November-to-January quarter ([Treasury refunding statement](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0678)). And it dropped the phrase "at least" from its guidance that coupon sizes would stay steady "for the next several quarters," a change that analysts read as opening the door to cutting twenty-year and thirty-year auction sizes later ([Reuters via Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/economy-news/us-treasury-shifts-language-opening-door-to-auction-size-changes-4999444)).

The previous installment named that language change as the third and most important thing to watch, and it happened. JPMorgan had said in July that the Treasury would avoid rattling the bond market before the midterms, holding off on any change to its auction guidance rather than signaling larger sales into an election ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/jpmorgan-sees-treasury-avoiding-bond-sales-tweak-due-to-midterms)). The curve did not steepen. The 10s30s ended the month at 37 basis points, unchanged, and the thirty-year ended two basis points lower than it began.

So the scorecard reads: the Treasury did the supply-side thing, and the long end shrugged. That is not an argument that the program is useless. It is an argument about what the long end is currently pricing. A market that can absorb a dovish supply signal, a $70 billion buyback program, and the worst equity week since 2020 without moving its thirty-year yield is not a market waiting for the Treasury to show up. It is a market that has already decided the Treasury is not the marginal buyer. The marginal buyer, this month, was whoever was selling semiconductors.

The honest conclusion after five entries is that buybacks are a liquidity tool with a term-premium story attached, and the story is doing less work than the tool. The dealers who intermediate the auctions benefit. The pension fund that has to match a thirty-year liability benefits from the slightly deeper market. The borrower who wants a thirty-year mortgage does not, because the mortgage rate went from 7.11% to 7.28% in the same month the buybacks were extended.

## The Governance Question

This series treats the rule of law as a standing input to the term premium rather than a political aside. November supplied three items, and the largest one is now measurable in dollars.

**The tariff refund is a $128 billion distribution made by filing date.** Customs had certified and sent **$128.4 billion** to the Treasury for disbursement as of November 5, out of about **$138.3 billion** accepted for processing, and **18,072 refunds worth roughly $737 million** had not gone out because the importer or its agent had not supplied bank details ([J.M. Rodgers](https://www.jmrodgers.com/news/tariff-watch-november-16-2026-ieepa-refunds/)). The underlying pool is about **$166 billion** collected from roughly **330,000 importers** on about **53 million entry summaries**, which the Supreme Court held in February the emergency statute did not authorize ([Representative Jill Tokuda](https://tokuda.house.gov/media/press-releases/tokuda-warnock-and-sherman-call-for-federal-audit-of-refunds-of-166-billion-in-illegal-trump-tariffs)). Senator Raphael Warnock, Representative Tokuda, and Representative Brad Sherman asked the Government Accountability Office in October to audit whether the refunds are complete, accurate, and protected against duplicate payments, and whether any of the benefit reached the consumers who paid the surcharge ([Senator Raphael Warnock](https://www.warnock.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warnock-colleagues-launch-bicameral-inquiry-into-trump-administrations-handling-of-tariff-refunds/)). The Justice Department is still arguing that relief belongs only to importers who sued, while Judge Richard Eaton's October 8 class certification opens a path for those who did not ([ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/customs-import-compliance-blog/the-customs-and-trade-world-turns-october-2026)).

Two importers with identical claims still face different outcomes because one filed and one did not, and nobody can tell you today which share of the $166 billion will be resolved by a rule and which by a lawyer. That is the material of a term premium: not a bribe, but a rule that produces different answers for identical facts and cannot explain why.

**The exemption channel remains pharmaceutical.** Under Proclamation 11020, patented drugs from most countries face duties of up to **100%**, while nine specialty categories pay **0%** when the product comes from one of nineteen named jurisdictions or when Commerce grants an "urgent health need" determination case by case ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/23/2026-19498/guidance-and-procedures-for-implementing-tariff-adjustments-for-specialty-pharmaceuticals-and)). The categories are defensible on the merits. The discretion is the price, and the *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* has measured what that price looked like in the older tariff exemption program: contributions to the party in power raised the odds of approval, and each grant was worth a median of about **$51 million** in firm value ([JFQA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6777AC0EB32328B3E45A617F9B3E07E5/S0022109024000437a.pdf/div-class-title-the-political-economy-of-tariff-exemption-grants-div.pdf)).

**And the Fed fight has a date again.** The White House moved its hearing on Governor Cook from November 5 to **December 14**. Cook's lawyer, Abbe Lowell, wrote that she has "no legal obligation to participate in an unfair hearing" and called the panel "a façade designed to obscure a predetermined decision to fire Governor Cook" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/17/lisa-cook-trump-fed-committee-hearing.html)). The committee can only recommend removal; the president decides. A proceeding aimed at the board's least dovish member is not a case about a mortgage. It is a case about who sets the price of money, and December 14 puts it inside this window.

The midterm result adds a fourth variable. Democrats won the House and the Senate, taking full control of Congress in January ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/midterm-election-house-senate-democrats-republicans-trump-f126e12e05b579a327b98481c0ae2226)). Divided government usually reads as gridlock, and gridlock usually reads as lower political risk. But a Congress with subpoena power over tariff refunds, pharmaceutical waivers, and the Fed is also a Congress that will keep those questions in the news. The premium does not necessarily fall when the oversight begins. It changes hands.

## The Ninety-Day Map

Take the premise seriously: no improvement in the war, no breakthrough, no formal reopening of the strait. Here is the path from November 24 to February 22.

**November 26 through December 4.** Thanksgiving, then OPEC+ on November 29, then November employment on December 4. The jobs report is the first real test of whether October's 165,000 was a blip or a trend. A second strong print and the January hike moves from a coin flip to a base case.

**December 8 through December 14.** The cluster. The Fed hikes on the ninth and publishes a fresh dot plot. November inflation lands on the tenth. The funding deadline is the eleventh, with another short-term stopgap into early 2027 the likeliest outcome. The Cook hearing is the fourteenth. A rate decision, an inflation print, a funding cliff, and a removal proceeding inside one week.

**January 3 through January 27.** A new Congress is seated, December employment and inflation land in the second week, and the Fed meets on the twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh with no projections. That meeting is where the market decides whether December was the only hike or the first of several.

**February.** The Treasury refunds again in early February, and the window closes on the twenty-second. By then the market will know whether the oil collapse fed through to core goods prices, and whether the Fed's two hikes have started to bite on the labor market.

That is the base case, and it lands the ten-year between **4.5% and 5.1%**, the thirty-year between **4.9% and 5.5%**, mortgages between **6.9% and 7.5%**, the 2s10s between **15 and 30 basis points**, and the 10s30s between **30 and 45**.

Two branches sit on either side of it.

**The escalation branch.** If the talks collapse, the blockade tightens, or strikes resume after the New Year and take Gulf output with them, oil goes back through $110 and the inflation impulse restarts from a higher base. The first move will look like October: yields fall on the risk event. The second move is the one that matters. A Fed that has already hiked twice into a four percent inflation rate cannot cut into a fresh supply shock without losing the room, and the 1979 precedent is not about the first month. It is about what a central bank does when a supply shock refuses to leave. In that world the ten-year tests **5.5% to 5.8%**, the thirty-year **5.7% to 6.2%**, and mortgages **7.6% to 8.0%**.

**The paradox branch.** The other branch is a rally that arrives because the AI buildout fails. If a hyperscaler cuts capital spending, or a large AI borrower misses a payment, or the equity market's appetite for AI paper closes, the economy loses its marginal source of demand and its marginal borrower at the same time. That is disinflationary, and it is exactly the kind of shock that produces a genuine flight to quality rather than a one-week one. The ten-year could fall to **4.2% to 4.5%** and the thirty-year to **4.6% to 5.0%**, with the curve bull-steepening as the front end prices cuts the Fed has not yet made. It would be the most expensive kind of rally if the Fed is only pausing, and the right kind if the economy is rolling over. The tell is the 2s10s: a move through 50 basis points would say the market has stopped pricing hikes and started pricing cuts.

The base case is not a forecast that nothing happens. It is a forecast that the two forces now in the market, a Fed that must hike and an oil price that is falling, roughly cancel, and that the long end spends the winter discovering that neither one is strong enough to set the yield on its own.

## What a Careful Investor Does

None of this is a forecast that yields fall in a straight line. It is a statement about what changed and what did not. What changed is that oil is a quarter cheaper without a peace deal, and the bond market has become an equity hedge. What did not change is that the inflation impulse is still running near four percent, the Fed is still hiking, and the deficit is still being funded into a market that has just demonstrated how fast it can turn.

- **Do not chase the long end on the equity crash.** The October and November rallies were real, and both were borrowed from the stock market. A thirty-year at 5.29% pays four basis points less than the twenty-year, which is a bad trade for money you might need and a reasonable one only for a liability twenty years out.
- **Take the belly seriously.** The five-year and seven-year are where the market prices the peak of the cycle, and the peak has moved closer as the data strengthened. A ladder weighted toward three to seven years captures the repricing without betting the whole position on the Fed stopping.
- **Own the front end as income, not as a trade.** A two-year at 4.63% with a hike coming is not a bargain. It is a place to park cash while the long end decides what it is.
- **Keep inflation protection while it is cheap relative to the risk.** TIPS breakevens are still pricing a world in which the war premium fades. If the escalation branch is right, that is the cheapest insurance in the market. If the paradox branch is right, the insurance costs little and the nominal bond does the work.
- **Keep the ladder, and keep some powder.** A ladder converts a two-branch map into a schedule. The cash is for the escalation branch, which arrives as a headline on a Saturday, and for the paradox branch, which arrives as a gap in the equity market on a Tuesday.

## The Long Way of Saying It

The bond market is a referendum on whether a government's promises are worth holding, and this month the vote did not move. The ten-year began at 4.94% and ended at 4.92%. Everything that happened in between, the blowout jobs report, the third hot inflation print, the worst equity week since 2020, the best equity day since 2025, a quarter off the oil price, and a Treasury that quietly opened the door to smaller long auctions, netted out to two basis points.

That stillness is easy to read as calm. It is not calm. It is a market with two strong arguments that happen to point in opposite directions, and the winter will decide which one is stronger. The argument for lower yields is that the war's inflation impulse has finally met a supply response, and that the AI trade, which financed itself on the assumption that capital was free, is now repricing the cost of that capital in public. The argument for higher yields is that a Fed hiking into four percent inflation will not stop at two moves, and that a government funding a war and a $40 trillion debt will keep asking the bond market to absorb both.

The clue this month is not in the strait. It is in the fact that the safest asset in the world rallied on the worst equity week in six years and then refused to sell off on the best equity day in nineteen months. That is not a market pricing peace or war. It is a market pricing the end of the AI borrowing cycle, one way or the other.

Storms do not send invitations, but they do leave clues. This month the clue is that the tide went out while the sky stayed black. Build the defenses before the lightning strikes, and notice that the waterline moved before the weather did.
