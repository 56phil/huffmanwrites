The Federal Aviation Administration stopped every departure in the United States at 3:54 p.m. eastern time on Friday and did not let the last of them go until 8:19 p.m., four hours and twenty-five minutes by the Command Center's own two signatures. The advisory that started it states no cause. Its only causal language is boilerplate: "DUE TO EXTRAORDINARY CIRCUMSTANCES AND FOR REASONS OF SAFETY." The advisory that ended it carries a REASON field that is present and blank. In between, the agency told the public that "communication issues impacting multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers" had forced the stop, and the re-issue that evening named the reason as COMMUNICATIONS FAILURE. The Secretary of Homeland Security, Markwayne Mullin, said there was no indication of a cyber-attack. Transportation Secretary Duffy said the outage would ripple for days. By Saturday morning FlightAware had counted more than 8,860 cancellations to or from the United States on Friday and about 2,650 more on Saturday. It was the longest nationwide ground stop since the attacks of September 11, 2001.

## The administration and the rule of law

The same agency described the same event in several ways. The Command Center's first advisory told controllers that "ALL AIRPORTS ARE GROUND STOPPED." The FAA's public statement said the stop followed communication issues at "multiple" centers. Its post on X described a temporary telecommunications outage. The Command Center's own operations system classifies the re-issued stop as EQUIPMENT / OUTAGE, while the free text beside the label reads COMMUNICATIONS FAILURE. None of them is a diagnosis. The agency's statement is marked "preliminary and subject to change," and no root-cause document has been published.

The record of the stop is unusually thin in one respect and unusually complete in another. Every numbered advisory survives, so the sequence can be read to the minute. What none of them supplies is why. The order that grounded the country carries no reason field at all, and the cancellation that lifted it leaves the field empty rather than saying the fault was cleared. A reader can know exactly when the country stopped flying and not one word about what stopped it.

The agency has been told before that its systems are not fully secured. In April the Transportation Department's inspector general published [FAA Does Not Effectively Secure Its High-Impact Systems Supporting the National Airspace System](https://www.oig.dot.gov/library-item/47238), report FI2026023. It found that 15 of the 45 high-impact systems reviewed had selected security controls under an outdated standard, and that the agency "had not fully implemented 1,836 (11.3 percent) of the 16,245 required controls for the 45 systems." The public version of the report is redacted for sensitive security information. That audit concerns security controls, not the communications failure of Friday, and it does not establish what went wrong; the agency has not said.

## Markets

Markets were closed on Saturday, so the board is Friday's close. The S&P 500 finished at 7,561.67, down 30.73 points or 0.40 percent; the Nasdaq Composite at 25,136.273, down 229.82 or 0.91 percent; the Dow Jones Industrial Average at 52,347.55, up 50.46 or 0.10 percent. The VIX closed at 19.30, up 0.60 or 3.21 percent; gold at $3,887.60 an ounce, up $2.30 or 0.06 percent; and the dollar index at 103.411, up 0.238 or 0.23 percent. The ten-year Treasury yield was 5.139 percent at 5:03 p.m. eastern, up 3.6 basis points on the day.

Treasury's daily curve for November 13 ran at 4.88 percent for two years, 5.15 for ten and 5.47 for thirty, a 2s10s spread of +0.27 against +0.24 the day before. All three tenors rose, the ten-year by five basis points and the two-year by two, so the curve steepened as it climbed.

Total public debt outstanding was $40,608,506,856,903 on November 12, up $24,376,776,630 from November 10, the prior business day. Treasury posted nothing for November 11. Of the total, $32,824,386,143,425 was held by the public and $7,784,120,713,478 was intragovernmental.

## The economy

The Bureau of Labor Statistics series has not moved, and no new release has landed since the October figures. The all-items consumer price index was 337.511 for October, a monthly rise of 0.17 percent; unemployment was 4.1 percent, a tenth below September; and total nonfarm payrolls were 159,293 thousand, 165 thousand above September. Prices decelerated in October while the labor market did not loosen. One month is not a trend, and this report has nothing to add until the series moves.

## Elections and the midterms

Mississippi's 82 counties transmitted certified results to the Secretary of State on Friday, the deadline toward which the count had been building. The secretary's office, as [WLBT reported](https://www.wlbt.com/2026/11/14/ms-secretary-state-releases-final-election-numbers/), put Scott Colom at 479,404 votes, or 49.3 percent, to 471,817, or 48.6 percent, for Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith, a margin of 7,587. The independent Ty Pinkins took 20,439, or 2.1 percent. The state processed 21,344 affidavit ballots and accepted about 73 percent of them. The Associated Press called the race for Colom on Wednesday, when he led by 4,669 with fewer than 12,000 ballots still under review; the completed county returns widened the margin to 7,587. Mississippi has no automatic recount law, so a challenge would need a court order. The secretary of state's office is scheduled to declare the statewide result on December 3.

Alaska is the last Senate race uncalled. [Alaska Public Media reported Friday](https://alaskapublic.org/news/politics/elections/2026-11-13/peltola-leads-in-u-s-senate-race-nearing-50-mark-with-almost-all-ballots-counted) that Mary Peltola stood at 49.8 percent and Senator Dan Sullivan at 46.1 percent with almost all ballots counted, and that the Division of Elections tabulates the rankings and declares unofficial winners on November 18.

Polymarket priced the remainder. Peltola stood at 99.8 percent on $132,011 of twenty-four-hour volume, with Sullivan at 0.1 percent. Chuck Schumer stood at 86.5 percent to be Senate majority leader against 12.7 percent for Chris Van Hollen. The venue put the Democratic House popular-vote margin between 14 and 16 points at 93.2 percent. Those are prices, not results.

## The world

Ukraine's president said Saturday that American negotiators have floated a trade: Ukraine withdraws its remaining forces from the Donbas in exchange for NATO membership. "The Americans raised a question: 'What if Ukraine joins Nato? Would Ukraine withdraw from Donbas?' This was the first time this had been raised," Volodymyr Zelensky told reporters. The idea runs against a stated American position, since United States officials had previously said that Ukrainian membership was off the table, and against Russia's own terms, which have demanded the Donbas and Ukrainian neutrality rather than an alliance guarantee.

Zelensky attached conditions of his own. He said that if a proposal carried security guarantees, no constraints on Ukraine's military and money to rebuild the country, he would put it to a referendum. That is a way of moving the decision off his desk without rejecting it.

The envoys who carried the question, Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, met President Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Thursday and Zelensky in Dublin the same day, before a European summit that was meant to settle who negotiates for Europe. The Kremlin's public position has not moved: Putin and his officials continue to demand territory and neutrality that exceed what Kyiv has said it will consider. Whether the offer is a real American position or a trial balloon is the thing to watch.
