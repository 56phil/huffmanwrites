Brazil votes today in a presidential runoff, and it is the only item on the day's record that is actually moving. Nearly 160 million citizens are eligible to vote for president, with polls open from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. local time (11:00 to 20:00 GMT). The runoff sets the incumbent, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, against Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, and it follows a first round on October 4 that Bolsonaro led 47 percent to 45 with about a fifth of voters abstaining. Final polling described the two as neck and neck, with Lula said to have gained ground over three weeks of campaigning.

Washington, by contrast, recorded nothing. The Federal Register published no documents for October 24 or 25. The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit filed no opinions. No new complaints were docketed in the District of Columbia. None of the five watched dockets moved. Markets have not traded since Friday, when the S&P 500 fell 0.52 percent and the Dow 1.23 percent, the ten-year Treasury yield closed at 5.00 percent, and total public debt stood at $40,444,342,709,389.

A quiet American weekend is a real finding, not a missing one.

## The administration and the rule of law

The Federal Register carried zero documents in the October 24 to 25 window: no presidential documents, no rules, no proposed rules and no notices. The weekend is routinely the thinnest part of the register's week, so the number says more about the calendar than about the government. Nothing is withheld and nothing is missing; the window simply has nothing in it. Friday's issue carried the week's last weekday documents, and yesterday's edition covered it.

## Elections and the midterms

Polymarket's chamber markets on Sunday morning put the House at 91.5 percent Democratic on $113,503 of twenty-four-hour volume against 8.5 percent Republican on $94,026, and the Senate at 66.5 percent Democratic on $70,079 against 33.5 percent Republican on $103,241.

The balance-of-power event told a more divided story. A Democratic sweep traded at 66.5 percent on $118,375, a Republican Senate with a Democratic House at 25.5 percent on $77,791, and a Republican sweep at 7.5 percent on $837,145. That last row is worth naming: the Republican sweep carries the largest twenty-four-hour volume of any midterm market in the pack and the lowest price. Volume and probability are different measurements, and a heavily traded long shot is still a long shot. A Democratic Senate with a Republican House priced at 0.8 percent on $176,224.

In the state races, Michigan gave Democrat Abdul El-Sayed 71.5 percent on $141,445 against Republican Mike Rogers at 28.5 on $64,340. Texas gave Democrat James Talarico 63.5 percent against Republican Ken Paxton at 36.5, and Maine gave Democrat Troy Jackson 63.5. North Carolina's 13th District put Democrat Paul Barringer at 44.0 percent.

These are the venues' prices, not this publication's view, and a thin book is not a forecast.

## The world

Brazil's runoff is the day's largest event outside the United States. Lula, 80, is seeking a fourth non-consecutive term. Flávio Bolsonaro is a senator and the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year sentence under house detention for staging an attempted coup. The younger Bolsonaro has promised 500,000 more prison places and spending cuts, and he is under investigation for corruption, money laundering and tax evasion. A Bolsonaro victory would align Brazil with Trump-friendly regional governments, a prospect that coverage of the race ties to American tariffs and to Washington's terrorist designation of Brazilian drug groups.

The prediction markets price the race very differently from the polls. Polymarket gave Flávio Bolsonaro 79.9 percent on $1,743,016 of twenty-four-hour volume and Lula 20.5 percent on $1,308,615, figures recorded before any result. A tied race and a market at roughly four to one point in different directions, and the count after polls close at 5 p.m. local (20:00 GMT) will settle which read the electorate better.
