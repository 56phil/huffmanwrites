Four days after three Russian cruise missiles landed in southeastern Poland, NATO's secretary general said the alliance would defend against threats in space, after German media reported that Moscow was preparing to launch a satellite armed with a nuclear warhead. Mark Rutte, speaking in Berlin beside Chancellor Friedrich Merz, said the alliance had a variety of response options, and that "when it comes to the military domain, we will always make assessments of our deterrence and defence and make updates where necessary." The German broadcasters NDR and WDR and the Süddeutsche Zeitung reported that the satellite, which they said was called "Sputnik-S," could be used to disrupt or destroy other satellites, including important communication and observation satellites, and that intelligence about a possible launch in the next 60 days had been shared among NATO countries this month.

The report is not confirmed, and the alliance's language was measured. But it arrived at the end of a weekend in which the war's edges moved outward in three directions. On Friday, Polish authorities identified three Russian cruise missiles that struck a rural part of southeastern Poland, about 30 miles from the Ukrainian border, during a European military exercise; no one was hurt, and Prime Minister Donald Tusk called the episode an "intentional provocation." On Saturday, a sanctioned Russian cargo ship in the Baltic Sea was hit by what is suspected to have been a Ukrainian maritime drone. On Sunday, Ukrainian drones sank a Russian warship at its berth in Benghazi, Libya. Rutte, NPR reported, was cautious: he wanted Poland to finish its investigation before the alliance drew conclusions.

At home the markets produced a violent reversal. The S&P 500 closed Monday at 7,237.17, up 326.78 points or 4.73 percent, more than undoing the 265.44 points it had lost on Friday. The Nasdaq Composite rose 1,471.825 points, or 6.51 percent, to 24,089.558, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average added 593.14 points, or 1.21 percent, to 49,690.20. It was a rebound led by the growth-heavy indexes rather than a broad advance. Whether it holds is the question this morning's tape raises: the volatility index was up again, 6.76 percent to 28.42, before the opening bell.

## The administration and the rule of law

No presidential document was published in the two-day window. The Federal Register carried 18 final rules, 13 proposed rules and 182 notices. Two of the proposals are worth a reader's attention.

The first is [the Labor Department's proposed rule on loyalty and the exclusive purpose rule in selecting plan investments and exercising shareholder rights](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/24/2026-23936/loyalty-and-the-exclusive-purpose-rule-in-selecting-plan-investments-and-exercising-shareholder), RIN 1210-AC37, at 91 FR 75986. The department says it "clarifies the fiduciary duty of loyalty under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974" and "affirms that ERISA's duty of loyalty requires fiduciaries to act with the exclusive purpose of ensuring retirement security and providing promised benefits to plan participants and their beneficiaries." The proposal would also, in the department's words, "clarify the neutral application of ERISA's fiduciary duties of prudence and loyalty to selecting investments and investment courses of action, and to exercising shareholder rights, including proxy voting." In plain terms it reopens the question of how far retirement-plan managers may weigh environmental, social and governance factors, and how they vote the shares they hold. Comments are due January 25, 2027.

The second is [the Securities and Exchange Commission's proposal to restructure the governance and funding of the Consolidated Audit Trail](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/24/2026-23951/restructuring-the-governance-and-funding-model-of-the-consolidated-audit-trail), File No. S7-2026-40, at 91 FR 76034. The CAT is the system that records every order and trade in the U.S. equity markets. The commission proposes to "assume direct control of the CAT" and to amend Regulation NMS in a way that "would rescind Rule 613," the provision that obliges the exchanges and the industry's self-regulatory bodies to build and maintain the system jointly. The proposal is about who governs and pays for a database on which the markets depend; comments are also due January 25, 2027.

The final rules were mostly routine, with one exception published yesterday: the Labor Department's test for who is an employee and who is an independent contractor. The rest of the list was airworthiness directives, airspace amendments, a further postponement of the horse-protection regulations, and technical and import measures. [Treasury's notice adjusting civil money penalties for inflation](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/24/2026-23851/notification-of-inflation-adjustments-for-civil-money-penalties) set the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's maximum penalties for 2026. The notices included sanctions actions by the Treasury and State departments, filings before the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, and the usual round of information collections and advisory-committee meetings.

## The economy

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' newest readings remain those for October 2026. The consumer price index stood at 337.511, up 0.17 percent on September's 336.940, after September's own 0.59 percent rise, so the monthly pace of price increases slowed by more than half. Unemployment was 4.1 percent, a tenth below September's 4.2 and level with August. Nonfarm payrolls reached 159,293 thousand, a gain of 165,000 on the month, after a 60,000 gain in September.

Taken together the three readings describe an economy that is not contracting: prices cooled, hiring strengthened, and the jobless rate held near four percent. That picture is a month old. The markets that fell on Friday and rebounded on Monday were not trading October's data; they were trading expectations about what comes next.

## The world

The German reporting, as the BBC summarized it, said the satellite was called "Sputnik-S," was equipped with a nuclear warhead, and could disrupt or destroy other satellites, including important communication and observation satellites. Intelligence about a possible launch in the next 60 days had been shared among NATO countries this month, according to German media.

Asked about the reports, Rutte said the alliance would "always make assessments of our deterrence and defence and make updates where necessary," and that it had a variety of response options. Josef Aschbacher, the head of the European Space Agency, told the Handelsblatt newspaper that having nuclear weapons in space was "the ultimate nightmare scenario." A director of the Space Research Centre at Durham University told the BBC that exploding a nuclear weapon in space would have "massive infrastructural effects" given more than 15,000 active satellites, and that "the effect of these weapons would be extremely indiscriminate."

The legal ground is old and clear. Russia is a signatory to the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which bans weapons of mass destruction from orbit. In 2024 the United States accused Russia of developing a space-based anti-satellite weapon armed with a nuclear warhead, and Russia denied it; President Vladimir Putin said at the time that Moscow had "always been categorically against the deployment of nuclear weapons in space." In September the U.S. Secretary of the Air Force, Troy Meink, said the United States had "on-orbit, space-control weapons," and the Kremlin's spokesman, Peskov, said space "must be free of any weapon." Neither side's declaration changes the treaty. Both describe a domain that the treaty was meant to keep empty and no longer does.

The satellite report lands on top of the more immediate problem of the missiles. NPR reported that the three cruise missiles that struck southeastern Poland on Friday did so during one of Russia's biggest aerial attacks on Ukraine since the full-scale invasion began, and that they came down about 30 miles from the border while European forces were exercising nearby. Rutte, in Berlin for an annual meeting with parliamentarians from NATO countries, urged Poland to finish its investigation before the alliance assigned blame. That caution is itself the signal: the alliance is treating the incursions as a pattern to be documented, not a single accident to be answered.
