The State Department approved the sale of 500 Patriot PAC-3 interceptors to Ukraine on Friday, a package worth $2.1 billion, and by early Saturday Volodymyr Zelensky had welcomed it while warning that delivery may not begin for years. The department's statement gave no timetable. Two people briefed on the decision told the Guardian that the interceptors have not been built yet and are not expected to reach Ukraine until a couple of years into the 2030s. Canada, Germany and Norway will pay through the Jumpstart program, with national contributions financed by the European Union's Ukraine Support Loan, and the State Department said no American funds are involved. Friday's trading repriced risk across the board. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.86 percent to 25,436.999, the S&P 500 fell 1.82 percent to 7,580.07, and the VIX rose 12.91 percent to 19.42, while gold dropped 3.63 percent to $3,884.90 an ounce. Gold falling with equities rather than against them is the part of the session that does not fit the usual pattern. The pack carries the prices and not the cause.

## The administration and the rule of law

No document in this report's window carries a Saturday date. All 119 were published on Friday, and yesterday's edition named the four that matter most: the proclamation rewriting the scope of the additional duties on Canadian motor vehicles, the accreditation rule implementing Executive Order 14279, the calendar 2027 dialysis payment rule, and the interim final rule placing centanafadine in schedule III. That ground is not covered twice here.

Two Friday actions went unmentioned. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published an amended order, issued October 28, that bars any traveler from boarding a flight to the United States from a foreign country without a signed attestation that the person has not been in the Democratic Republic of the Congo during the 21 days before boarding. It takes effect at 11:59 p.m. Eastern on November 3 and runs through December 18. A 21-day lookback and a six-week window are the shape of a containment measure, and the order takes effect in the last minute of Election Day.

The State Department's sanctions notice records additions to the Treasury's list of specially designated nationals under four authorities: weapons of mass destruction proliferation, Iran's conventional arms activities, harmful foreign activities of the Russian government, and repression in Cuba. The notice names the persons. The abstract does not.

Homeland Security published a rule allowing electronic payment of Freedom of Information Act fees through pay.gov. It is a small change to the machinery of access, and the machinery of access is where a request either gets filed or does not.

## Markets

Friday closed sharply lower. The S&P 500 finished at 7,580.07, down 140.32 points or 1.82 percent; the Nasdaq Composite at 25,436.999, down 747.726 or 2.86 percent; and the Dow Jones Industrial Average at 51,770.31, down 572.25 or 1.09 percent, all as of October 30. The VIX closed at 19.42, up 2.22 points or 12.91 percent on the session; Thursday morning it had printed 18.45.

Gold's front-month contract fell $146.50 to $3,884.90, a 3.63 percent drop that carried it below $3,900, and front-month WTI crude rose 95 cents to $102.95 (CNBC prints, October 31). The dollar index was 101.964, down 0.073 or 0.07 percent, as of October 30.

The ten-year note's live quote was 4.933 percent at five o'clock Eastern on Friday, down 5.6 basis points on the session. Treasury's official curve for October 30 ran 4.56 percent at two years, 4.94 at ten and 5.31 at thirty, with the 2s10s spread at +0.38. Against October 29's 4.61, 4.98 and 5.32, the two-year fell five basis points, the ten-year four and the thirty-year one, and the spread widened to 38 from 37. Long rates easing while equities fall is a flight to safety; the thirty-year at 5.31 percent is what it costs to be the destination.

Total public debt outstanding was $40,593,637,262,883 on October 29, up $42,582,410,521 from October 28. Debt held by the public was $32,712,190,190,967 and the intragovernmental share $7,881,447,071,916. Yesterday's edition carried October 28's total at $40,551,054,852,362, and the two figures agree to the dollar.

## The economy

The three BLS series carry no release in this window, so the readings are the ones this report carried yesterday. September's consumer price index stands at 336.940 on the 1982-84 base, against 334.980 in August and 333.918 in July, a one-month move of 0.59 percent. Unemployment is 4.2 percent in September, up from 4.1 in August and in July. Nonfarm payrolls are 159,044 thousand, 29 thousand above August's 159,015 thousand. Prices are climbing at a faster monthly rate than at midsummer while hiring has flattened, and that pair is what has held the ten-year near five percent all week.

## Elections and the midterms

Polymarket's midterm contracts moved further toward the Democrats, and the money shifted toward the simpler question. The House contract stands at 94.5 percent Democratic on $833,003 of twenty-four-hour volume, up a point from 93.5 percent yesterday, with the Republican side at 5.5 percent on $1,371,112. The Senate contract is 70.5 percent Democratic on $393,176, up two points, against the Republican side at 29.5 percent on $853,203.

A Democratic sweep is priced at 70.5 percent on $1,345,005, up a point from 69.5 percent; a Republican Senate with a Democratic House at 23.5 percent on $312,972, down from 24.5; a Republican sweep at 5.5 percent on $603,167; and a Democratic Senate with a Republican House at 0.7 percent on $312,276. Volume on the Democratic House contract fell by more than half while the sweep event's volume nearly doubled, which is money moving to the plainest question rather than a fresh conviction about either chamber.

In the states, Michigan moved three points toward the Democrat. Abdul El-Sayed stands at 79.5 percent on $763,945 against Mike Rogers at 20.5 percent on $740,492. In Nebraska, Pete Ricketts holds 78.5 percent on $485,369 against an independent at 21.0 percent on $305,432.

These are the venues' prices, not this publication's view. The Republican House contract again drew more money than the Democratic one at roughly a seventeenth of the price, a cheap tail trading heavily rather than a forecast that it arrives.

## The world

The Patriot approval is the clearest signal yet of how the war is now financed: as a foreign military sale paid for by Ukraine's partners rather than by Washington. The State Department's statement names Lockheed Martin as principal contractor and says the purchase runs through Canada, Germany and Norway's contributions to the Jumpstart program and through national funds financed by the European Union's Ukraine Support Loan. The statement says that "no US funds are involved in this sale". The approval follows the row between Zelensky and President Donald Trump earlier this month, when the United States eased sanctions on Russia as part of a deal to secure Russian diesel.

Zelensky's objection is arithmetic rather than sentiment. The Pentagon's production figure, which the BBC reports, is 600 interceptors a year; Ukraine has ordered 500 in one package, and the Guardian's sources say the order is for new builds that will not arrive until the 2030s. In a post on X early Saturday he wrote that deliveries may begin "ONLY IN A FEW YEARS" and asked Washington to find "a solution for the time when there is a real danger and a real need to use PAC-3 interceptors." He said Russia had struck "critical parts" of Ukraine's power grid more than 30 times in the past two weeks.

The same window carried the cost. The Guardian reports that a Russian drone strike on Thursday killed at least 14 people on a crowded rail platform in Dnipro, that five more died in daytime strikes on two other provincial cities, and that a late-evening missile strike killed one person in Kyiv. At the G20 foreign ministers' meeting in Atlanta, European diplomats refused to stand for the official photograph rather than appear beside Russia's Sergei Lavrov, and Britain's foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, told him: "We all know who the aggressor in this war is."

On the war's other front, the prediction markets carry a clock. The contract asking whether the US-Iran ceasefire continues through October 31 stands at 97.2 percent, through November 2 at 92.3 percent, and through November 8 at 73.5 percent, so the venue expects the truce to survive the weekend and gives it roughly three chances in four of surviving another week. A contract on Washington announcing the end of the Iranian blockade stands at 2.7 percent for October 31 and 12.5 percent for November 2, one on Strait of Hormuz traffic returning to normal by December 31 is at 11.5 percent, and one on the Iranian regime falling before 2027 is at 5.5 percent.
