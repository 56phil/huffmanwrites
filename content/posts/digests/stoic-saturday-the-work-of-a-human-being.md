"I am rising to the work of a human being. Why then am I dissatisfied if I am going to do the things for which I exist and for which I was brought into the world?" — Marcus Aurelius, [*Meditations* 5.1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Thoughts_of_the_Emperor_Marcus_Aurelius_Antoninus/Book_V) (trans. George Long, 1862)

**The reflection**

The arithmetic arrived before the argument did. Seven Republican Senate seats fell on Tuesday, and with them the majority: fifty-four seats to forty-four, two still counting. The House went the same way, and so did a majority of governors' offices, which the Democratic Party has not held since 2010. It took one night, and the first thing worth saying about it is what it did not buy. Nobody voted for a program. The voters, by the exit polling, wanted the price of things addressed and the president checked, and what they purchased with those votes was an instrument: a gavel that a chairman can use alone, a subpoena a minority cannot block, and a calendar that turns at noon on January 3. The pastor who won Kansas told his crowd that history had been made that night and that the real work would begin on January 3. He was describing a date accurately, and he was making the only honest promise a winner can make, which is that he has not yet done the thing.

Marcus Aurelius wrote the line above for a man getting out of bed, not for a victor. That is the whole of its use this morning. The premise is that the work is the reason he exists and was brought into the world, and the question he asks is aimed at his reluctance rather than at the work, which he takes as settled and daily and not up for negotiation. Nothing in the passage suggests the work has a season, a scoreboard, or a conclusion. Read it on the Wednesday after a wave and it does something a victory speech cannot: it moves the election out of the center of the frame. A win is a change in the conditions of the work. It is not the work, and the men who confuse the two are the ones whose majorities evaporate while they are still taking credit.

The count is where the confusion shows first. Two Senate seats were still uncounted on Friday, and the closer of them is Mississippi, where Scott Colom's lead sat just under two thousand votes while the secretary of state reported more than nine thousand affidavit ballots processed by midday and counties prepared to transmit their certified returns next week. Mississippi has no recount provision at all. Both campaigns spent the week writing to county officials about how those affidavit ballots should be separated, preserved, and counted, and the substance of the Republican demand is that the ballots be set aside until someone decides what they are worth. A majority that won on the promise to count every vote now holds the power to define what counting means, and the Stoic version of that duty is narrow and uncomfortable: the rule that legitimized your victory is the same rule that protects your opponent's claim, and a party cannot keep the first half and drop the second without making its own win provisional too. The other seat, Alaska, will not finish until November 18, when ranked tabulation begins. In Colorado's fourth district, the incumbent trails by 3,069 votes and the automatic recount threshold is about 923. All three are the same question wearing different county seals.

The market offered its own reading, and it lasted one session. The Dow rose 3.03 percent on Wednesday, the first day of trading after the result, and on Thursday the S&P 500 fell 4.36 percent with all eleven sectors lower and the Nasdaq down 5.52 percent. That is not a verdict on the election, and it would be a mistake to read it as one; prices are not a moral instrument and a market is not a jury. It is a reminder that a result is not a receipt. The Stoics put a majority in the same category as health, wealth, and reputation: worth choosing, worth working for, and not worth being owned by, because it can be lost without your becoming worse and held without your becoming good. The test of the next two years is not whether this majority survives 2028. It is whether groceries cost less in 2027 than they did on Tuesday, and no subpoena reaches a price. That work is slower than a wave, it is invisible on election night, and it is the only work that will be remembered.

So the phrase needs correcting. There is no "real work" that begins on January 3 and finishes at some later date; there is work that begins every morning and is never once completed, which is why Marcus wrote it as a question about getting out of bed rather than a plan with milestones. Kansas waited ninety-four years for a Democratic senator. The man who ended the wait has a six-year term, not a conclusion, and the floor that moved this week is only a floor if it holds in a year when the tide runs the other way. A republic is a practice before it is an achievement, and practices are kept or lost by repetition. What the wave bought was the chance to practice again on better terms. That is a great deal, and it is not the thing itself.

**Sunday, the last forecast.** The [Senate Race Report of November 1](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/senate-race-report-2026-11-01/) carried Cook Political Report's closing analysis, which called the Democrats slight favorites and put their expected gain at two to six seats. The result was seven, one seat beyond the top of that range, which is the rare case of a forecast that was wrong in the direction of being too cautious.

**Tuesday, the vote.** [SITREP: November 3](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/sitrep/sitrep-2026-11-03/) records the mechanics on the morning of: all 435 House seats, 35 Senate seats and 36 governorships on the ballot, more than 57 million early votes already cast. Two facts from that edition belong in any account of the week. On Monday the Supreme Court denied the Justice Department's application to stop California counting 20,000 disputed ballots, in a one-line order that left the count standing on the eve of the vote. And an overnight strike on Qeshm made the Iran war an election-day fact rather than a campaign theme.

**Wednesday, the result.** [Seven Seats](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/seven-seats-the-2026-verdict/) sets out the arithmetic: North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, Ohio, Kansas, Iowa and Maine changed hands, the Democrats lost none of the thirteen seats they were defending, and they took the House and a majority of governorships. Kansas elected a Democratic senator for the first time since 1932, Adam Hamilton with 51.7 percent. Alaska remains uncalled and no longer decides control.

**The floor.** [The Year the Floor Moved](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/the-year-the-floor-moved/) makes the argument the numbers support: an eighteen-point win in Texas and a twenty-point win in North Carolina are not the margins of a campaign that found a few thousand votes, and Nebraska is the control group that tells you which party the voters were punishing. Pete Ricketts beat Dan Osborn, an independent who ran on the theory that the Democratic label was the liability, by seven points. A wave that removes a party is a realignment; a wave that removes incumbents is a mood. The margin in Mississippi stood at 43 votes shortly before 4 a.m. on Wednesday, when that essay was written.

**The money.** [A Loss Is Not a Refund](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/civics/a-loss-is-not-a-refund/) takes the week's most repeated conclusion and turns it over. Elon Musk put $445 million into federal races; three of the 43 Republican House candidates he backed won, 33 lost, and seven were uncalled when Reuters tallied it. The conclusion being drawn is that money cannot buy elections, and the essay argues that the money rented the terms on which they were fought, which is a thing it will be able to rent again. Texas PAC reported $158 million in receipts for the third quarter, and its itemized donor schedule was empty when the FEC's data was queried.

**The small electorate.** [Who Chooses the Choices](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/civics/who-chooses-the-choices/) puts the cycle at $11.9 billion, the most expensive midterm on record, and sets it beside a smaller number: 18 of 435 House seats were rated toss-ups as the cycle closed. The decisive electorate is not the general one. Campaign finance reform, ranked-choice voting and open primaries are three answers to one question, which is why each of them alone disappoints.

**The amendment question.** [A Fix the Court Cannot Undo](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/civics/a-fix-the-court-cannot-undo/) walks three routes out of Citizens United, two of which the same Court could strike down, and sets the cost of the third honestly. It is the piece to read before the new majority discovers that a subpoena is easier to issue than a constitutional amendment is to ratify.

**Not to do likewise.** [Not to Do Likewise](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/not-to-do-likewise/) argues that American politics did not become indecent because Americans became worse, but because the institutions that made decency pay stopped paying. A norm is a strategy, and it holds while breaking it costs something. The Stoic discipline it recommends is for the citizen who cannot fix the chamber and refuses to be fixed by it, which is the discipline this whole letter keeps circling.

**Thursday, the bonfire.** [SITREP: November 5](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/sitrep/sitrep-2026-11-05/) records the session after the celebration: the S&P 500 down 4.36 percent, the Nasdaq down 5.52 percent, the Dow down 3.44 percent, every sector lower, and the VIX up from 16.80 to 26.78 in a morning. It also records the losing party's account of itself, in which the vice president blamed the Iran war, the president agreed, and the program that emerged was four lines long. And it records a sentence in Hong Kong: Joshua Wong, 30, was sentenced to ten years for his second conviction under the national security law, with about two months left on the term he was already serving.

**Friday, the count and the war.** [SITREP: November 6](https://www.huffmanwrites.org/posts/sitrep/sitrep-2026-11-06/) reports that American officials have warned Gulf allies to prepare for new strikes while an Iranian lawmaker told Euronews the Hormuz standoff is not sustainable, so a negotiation and a bombing campaign are being prepared at once. It also carries the count: Mississippi just under two thousand votes apart with more than nine thousand affidavit ballots processed, Alaska's tabulation due November 18. In Colorado's fourth district, where the count has not moved since Thursday night, Eileen Laubacher leads Lauren Boebert by 3,069 votes, and the candidates' lawyers are already arguing about recounts before anyone has certified a number.

**The weekly reports**

The **Senate Race Report** ended with its November 1 edition, which is linked above. It published on the Sunday before the vote with Cook calling the Democrats slight favorites and the markets badly wrong in three states, and the election then came in a seat beyond the top of the published range. The series is not scheduled to continue; the wave it spent a season measuring is the thing of which it now becomes the record.

The **Docket Report** published its October 31 edition on the East Potomac golf course, and its finding is that a federal judge established as fact what the government had told her did not exist. Judge Ana Reyes granted the preliminary injunction and held that the Washington National Plan exists and that the government's stated reason for the tree removal was pretextual. Two days later the government filed a notice confirming the plan, with a closure date of November 15 and the designer's name. The next edition publishes at 08:00 this morning.

The **Bond Market's Next Ninety Days** published on November 1 and did something this series does rarely: it led with its own error. Last month's call was for the ten-year between 5.2 and 5.8 percent by December 30; it closed October at 4.94 percent, twenty-six basis points below the floor of the range. The mistake was assuming the bond market would keep pricing the war. The shock that moved it was an equity valuation event, which the Fed has nothing to say about, and the window now runs to January 30.

The **S&P 500's Next Ninety Days** published the same morning on a seam in the keystone: the index set its twenty-eighth record of the year on October 6 at 7,818.93, gave all of it back by October 30, and lost a trillion dollars of technology value in three weeks while earnings kept growing and the discount rate finally fell. One of those should have held the index up, and the week that followed supplied its own answer.

The **Chiefs Report** published its November 3 edition on the first loss of the division lead. Denver erased a 24-13 fourth-quarter lead and stopped five plays inside the 4-yard line, and the Chiefs were 80-2 under Patrick Mahomes when leading by ten or more in the fourth quarter before Sunday. Eleven penalties for 105 yards, the worst penalty margin in the league, and a pass rush that pressured Bo Nix on six percent of his dropbacks. Mahomes called it a dogfight for the division, and ten games remain in which to win one.

The **Global SITREP** publishes every morning, and the editions of November 1 through 6 are linked above.

The **Repair Plan** is a quarterly working document for a future administration rather than a letter to you.

**The Practice**

Pick one thing this week that the election did not change and one thing it did, and write each in a single sentence. The first is your practice for the winter: a neighbor, a local board, a polling place that needs workers in the spring, something small enough to start on a Tuesday and repetitive enough to keep. The second is your test for 2028, and it should be a number you can look up rather than a feeling you can defend, because a majority is judged by what it did to the price of things and not by how it sounded while doing it. Then set both where you will see them, and give the first one a date in the next seven days, since the only difference between a resolution and a habit is that one of them has a morning attached. Marcus governed through a plague, a frontier war, and a rebellion, and the sentence he wrote for himself is not about any of them. It is about getting up.

See you next Saturday. — Phil
