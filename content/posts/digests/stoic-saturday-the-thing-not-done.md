"He often acts unjustly who does not do a certain thing; not only he who does a certain thing." — Marcus Aurelius, [*Meditations* 9.5](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Thoughts_of_the_Emperor_Marcus_Aurelius_Antoninus/Book_IX) (trans. George Long, 1862)

**The apology that was doing the work of outrage.** On Wednesday I published an essay I did not expect to write, and it is the week's first item because everything after it is quieter. [My Apologies](https://huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/my-apologies/) is seven confessions in a row, each one a sentence I spoke in good faith and the fact that undid it. I explained the talk away, and then a rally named two American cities as acceptable losses. I said he would run the government like a business, and then the East Wing came down for a ballroom whose price climbed while the agencies with jurisdiction over it were told to "just trust the process." I said at least he was smart, and then his own new AI portal, asked about 2020, answered with the correct electoral count. I said he was draining the swamp, and then seventeen inspectors general were fired on a Friday night without the notice the law requires. The essay's argument is not about him. It is that the explaining was doing the work outrage was supposed to do, and that normalization is not a decision anybody makes but a series of favors done to a conversation.

**The number the government does not keep.** On Thursday came a quieter essay with a harder finding. [On Time and Under Budget](https://huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/on-time-and-under-budget/) asks a plain question: what share of federal contracts finished on time and under budget, and how does that compare with the last administration? The answer is that the number does not exist, and no system could produce it on request. The federal government publishes what contracts cost and who holds them. Whether the work arrived late or over budget was never a field in the record. The instrument that does capture it, CPARS, is not public and is filled in late: the Transportation Department's own inspector general found the department took an average of 421 days to register contracts, with fewer than 26 percent meeting the 30-day requirement. Aggregate those and you would not be measuring contract performance. You would be measuring reporting performance.

**Three mornings of notice.** The [Global SITREP](https://huffmanwrites.org/posts/sitrep/sitrep-2026-10-09/) filed three editions this week, and read together they show the same move three times: something consequential arriving as a notice rather than a decision. On Wednesday the Federal Register carried a withdrawal of two FinCEN crypto rulemakings; on Thursday, a Homeland Security proposal to charge each F-1 student $70,000 for a first term of work authorization; and on Friday morning, an executive order deferring the federal diesel excise tax through December 31. The diesel order is the clearest of the three because it states its own reason in its first paragraph, and because it is a tax the country will not collect, signed five days before it was published, on the argument that the supply is restricted. None of these is a scandal. All three are the same shape: the decision happened somewhere the reader was not.

**Thirty days out.** The [Senate Race Report](https://huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/senate-race-report-2026-10-04/) moved on Sunday, and for the first time the professionals said it with numbers. Inside Elections shifted seven states in a single update, six of them toward the Democrats, projecting a gain of two to four seats against the four they need. The Senate Leadership Fund pulled its money out of North Carolina to defend Kansas. The New York Times found Kansas tied at 45 percent, and Kalshi's own desk put Democratic control at 63 percent, up from 49 in mid-September. The report's own question is the one worth keeping: is this still whether the Democrats can win the majority, or has it become how many ways they can still lose it?

**A record that did not wait, and a room that got thinner.** Sunday's [docket report](https://huffmanwrites.org/posts/essays/docket-report-2026-10-03/) found the government asking two courts to decide disclosure cases before any record of compliance exists, and the Kennedy Center still closed with no reopening date at all. Friday's [Chiefs report](https://huffmanwrites.org/posts/sports/chiefs-report-2026-10-06/) found the team at 4-0 and alone atop the AFC with Tyquan Thornton's season in doubt and Rashee Rice's hamstring unresolved. The month's two [ninety-day](https://huffmanwrites.org/posts/investing/sp500-next-ninety-days-2026-10-01/) installments closed the same window into December 30: the bond market's ten-year reached, seven weeks early, the top of the range the series had set for December, while the S&P finished the quarter up two percent on the back of one sector, with the strategists raising targets into a range now nineteen percent wide.

Here is the honest reading of the week, and it is why the epigraph is a line about omission rather than an act. Every item above is a thing that did not happen. The apology that was never given at the dinner table. The measurement that was never kept, so the question cannot be answered and the accountability never arrives. The tax that was deferred, the fee proposed, the rule withdrawn: each a decision taken in a register rather than a room. A ballroom that came down while the agencies that had jurisdiction were not asked. A record asked to close before it is complete. Marcus's sentence is the shortest one in the book, and it is the one this week earns: wickedness is not only what a hand does. It is also what a hand declines to do, and most of what went wrong this week went wrong by omission.

That is what makes it a citizen's problem rather than a spectator's. Corruption in a republic rarely announces itself with an envelope. It arrives as a normal process, a routine register entry, a cost that nobody totalled, a duty nobody performed. The answer the Stoics left us is not outrage, which costs nothing and changes nothing, but the performance of the duty nearest to hand. For a citizen in a republic, the nearest and heaviest of those is the ballot, and it is thirty days away. A vote is not a statement of what you feel about the last four years. It is the one appointment in public life that happens at a fixed time and cannot be deferred by notice.

**The weekly reports**

The **Senate Race Report** published its October 4 edition on Sunday, and this week it stopped hedging: Inside Elections moved seven states on October 1, six toward the Democrats, the Senate Leadership Fund abandoned North Carolina to defend Kansas, and the Times found Kansas tied at 45.

The **Docket Report** published its October 3 edition on Saturday, on two courts asked to decide disclosure cases before any record of compliance exists, and on a Kennedy Center that has not reopened.

The **Bond Market's Next Ninety Days** and the **S&P 500's Next Ninety Days** both published on October 1, closing the window at December 30.

The **Chiefs Report** published its October 6 edition on Tuesday, with Kansas City 4-0 going into the bye and both of the receivers who made that record work now uncertain.

The **Global SITREP** published three editions this week, on October 7, 8, and 9, the newest covering the diesel excise deferral and the President's statement that the country will not attack Iran before the midterms.

The **Repair Plan** is a quarterly working document for a future administration rather than a letter to you.

**The Practice**

This week, take one duty you hold and have let slide, and name it in a single sentence. Not the largest thing in your life, which is usually a way of avoiding the exercise, but one you have been telling yourself is not yours to do because it is small: the ballot you have not checked, the local race you have not read about, the call you have been meaning to make, the thing you noticed and left unmentioned. Set a date on the calendar inside the next seven days and do that one, and tell no one you did it. Marcus's point is not that wickedness is dramatic. It is that most of it is unperformed duty, and duty, unlike outrage, has a date.

See you next Saturday. — Phil
