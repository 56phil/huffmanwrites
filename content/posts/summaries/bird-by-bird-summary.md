# *Bird by Bird*

### Some Instructions on Writing and Life

### Anne Lamott, 1994

---

## Executive Summary

*Bird by Bird* is a writing book whose most quoted advice is a permission slip. The title comes from her father's answer to her ten-year-old brother, paralysed at the kitchen table the night before a bird report was due: "Bird by bird, buddy. Just take it bird by bird" ([Vintage](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/97395/bird-by-bird-by-anne-lamott/)). Everything the book teaches about craft sits inside that instruction, which is about scope rather than quality. You do not write the book. You write the paragraph in front of you, and then the next one.

Her second permission slip is the one that gives a chapter its name, and it is the reason the book survives. Lamott's claim is not that great writers compose badly and fix it later; it is that the discomfort is universal among them and hidden by the finished page. "Not one of them writes elegant first drafts," she writes. "All right, one of them does, but we do not like her very much" ([chapter three](https://www.nicoleannwilliams.com/uploads/5/6/9/7/569783/shitty_first_drafts_lamott-_full_text.pdf)). The book's authority is that voice: funny, wounded, unembarrassed about her own envy and dread, and unwilling to pretend that publishing is a reward for virtue.

The structure is five parts, and it moves from the desk outward. Part One is the work itself: short assignments, first drafts, perfectionism, character, plot, dialogue, set design, false starts. Parts Two and Three are the mind and the company you keep, from jealousy and the radio station of self-loathing to writing groups and index cards. Part Four is publication and the reasons besides publication, and Part Five is a single closing piece called "The last class" ([library contents note](https://find.library.upenn.edu/catalog/9979474618003681)).

---

## 5 Core Arguments

**1. Scope is the cure for paralysis, not discipline.** The one-inch picture frame is Lamott's instrument: when the food review had defeated her, she picked it up, "stare[d] into it as if for the answer," and the answer was always the same, "all I had to do was to write a really shitty first draft of, say, the opening paragraph." The frame does not make the task smaller in truth; it makes the visible task small enough to begin. A report on birds becomes one bird, and then the next.

**2. Nobody's first draft is written by the writer they hope to be.** The chapter turns on an inversion of the reader's fantasy: successful writers are imagined to sit down "feeling like a million dollars" and type "fully formed passages as fast as a court reporter," and that picture is "the fantasy of the uninitiated." The real account is worse and more useful, and she supplies the taxonomy that has outlived the book: "the first draft is the down draft," the second "the up draft," and the third "the dental draft," where you check every tooth to see whether it is "loose or cramped or decayed, or even, God help us, healthy."

**3. The voices are the obstacle, and they can be moved rather than argued away.** Her hypnotist's exercise is the book's strangest and most practical passage. Each critical voice becomes a mouse, picked up by the tail and dropped into a mason jar, and the reader turns the volume up, then down, and gets back to work. The insight is that the inner critic cannot be refuted, because it is not making an argument; it can only be contained, and the containment is a ritual rather than a proof.

**4. Craft is learned in public and in company.** The book's middle is not about sentences. Index cards, calling around, writing groups, someone to read your drafts, letters: the argument is that writing is a solitary act sustained by a social structure, and that most blocked writers are isolated rather than untalented. That is why the book reads as a memoir with instructions folded in, and why the instructions are mostly about the conditions of work rather than the work.

**5. Publication is not the point, and pretending otherwise is what breaks writers.** Part Four exists to dismantle the reward the reader came for: writing a present, finding your voice, giving, and then publication, in that order. The ordering is the argument. A writer who puts publication first has arranged their life so that the only evidence of progress arrives from strangers, at intervals, and on someone else's schedule.

## The Architecture

Five parts and 29 titled pieces in the paperback, though the parts are what a reader navigates by, since the chapters are short and the sequence is a mood rather than a curriculum ([Metropolitan Library](https://metro-prod.groveforlibraries.com/Record/CARL0000143113)).

## A Respectful Disagreement

**The permissions are real and the diagnoses are thin.** Lamott is superb at the moment before writing and at the moment after a bad draft, and thin in between. "Bird by bird" tells you to narrow the task, which is exactly right when the obstacle is scale, and offers nothing when the obstacle is that the paragraph is boring, the character is inert, or the argument does not hold. A method that treats every blockage as anxiety will keep a writer working and will not tell them why the work is not landing.

**The book's comfort depends on liking the narrator.** Its authority is Lamott's voice: self-deprecating, confessional, funny at her own expense, and shaped by recovery, faith, and a large family of friends. That is a pleasure to read and it is also the mechanism. A reader who does not find that voice companionable, or who does not want to be addressed as a nervous child at a kitchen table, gets much less from the book than its reputation suggests, and the reputation does not mention the temperament it requires.

**The publishing advice has expired.** The 1994 book assumes agents, advances, and a magazine market that paid for food reviews written over two days. A writer in 2026 faces a different economy, and the section that was reassuring then is close to archaeological now. The parts about drafts, jealousy, and index cards have not aged at all, which is a useful way to see which half of the book was about writing and which half was about a career.

**"Shitty first drafts" is a permission that can curdle into a policy.** The advice is a corrective for writers who cannot begin, and it becomes an excuse for writers who will not revise. Lamott does not say that, because she is writing to the first kind of reader. But the phrase has travelled further than the chapter: it is quoted as though the draft were the point, when the chapter's own structure, through three named drafts, is an argument that the draft is only the raw material.

---

## Bottom Line

*Bird by Bird* is the best-loved book on writing that is not really a craft manual. Its subject is the emotional weather of the work, and its method is to remove the two obstacles that stop most people: the size of the task and the certainty that everyone else finds it easy.

What stays with you is the jar. The voices do not get refuted or resolved; they get turned down, and the paragraph gets written.
