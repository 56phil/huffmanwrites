# *10x Is Easier Than 2x*

### How World-Class Entrepreneurs Achieve More by Doing Less

### Dan Sullivan with Benjamin Hardy, 2023

---

## Executive Summary

The title is a provocation with an argument behind it, and the argument is about arithmetic of a particular kind. Aim at twice what you do now and every existing client, task, and commitment still looks worth keeping, because twice is reachable by working harder. Aim at ten times and almost everything you currently do is revealed as irrelevant, since very few activities can produce a tenfold result. The number is not a forecast. It is a filter that makes subtraction unavoidable ([Hay House](https://www.hayhouse.com/10x-is-easier-than-2x-hardcover)).

Sullivan, the co-founder of Strategic Coach, and Hardy, an organizational psychologist, put the claim this way in the publisher's description: "10X is not the outcome; it's a counterintuitive process you can apply every time you want exponential growth in your life and business." They also name the scoreboard. The four freedoms are time, money, relationship, and purpose, and the book's claim is that expanding them is what makes growth possible rather than what growth buys ([Hay House Business](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/710816/10x-is-easier-than-2x-by-dan-sullivan-founder-of-strategic-coach-with-dr-benjamin-hardy/)).

The third piece is a reframe of the past. Sullivan's standard move with a client is to ask when their revenue was one tenth of today's, and then to break that climb into five jumps. The exercise is designed to prove that the reader has already done this once, which is the only evidence that makes the next leap credible. His summary of the logic is one sentence: "things are only logical looking backwards" ([Strategic Coach](https://www.strategiccoach.com/resources/podcasts/podcast-payoffs/97135361/how-to-win-big-by-doing-less)).

## 5 Core Arguments

**1. 10x is subtraction wearing a big number.** The chapter sequence gives this away. After an opening chapter titled "10x Is the Opposite of What You've Been Told," the principles section establishes quality, then abundance, and only then does the applications section arrive at what most readers came for: free days and a company that runs without them. The order is the argument. You cannot schedule your way to a tenfold result, because the schedule is what is in the way.

**2. The growth comes from what you leave behind.** Sullivan's account of his own growth is not about adding customers. It is about removing: "you leave behind methods that don't work anymore. You leave behind people that don't work anymore… and there were customers that just didn't do it anymore" ([Strategic Coach](https://www.strategiccoach.com/resources/podcasts/podcast-payoffs/97135361/how-to-win-big-by-doing-less)). Each of those removals is uncomfortable and each produced a burst of growth, which is why the book spends its middle arguing about courage rather than about tactics. The subtraction is the method; the discomfort is the price, and the book is honest that it precedes any evidence of payoff.

**3. Quality is the lever, quantity is the trap.** The publisher states the distinction plainly: "10X is fundamentally about quality vs quantity, and the quality of your freedoms determines the results you achieve." Fewer things done far better is a different strategy from the same things done faster, and only the first produces the tenfold jump, because a crowded offer competes on the same terms as everyone else. That is why the four freedoms are the scoreboard and revenue is treated as a consequence: time becomes more valuable, money follows the value, relationships become the way work gets done, and purpose outlasts the transaction.

**4. Want, do not need.** The abundance principle separates wanting something from needing it, and the distinction matters because a need is a scarcity claim that someone else's gain must be your loss, while a want requires no justification and produces no resentment. A reader pursuing what they need stays inside the existing business, improving it. A reader pursuing what they want can leave it, which is the only route to a different order of magnitude.

**5. The company has to survive your absence.** The last two chapters are the ones with the least glamour and the most consequence: at least 150 free days a year, and a self-managing company. Both depend on other people owning work in their own right, which is the same principle as the book's predecessor, *Who Not How*. The free days are not a reward for the leap; they are the condition of it, because a calendar with no room in it cannot hold a new direction.

## A Respectful Disagreement

**"Easier" is doing two jobs in one word.** A tenfold goal is easier to decide about, because it eliminates alternatives. It is not easier to execute; nothing about the book makes the work of building a tenfold business smaller, and the case studies are people who had already built something before they tried. The claim is true in the sense that matters for attention and false in the sense that matters for effort, and the title does not distinguish the two. What survives the ambiguity is still valuable: subtraction is easier to see from a big goal than from a small one, and that insight does not need the multiplier.

**The tenfold arithmetic is a rhetorical device, not a finding.** Nothing in the argument establishes that ten is the right multiplier or that it produces better decisions than three or twenty would. The number is chosen because it is shocking enough to break the habit of incrementalism, which is a legitimate use of it. But a reader who takes the target literally will set a number that neither their market nor their capability can support, and the book offers no test for whether a tenfold goal is achievable in a given business.

**The exercise assumes a past that went up.** "Find when you were one tenth of today's revenue" is a powerful demonstration for a successful entrepreneur with a decade of growth behind them. For a first-time founder, a reader whose income has fallen, or anyone in a profession that does not compound, the exercise either fails to produce five jumps or flatters a flat line. Sullivan's evidence is drawn from a coaching population that has already self-selected on growth, which is the same survivorship problem that runs through the genre.

**The last chapter is the first requirement.** Free days and a self-managing company are presented as applications, after the principles. In practice they are preconditions. A reader with no team cannot take 150 free days, and a reader who cannot take free days has no space in which to design the leap. The book's ordering is pedagogical, and its advice is therefore available in full only to readers who have already solved the hardest part.

---

## Bottom Line

*10x Is Easier Than 2x* is a subtraction argument dressed as a growth argument, and the dress is what sells it. As a growth promise it is unfalsifiable; as a method for deciding what to stop doing, it is the sharpest thing Sullivan and Hardy have written together.

What stays with you is the exit list. The methods that no longer work, the clients who no longer fit, the team members who have outgrown their seat: naming those is the work, and a tenfold goal is mainly useful for making the names obvious.
