# *The Stoic Virtues*
### Ryan Holiday, 2021–2025

---

## Executive Summary

Four books, published one a year from 2021 to 2025, on the four cardinal virtues of ancient philosophy. Each opens with the same short preface: Hercules at the crossroads, choosing between pleasure and virtue, and then the list: "Four books. Four virtues. One aim: to help you choose . . ." The footnote under that sentence counts the volume you are holding, from book one to book four.

The series is a single argument delivered in four installments: **the virtues are not four good habits bolted together. They are one character described from four angles, and you cannot have any of them properly without the others.** The order is not arbitrary. Courage is what a person needs before anything else can be tried, temperance is what keeps the trying from destroying the trier, justice is what the result is owed to, and wisdom is what tells you which of the three a situation demands.

The publisher sells four books. The books themselves keep insisting that there is only one, and the fourth volume says so outright: wisdom is called the mother of all virtues because the other three are born from it. That claim is the series' best idea, and the fact that it arrives in the fourth volume, after 1,024 pages, is the series' main problem.

---

## The Architecture, and the Line

The four volumes share a shape. Each is built as a three-stage ascent, each stage carries a name, and each book ends on a closing formula that identifies its virtue with virtue as such.

| Volume | Year | Pages | Parts | Chapters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Courage Is Calling* | 2021 | 304 | Fear / Courage / The Heroic | 65 |
| *Discipline Is Destiny* | 2022 | 352 | The Exterior (the Body) / The Inner Domain (the Temperament) / The Magisterial (the Soul) | 52 |
| *Right Thing, Right Now* | 2024 | 368 | The Me / The We / The All | 43 |
| *Wisdom Takes Work* | 2025 | 400 | The Agoge / The Sirens / The Apotheosis | 45 |

That is 1,424 pages across 205 chapters, which works out to roughly seven pages a chapter. The chapter is the real unit of the series, not the book: short, titled as a command ("Attack the Dawn," "Empty the Cup," "Quit Being a Slave"), often built around a single figure, and over before it wears out its welcome. Holiday has been writing this way since *The Obstacle Is the Way*, and at this scale it is the only thing keeping the project readable.

Three of the four close with the same chord. *Courage Is Calling* ends "Courage Is Virtue. Virtue Is Courage." *Discipline Is Destiny* ends the same way about self-discipline, and *Wisdom Takes Work* does it for wisdom. *Right Thing, Right Now* does not. It closes on "Pay It Forward," which is either an oversight or the one honest structural admission in the series: justice is not a virtue you complete. It is a debt that keeps being owed.

The connective tissue is a joke, and it is the most revealing thing in the four books. Courage, Holiday writes, is "the kind where you put your ass on the line." Temperance is "the ability to keep your ass in line." Justice, he continues, is "holding the line, or drawing up our 'Flat-Ass Rules,' to borrow a phrase from the great General James Mattis." ([*Courage*](https://profilebooks.com/wp-content/uploads/wpallimport/files/PDFs/9781788166270_preview.pdf), [*Discipline*](https://profilebooks.com/wp-content/uploads/wpallimport/files/PDFs/9781788166331_preview.pdf), [*Right Thing*](https://profilebooks.com/wp-content/uploads/wpallimport/files/PDFs/9781788166317_preview.pdf))

It is funny, it is memorable, and it is a geometry. Every virtue in the first three volumes is a posture relative to a line: expose yourself to it, contain yourself behind it, hold it against others. That is a military account of character, and it explains a great deal about the reader the series has in mind.

*Wisdom Takes Work* drops the metaphor entirely: there is no line anywhere in its front matter or its first part, because wisdom is not a posture. It is a capacity, and a capacity cannot be described by where you are standing. The fourth book quietly concedes that its subject belongs to a different category than the first three, which is why it also has to argue that the other three depend on it.

The other thing worth noticing in the shared preface is the word Holiday chose for volume two. The list of synonyms for temperance runs "self-control, moderation, composure, balance." The book is titled *Discipline*, and its thesis sentence is about keeping yourself in line. He picked the control word out of a family that also contains the balance word, and the substitution is the series' most consequential editorial decision. More on it below.

## 5 Core Arguments

1. **Fear is the default state, and courage is decided before it is needed**: Holiday's first volume treats cowardice not as a vice of the weak but as the ordinary human setting, and courage as a decision made in advance of the moment that tests it. Florence Nightingale receives her call in 1837 and then waits years for anyone to let her answer it; the courage in Holiday's telling is not the night in the Crimea but the refusal to let the calling expire. "There is nothing we prize more than courage, yet nothing is in shorter supply."
2. **Temperance is load-bearing, and it is the virtue that makes the others survivable**: In the second volume, self-control is what keeps talent from becoming self-destruction. Lou Gehrig carries its opening chapter; Napoleon, Fitzgerald, and Babe Ruth are the cautionary set. "Discipline, then, is both predictive and deterministic." Holiday's claim is stronger than it first appears: without restraint, the courageous man is merely reckless and the just man is merely angry.
3. **Justice is a verb, and it begins with the small transaction**: The third volume expands the circle from the self to the household to the polity, and its insistence is that justice is not a theory but a practice measured in ordinary dealings. "Because that's what justice should be, not a noun but a verb." Truman and Rickover are the working examples; the claim is that integrity is built out of unglamorous decisions nobody watches.
4. **Wisdom cannot be shortcut, which is why it is the last volume and the longest**: "Wisdom takes work. Like love and happiness and everything worthwhile, wisdom can't be accessed through hacks or shortcuts." The fourth book is structured as training, then temptation, then something like arrival, and its middle section is a catalogue of intellectual failure modes: the empty cup, the poisoned information diet, the flattered mind. It is the most interesting volume in the series because it is the only one whose subject resists the case-study method.
5. **The four are one system, and the series is the demonstration**: Read separately, each volume is a motivational book about a virtue. Read together, they are an argument about dependence: courage without discipline is a fire, discipline without justice is a machine, justice without wisdom is a slogan. The series' formal claim is that sequence matters, and its formal weakness is that it takes four books to say what one good essay could.

## A Respectful Disagreement

The strongest published objection is not that Holiday is wrong about the virtues. It is that the series is not really about them.

Mark Athitakis, reviewing the project for The Washington Post, put it precisely: "Most of Holiday's books are published by Portfolio, Penguin Random House's business imprint, and to a remarkable extent his series on the Stoic virtues doesn't deal with Stoicism as such." His verdict on the movement Holiday leads is harsher than anything I would write: "This is many things, but it's not a coherent moral philosophy." ([The Washington Post](http://web.archive.org/web/20250724015501/https://www.washingtonpost.com/books/2024/03/28/stoicism-ryan-holiday-mark-tuitert/))

Kirkus made the narrower version of the same complaint about the justice volume, and it is the one I find hardest to answer: "for a book purportedly rooted in the teachings of the ancients, Holiday gives surprisingly little attention to Stoic standard-bearers like Cato the Younger, Marcus Aurelius, and Epictetus." Its one-line verdict is entirely fair: "Time-honored advice, if not always timely." ([Kirkus Reviews](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/ryan-holiday/right-thing-right-now/))

Here is the disagreement in my own words. **The series renames temperance as discipline, and the rename changes the virtue.** *Sophrosyne* is about fit: knowing how much, in this situation, is the right amount. Discipline is about control: knowing who is in charge. The first is a virtue of proportion, and proportion is a relationship between a person and a circumstance. The second is a virtue of command, and command is a relationship between a person and himself. Holiday keeps the vocabulary of the older idea in his preface and then builds the book around the newer one, and the newer one is what a business imprint can sell. The result is a series that reads like management literature with Roman decoration, which is a real achievement and not the same thing as a book about Stoicism.

The second objection is structural. A 205-chapter anthology organized around one virtue per volume is a filing system, not four arguments. The exemplar method confirms the thesis in every chapter, and it cannot do anything else: Florence Nightingale is in the courage book because she was courageous, and the reader who wants to know whether courage is really the first virtue will not find the question tested anywhere. The Washington Post's reviewer called the result incoherent; I would call it unargued. The claim that the four virtues depend on one another is asserted in four prefaces and demonstrated nowhere, because demonstrating it would require a case where the virtues pull against each other: the just act that is not the kind act, the disciplined life that is not the good one. Those cases are exactly what the series never stages.

And it should be said plainly that none of this makes the books useless. A reader who wants a page a morning about how to behave has been well served, and the fourth volume in particular does something the earlier three do not: it admits that wisdom is slow, that reading is the only way into it, and that a person who skips the reading is not wise but merely confident.

---

## Bottom Line

*The Stoic Virtues* is one good book in four volumes, and the good book is the argument in the prefaces: that character is a single thing, that its parts hold each other up, and that the order in which you acquire them matters more than the intensity with which you pursue any one.

Read *Courage Is Calling* and *Wisdom Takes Work*. The first is the most conventional and the most useful to a reader who has never had to be brave on purpose. The fourth is the one Holiday seems to have needed to write, and the only one whose subject he cannot reduce to a case study. *Discipline Is Destiny* and *Right Thing, Right Now* are competent and largely interchangeable with each other and with the shelf of books around them.

Then put the series down and read Epictetus. The point of a doorway is not the doorway.
