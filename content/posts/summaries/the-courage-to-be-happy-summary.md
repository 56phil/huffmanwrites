# *The Courage to Be Happy*
### Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga, 2016

---

## Executive Summary

Three years after the conversations in *The Courage to Be Disliked*, the young man returns to the philosopher's study. He has become a teacher, tried to put Adler into practice in a real classroom, and watched it fail. The theory that once freed him now looks like an abstraction fit for a study and useless in a room full of children. The philosopher does not retreat. He argues that the young man has mistaken the map for the territory, and the second dialogue takes up the question the first book left unanswered: not how to be free of other people's expectations, but what freedom is meant to accomplish.

The answer the book builds toward is that **happiness is not a discovery but a decision, and the decision requires courage**: the courage to see others as equals, to treat education as assistance rather than correction, to love without a guarantee, and to keep choosing a life after the first choice has been made. Where the first book was a map of Adlerian psychology, the authors describe this one as a compass, a set of instructions for walking the ground the map describes. The Japanese original appeared in 2016; the English edition followed from Atria Books in 2019. ([Publisher's page](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Courage-to-Be-Happy/Ichiro-Kishimi/9781982123000))

---

## 5 Core Arguments

1. **The goal of education is self-reliance, not obedience.** A teacher's task is not to mold a child into a preferred shape but to help the child stand alone. That rules out both rebuke and praise, because both are judgments delivered from above; praise no less than punishment teaches a child to act for someone else's verdict. The classroom, on this account, is a democratic nation rather than a hierarchy, and the teacher's authority is the authority of a fellow citizen.

2. **Respect means seeing a person as they are.** The book treats respect as the precondition for everything else: not admiration for qualities one approves of, and not the wish that a person become someone else, but the act of looking at another human being without a plan for improving them. Encouragement follows from respect, and the courage to take on a task follows from encouragement.

3. **Problem behavior has a purpose.** The classroom chapters read misbehavior as communication aimed at a goal: attention, a power struggle, revenge, or a display of inability. The catalogue is Adlerian, and its logic is that punishment cannot dissolve a goal, because the goal is relational and punishment confirms the relationship the child is testing. The alternative is to address the purpose, which is slower, harder, and far less satisfying than a punishment well delivered.

4. **Self-reliance is breaking away from the "me" at the center.** Adler's self-reliance is not economic independence. It is the move from a life organized around one's own satisfactions to a life organized around contribution, and the book argues that happiness is felt precisely there, in the sense of being useful to other people. Work, friendship, and love are the three tasks in which that sense is built. ([Diamond's page for the Japanese edition](https://www.diamond.co.jp/book/9784478066119.html))

5. **Love is a decision, not a discovery.** The final chapters are the book's climax. Love is not something one falls into; it is an art one practices, a task two people accomplish together, and a choice that can be made again when the first feeling has gone. There is no destined person, because a destiny would remove the choice, and the choice is the point. The same logic governs a life: one chooses a lifestyle, and one can choose it again.

## The Three Tasks

Adler named three tasks that every adult faces: work, friendship, and love. The book's structure follows them, and its most interesting claim concerns the boundary between the first two. Education, the philosopher insists, belongs to friendship rather than to work. A teacher who treats the classroom as a job manages children; a teacher who treats it as friendship meets them. The distinction is not sentimental. It changes what counts as success: compliance is a work outcome, while the growth of another person's judgment is a friendship outcome, and the second cannot be compelled.

Love is the hardest of the three because it requires the self-reliance the earlier chapters describe. A person who needs the other's approval cannot love the other; they can only manage the other's impression. That is why the book spends so long on self-worth before it arrives at romance, and why its account of love is finally an account of two people who can each stand alone choosing to stand together.

## Adler and the Stoics

The parallel is direct enough that a blurb on the publisher's page describes Adlerian psychology meeting Stoic philosophy in a Socratic dialogue. Epictetus begins with the distinction between what is in our power and what is not; Adler begins with the claim that the past does not determine the present, because the present uses the past for its own purposes. Both refuse to let a person's history serve as a verdict. Both treat philosophy as a practice rather than a body of text, which is why both arrive in the form of a conversation.

The differences matter too. Stoicism aims at virtue and at the calming of disruptive passion; Adler aims at community feeling and at the felt sense of contribution. The Stoic sage needs nothing outside the self; the Adlerian person is constituted by relationships and cannot be happy alone. Read together, the two traditions correct each other's excess: Stoicism supplies the discipline that Adler's optimism can lack, and Adler supplies the relational warmth that Stoic self-sufficiency can make sound like isolation.

---

## A Respectful Disagreement

The book's central move is to convert every limit into a choice, and that move is liberating exactly where the limit is a story. It is cruel where the limit is a fact. A teacher whose class will not settle may be facing a room shaped by hunger, violence, or a school that has abandoned him; a reader with depression is not suffering from a failure of courage, and telling them that happiness is a decision adds shame to an illness. The treatment of trauma carries the same problem in a softer form: the claim that the present chooses its past is a useful corrective to fatalism and a poor description of a nervous system that has been injured.

The account of love has a sharper edge. The claim that a person can love anyone is a fine antidote to the fantasy of the destined soulmate and a dangerous instruction inside an abusive relationship, where the problem is rarely insufficient effort and often a person who should be left. A philosophy of choice needs an account of when choosing again means choosing to go, and this book does not supply one.

There is also a rhetorical cost to the form. The youth raises objections and the philosopher answers them, but the answers are aphorisms and the objections are staged to be overcome. A reader who wants the strongest case against Adler will have to bring it himself.

---

## Bottom Line

*The Courage to Be Happy* is the practical half of a two-book argument, and it should be read after the first. Its best pages concern teaching, where the distinction between correction and assistance is concrete enough to use on Monday morning, and love, where the refusal of destiny is genuinely bracing. Its weakest pages are the ones that promise more than a philosophy can deliver: that attitude can dissolve constraint, and that every unhappiness is a choice not yet made.

Read it as a compass, as the authors ask, and keep an honest map of your own limits beside it.
