# *Betrayed: America Didn't Vote for This*
### Ira Shapiro and Anne Kim (eds.), 2026

---

## Executive Summary

*Betrayed* began as a twelve-month project and took sixteen, because the material kept arriving. What the Common Sense Coalition published in May 2026 is a damage assessment rather than an argument: twelve chapters of roughly 3,000 words each, 176 pages, 643 endnotes, written by about four dozen former officials, military officers, and scholars, and edited by Ira Shapiro and Anne Kim. Each chapter takes one domain and audits it, the way an inspector general would. The book's claim is narrower than its title's heat: **the damage is general across institutions, some of it will not be repaired on any electoral timetable, and the specific things documented here were not what Americans were promised.**

That is the shape of the thing, an inventory with a deadline attached. Kim names Thomas Paine's 47-page pamphlet as the model, and the homage explains both the book's brevity and its intended use: a document for ordinary readers, assembled to be read before a vote rather than after a verdict.

---

## 5 Core Arguments

1. **The check failed everywhere at once.** The chapters open with the rule of law and close with civil discourse, and the ten domains between them are varied: alliances, prices, health, universities, the civil service, climate, immigration, elections. The pattern is not one failure repeated. It is a single structure of accommodation, in which institutions built to say no found reasons to say yes. Colin Robertson's review states the finding in institutional terms: independent bodies "increasingly adapt themselves to political pressure instead of acting as constitutional counterweights."

2. **The economic bill lands on households.** The affordability chapter is the book's most concrete. A New York Fed analysis puts 90 percent of the tariff burden on American consumers and companies; farm bankruptcies rose 46 percent as costs climbed; the budget adds $3.4 trillion to the deficit across the decade. The health chapter carries a line sardonic enough to read as a section head, "Make Americans Greatly in Debt Again," and the book earns the joke.

3. **Security was traded for loyalty.** Alliances became transactional, intelligence assessments were politicized, and the Agency for International Development was dissolved in the administration's first two weeks. The book's accounting includes a war of choice with Iran, entered without a stated strategy or an exit. The coalition's own summary of the result is a slogan it means literally: America First is America Alone.

4. **Government became the instrument of self-dealing.** The corruption chapter carries 79 endnotes, the most in the book, and it documents contracts rather than scandals: private prison operators positioned to absorb the immigration enforcement money, contractors hired by states to build the work-reporting regimes that will remove people from Medicaid, a no-bid contract to repaint the reflecting pool. The argument is that this is a method of governing rather than an aberration from it, and the chapter is written to be usable by a prosecutor.

5. **The future was damaged deliberately, and that is the part an election cannot reach.** Research capacity, the university partnership, climate infrastructure, the professional civil service, and the government's own credibility. The authors' formulation is that Trump's "loose relationship with the truth has also made honest debate impossible," which is a claim about the conditions of argument rather than about any single lie. Kim puts the consequence bluntly: future generations now have no reason to believe what their government tells them.

---

## The Reversibility Problem

The book's real contribution is not the inventory. It is a classification the chapters never state outright and enforce on every page.

Some damage is reversible on a normal political timetable. Policies can be rewritten, appointees replaced, budgets restored. Other damage is not: deaths in detention, a cohort of children who lost coverage for the years that matter most to a childhood, research programs wound down and the people who ran them dispersed, a civil service that has learned to keep its head down, and a public that has stopped believing anything the government says. The inventory is long; the part that matters is the part that does not come back.

That classification is what makes the electoral argument coherent, and it is why the book was timed for November 2026 rather than published as history. If everything here were reversible, the midterm would be a preference. Because some of it is not, the midterm is a deadline. The coalition states the conclusion without ornament: the abuses "must be stopped, and Trump and his loyalists must be held accountable."

---

## A Respectful Disagreement

The subtitle is the weakest joint in an otherwise careful book. "America didn't vote for this" is a claim about what voters believed, and a damage assessment cannot establish it. Mass deportation, tariffs, and retribution against institutions were advertised in advance. A reader who supported those things will say he voted for exactly this and object to the rest, and the book has no evidence with which to answer him. What it demonstrates is narrower and stronger: a gap between what was promised and what was delivered, prices down on the first day and no new wars among the promises. That is a charge of broken faith, and it stands on the record without the counterfactual about intent.

The second reservation is structural. Twelve chapters, each written by people who already hold the conclusion, arguing in one direction, is a prosecutor's brief, and the form carries a bias that endnotes cannot cure. Nothing in the book makes the administration's best case; nothing weighs what the alternatives would have cost or concedes where the previous order failed on its own terms. The evidentiary floor is unusually high for a book of this kind, and the adversarial testing is absent. The prescription is thin as well: an appendix playbook and a call to vote. A book whose own evidence says the ballot is under attack owes its reader more than an instruction to use it.

---

## Bottom Line

*Betrayed* is the best-organized indictment of Trump's second term yet published, and its discipline is the reason. Twelve domains, one method, 643 endnotes, almost no rhetoric. It is not written to change the mind of anyone who read the campaign as a mandate.

Read it as a record rather than a revelation. Its value is not that it tells you what happened; the news did that. Its value is that it fixes the cost in one place, with citations, before memory softens and the argument becomes about how things felt. Robertson's formulation is the right one: *Regime Change* explains how Trump governs, and *Betrayed* explains what that governing has wrought.
