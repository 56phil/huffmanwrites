# *Tiny Experiments*

### How to Live Freely in a Goal-Obsessed World

### Anne-Laure Le Cunff, 2025

---

## Executive Summary

The book's target is the linear plan: four-year degree, ten-year career, thirty-year mortgage. Le Cunff's objection is that these are forecasts wearing the costume of commitments, and that a life organised around one can only report two outcomes, arrival or failure. Her replacement is a small, time-bound experiment with an end date, run the way a scientist runs a trial: commit, act, review, and then decide what to do with what you learned.

The unit that carries it is the **pact**, and its shape is deliberately modest: "I will [action] for [duration]" ([Ness Labs](https://nesslabs.com/writing-personal-growth)). A goal asks whether you arrived. A pact asks whether you showed up for the period you named, which is a question you can answer honestly on the last day. The publisher's description puts the shift in one line: readers "replace the old linear model of success with a circular model of growth in which goals are discovered, pursued, and adapted" ([Avery](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/737291/tiny-experiments-by-anne-laure-le-cunff/)).

Le Cunff is a neuroscientist and the founder of Ness Labs, and the book's manner reflects that background: the claims are about attention, time perception, and the mechanics of motivation, and the tools are small on purpose. There is no system to install. There is a sentence you write, a window you protect, and five minutes on a Sunday to record what worked, what did not, and what comes next.

## 5 Core Arguments

**1. Goals are broken by their own arithmetic.** A linear goal contains a hidden assumption that the future is knowable, and the book's opening chapters take that apart: why goal setting is broken, and why the pressure to find a purpose is worse than the uncertainty it is meant to cure. The reframe is not that ambition is bad. It is that the path to an ambitious result is usually illegible in advance, so the honest instrument is a series of probes rather than a plan, because a plan that must be right before you start cannot learn anything.

**2. An experiment is defined by having a fixed number of trials.** This is the book's sharpest mechanical distinction, and it is worth carrying out of it: "Experiments follow a simple format: one action repeated enough times to collect sufficient data. In contrast with a habit where you'd ideally repeat the action forever, an experiment has a predefined number of trials" ([Ness Labs](https://nesslabs.com/personal-experiments)). A habit is judged by whether it continues; an experiment is judged by whether it finished, and finishing is what produces the review.

**3. The four stages make the loop, and the loop is the book's spine.** PACT commits you to something specific for a duration. ACT is the doing, with notes kept while it happens. REACT is the review: what worked, what did not, what next. IMPACT is what the accumulated experiments become when they are shared. The four part titles in the book correspond to these stages, which is why the contents read like a method rather than a set of essays: commit to curiosity, practice mindful productivity, collaborate with uncertainty, grow with the world.

**4. Failure is converted rather than avoided.** Le Cunff's claim is categorical: "The great thing about personal experiments is that you cannot fail. Any outcome is a source of data." That sentence is the book's promise and its most contestable move, and both halves are worth stating plainly. The promise is that a bad result becomes information, which is true of the experiment and not true of the mortgage; the contest is whether a frame that cannot fail can tell you when to stop.

**5. Attention and time are the resources the method actually spends.** The middle of the book is about time perception, procrastination, and intentional imperfection: why hours at a desk are not the unit of progress, why delay is often a signal rather than a defect, and why doing some things only well enough is what makes the rest possible. This is where the neuroscience does its work, and it is the part of the book that changes behaviour fastest, because it asks the reader to move a boundary rather than to acquire a discipline.

## The Architecture

Four parts plus an introduction and a conclusion, 304 pages, from Avery on 4 March 2025 ([Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/737291/tiny-experiments-by-anne-laure-le-cunff/)). The page starts are the table of contents' own, and they show how much of the book is the middle two parts.

| Section | Opens |
|---|---|
| Introduction: Goodbye, Linear Life | 13 |
| Commit to Curiosity | 23 |
| Practice Mindful Productivity | 71 |
| Collaborate With Uncertainty | 127 |
| Grow With the World | 173 |
| Conclusion: Hello, Experimental Life | 235 |
| Experimentalist, glossary, notes, toolkit | 242 |

Each part holds three chapters. The first asks what you want and how to commit to it, the second asks how to spend attention while you are in it, the third asks how to decide and adjust when the data arrive, and the fourth asks what the experiments are for once they accumulate. The toolkit at the back is the book's concession that readers want the instruments without the argument, and it is where most readers will end up.

## A Respectful Disagreement

**"You cannot fail" is a definition, not a finding.** If every outcome counts as data, then the frame has removed the possibility of failure by fiat, which also removes the possibility of success. What remains is a permanent middle state, and the reader who most needs a verdict, on whether to keep going with a business, a degree, or a relationship, is the one the frame is least able to give one. The book partly answers this with the end date and the persist, pause, or pivot decision that follows it, which is a real answer. The rhetoric does not always wait for the mechanism.

**The pact is a goal with better epistemics.** Le Cunff's method is presented as the end of goal setting, and a pact is a commitment to an action for a fixed period, which is a goal by any ordinary definition. What has actually changed is what the goal is for: the pact is designed to produce knowledge rather than to produce an outcome. That is a genuine and important difference, and it is smaller than the book's framing implies. A reader who resents goals may find the pact easier to keep precisely because it is a goal they are allowed to abandon on schedule.

**The evidence is her practice plus her reading.** The credential is real and the citations are there, and the case material is drawn from her own experiments and from other people's stories. What the book does not offer is a trial of the method: no comparison group of people who set goals and did well, no measure of how many readers finish their pacts. That is a normal limitation for the genre and it matters here because the claim is strong, that this approach beats the linear one rather than merely complementing it.

**The preconditions are not in the method.** A thirty-day pact assumes thirty days you can allocate, and a review on Sunday assumes a Sunday. Magic windows, protected writing time, and a public experiment all presuppose a life with slack in it, and the reader with two jobs or a caregiving load is being invited to a laboratory they cannot staff. The book is aware of systemic barriers and says so; the tools still arrive as though the calendar were the reader's to rearrange.

**Learning in public distributes risk unevenly.** Publishing the experiment is treated as a way to create accountability and to compound learning, and for a reader with a reputation to protect, a public failure is not a data point but a cost. Students, junior employees, and anyone whose income depends on appearing competent are being asked to spend something the author, as an independent researcher and founder, does not spend in the same currency.

---

## Bottom Line

*Tiny Experiments* is the best-argued case for treating a life as a research programme rather than a project plan, and its central instrument is genuinely usable: a sentence, a duration, and a review.

What stays with you is the distinction between a habit and an experiment. One is judged by whether it continues, the other by whether it ends, and knowing which you are running is most of the work.
