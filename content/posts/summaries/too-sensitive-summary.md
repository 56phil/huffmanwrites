# *Too Sensitive*

### Rejection, Resilience, and the Science of Feeling Deeply

### Sasha Hamdani, MD, 2026

---

## Executive Summary

*Too Sensitive* takes a complaint that has been treated as a character verdict and moves it into the body. The complaint is the one in the title: a delayed reply, a flat tone, a meeting invitation that arrives late, and then an afternoon spent building a case against yourself. Hamdani's answer, in the introduction, is that this is not a personality flaw but "a highly responsive and attuned nervous system," and that the clinically useful name for its most severe form is rejection sensitive dysphoria, or RSD ([Flatiron Books](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250423764/toosensitive)).

The book's structure follows from a gap she states without softening: RSD is not in the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, and no validated test for it exists. What the field does not supply, the book constructs. Chapter three carries a self-assessment that places a reader on a sensitivity spectrum, and the second half carries a twelve-part regulation framework she calls CALM YOUR MIND, assembled from three years of reading and clinical observation, by her own account in the introduction.

The foreword is by William Dodson, MD. The book reached sixth on the *Publishers Weekly* hardcover nonfiction list for the week ended August 29, 2026 ([*Publishers Weekly* bestseller list](https://www.arcamax.com/entertainment/booksnews/s-4290647)), and Flatiron sells it as an instant *New York Times* bestseller, a claim this summary reports without independent confirmation.

## 5 Core Arguments

**1. Rejection is processed as injury, and that is measurable.** The book's central mechanism is not metaphor. Social exclusion activates the same pain circuitry that physical injury does, and the anterior cingulate cortex responds to a simulated slight the way it responds to a wound. The practical consequence is that a reader cannot reason their way out of the first thirty seconds, because the alarm sounds before the reasoning arrives: the amygdala and the limbic system fire ahead of the prefrontal cortex, which is the part that would have said the email was fine.

**2. The system is ancient and the inputs are modern.** Hamdani grounds the intensity in evolutionary terms. For most of human history, exclusion from the group was a survival event rather than an emotional one, so the brain evolved to treat social rejection as a threat to life. The reader is running that software on a delayed text message. What differs between people is the sensitivity of the response, not the presence of the system.

**3. The problem is the gap between feedback and failure.** Her account of her own training makes the case better than a definition does. During a 2 a.m. sign-out, an attending physician interrupted her report with sighs, and she froze. What the attending said afterward is the book's thesis in one line: "If I interrupt you, it's so I get the full picture. If I give you ideas on how to do things differently, it's feedback, not failure." Hamdani's reply to herself is blunter: "To me, all feedback was failure, and all failure was too painful to tolerate."

**4. Sensitivity is a capacity, and the work is emotional intelligence.** The book refuses the trade that most advice offers, in which the sensitive person is asked to feel less. "Sensitivity is not the problem—it's what you do with it that shapes your experience," she writes, and the distinction she draws is between the ability to perceive emotional shifts and the ability to work with what was perceived. The second is learnable. The first is not a defect in need of correction.

**5. What the field has not built, the reader must be given.** Because RSD carries no diagnostic criteria, an assessment and a structured protocol do the work a diagnosis would otherwise do. She describes testing cognitive behavioral therapy, dialectical behavior therapy, somatic tracking, narrative exposure, and mindfulness-based stress reduction against what helped in the moment, across three years of reading and clinical observation, and then building the twelve-strategy toolkit from what survived. The book's ambition is that a reader can apply it during the reaction rather than after it.

## The Space Before the Spiral

The book's most useful sentence is not in the book. Asked what she hoped a reader would take away, Hamdani put the intervention between the trigger and the reaction: "the goal isn't to stop being sensitive. It's to understand what happens between the trigger and the spiral, because that's where we can actually intervene" ([TAAF interview, August 25, 2026](https://www.taaf.org/blog/understanding-emotional-sensitivity-dr-sasha-hamdani-takes-us-through-her-new-book-too-sensitive)).

That interval is short, physical, and mostly automatic, which is why the book spends its first half on mechanism before it offers a technique. A reader who does not know that the chest pain is a stress response rather than a verdict has nothing to work with in the four minutes that matter.

---

## The Toolkit and Its Honest Limit

The second half is organised around the situations in which RSD actually fires: a manager who will not give feedback, a partner who goes quiet, a family that dismisses the reaction as drama, a promotion that feels unearned. One reviewer of an advance copy describes the interventions as drawn from CBT, DBT, and acceptance and commitment therapy, and reports that the medication discussion sits in the reference material rather than in a chapter of its own ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/244967405-too-sensitive)).

The book's own honesty about the construct is worth recording, because it is rarer than it should be. In the interview above, Hamdani states the limit directly: "RSD is not an official DSM diagnosis, and we don't yet have universally accepted diagnostic criteria or a validated clinical test specifically for RSD (which is way overdue)." A book built to fill a gap is a book that has to say where the gap is, and this one does.

---

## A Respectful Disagreement

The toolkit asks for a capability the book has just finished describing as impaired. Every strategy in the second half begins with the same move: notice the reaction, name the feeling, choose a response. But the first half establishes that the reaction arrives before the naming, that the alarm precedes the reasoning, and that the interval is measured in seconds. A reader who could reliably pause inside that interval would not have the problem the book describes. The gap is not fatal, since a trained pause can be shortened with practice, but the book does not say how a person reaches the first successful pause, which is the only step that matters when the system is already flooding.

The categorical claim outruns the evidence. RSD is a term in wide clinical and community use with no agreed criteria, so any statement about how many people with ADHD have it is an estimate wearing the clothes of a prevalence figure. One reviewer of an advance copy flags the book's claim that nearly all of them do, and the objection holds on the book's own terms: emotional dysregulation in ADHD is well documented in the literature, while RSD as a discrete entity is not, and authority on the first tends to be borrowed for the second.

There is also a question the book raises and does not answer. If the response is neurological and the culture is what makes it costly, then accommodation is owed by the people around the sensitive person and not only by the sensitive person. The book spends its pages on what the reader can do alone. The manager who sighs, the family that calls it drama, and the workplace that reads a flinch as weakness are all present as triggers, and none of them is asked to change.

---

## Bottom Line

*Too Sensitive* is a careful book about a construct that medicine has not formalised, and its value is that it takes the reader's experience seriously without pretending the category is settled. The first half is the part to keep: a mechanism, a vocabulary, and a name for a pattern that has been dismissed for most of the reader's life.

What it gives is a map of the interval between a trigger and a spiral, and a set of tools for that interval. What it does not give, and does not claim to give, is a diagnosis, a cure, or a reason to stop feeling deeply.
