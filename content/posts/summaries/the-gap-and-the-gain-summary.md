# *The Gap and the Gain*

### The High Achievers' Guide to Happiness, Confidence, and Success

### Dan Sullivan with Benjamin Hardy, 2021

---

## Executive Summary

The book makes one distinction and spends two hundred pages applying it. We all carry an "ideal," a moving target that is always out of reach. Measure yourself against that ideal and you are in **the Gap**, where every result looks like a shortfall. Measure yourself back toward where you started and you are in **the Gain**, where the same result reads as progress ([Hay House](https://www.hayhouse.com/the-gap-and-the-gain)).

Dan Sullivan built the concept across decades of coaching entrepreneurs at Strategic Coach, and the publisher's summary of the effect is blunt: "unsuccessful people focus on 'The Gap,' but successful people focus on 'The Gain'" ([Hay House Business](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/679143/the-gap-and-the-gain-by-dan-sullivan-and-dr-benjamin-hardy/)). Benjamin Hardy, an organizational psychologist, wrote the book with him, and the division of labor shows. Sullivan supplies the coaching insight and the case material, Hardy supplies the psychological framing.

The diagnosis is not that ambitious people are ungrateful. It is that they are running the wrong instrument. An ideal is a horizon: useful for pointing the direction, useless as a distance to be arrived at, and the book's strongest single sentence is Sullivan's own formulation that "the ideal is not a measuring stick" ([Strategic Coach](https://www.strategiccoach.com/resources/the-multiplier-mindset-blog/the-best-goal-measurement-strategy-for-entrepreneurs)). A goal has measurable attributes and can be reached; an ideal cannot be reached even by the person who holds it, which means measuring against one guarantees a verdict of falling short no matter what the reader actually did.

---

## 5 Core Arguments

**1. The Gap is a measurement error, not a character flaw.** Sullivan describes noticing two kinds of equally successful clients: those who were happy with their lives and those who felt like failures, and concluding that the difference lay in "how they measure their progress in terms of goals and their ideal outcome" ([Strategic Coach](https://www.strategiccoach.com/resources/the-multiplier-mindset-blog/the-best-goal-measurement-strategy-for-entrepreneurs)). That reframing is the book's gift to its reader. It moves the problem from identity ("I am never satisfied") to method ("I am comparing against the wrong thing"), and a method can be changed by anyone who can do arithmetic.

**2. The two meters compound in opposite directions.** A Gap reading produces disappointment, which lowers confidence, which makes the next attempt harder, which produces a worse result. A Gain reading produces satisfaction, which raises confidence, which is what makes the next attempt possible. The book calls this the compound effect of whichever meter you choose, and it is the argument that carries the most weight, because it costs nothing and is self-correcting: the reader who measures backward gets a reason to keep going rather than a reason to stop.

**3. Your success criteria must be yours.** The second chapter is titled "Be self-determined: define your own success criteria," and the excerpt Sullivan published makes the case in a line worth the book: "A lot of people have other people's brains: their parents', their teachers', their mentors'—whoever's telling them how they're supposed to be" ([Strategic Coach](https://www.strategiccoach.com/resources/the-multiplier-mindset-blog/unsatisfied-with-your-progress-this-could-be-why)). A borrowed ideal cannot produce a satisfying Gain, because the Gain is measured to a starting point you did not choose and toward a standard you did not set. Self-determination here is not a value the authors assert; it is a precondition for the arithmetic to work.

**4. Measure backward on a schedule, not on a whim.** The book's practice is not a mood but a cadence: a chapter on always measuring backward to increase hope and resilience, and one on measuring three wins daily. The daily version matters more than it sounds. A quarterly review tells you whether the year went well; a daily count of three specific wins trains the attention that produces the reading, and Sullivan's own advice is that specifics are the only thing that can be measured: he writes in the excerpt that "specifics must be measured against specifics," and a vague sense that things went "pretty well" cannot be compared with anything ([Strategic Coach](https://www.strategiccoach.com/resources/the-multiplier-mindset-blog/unsatisfied-with-your-progress-this-could-be-why)).

**5. Every experience can be converted into a gain by owning it.** The third part turns from measurement to ownership: "take ownership of your past," and a closing chapter on improving your success criteria, subtitled "always be the buyer" ([library contents note](https://catalog.wake.gov/Record/771441)). The logic is that a past you disown is a cost, and a past you have bought is an asset you can build from, which is the same move Sullivan makes with clients who cannot enjoy an achievement until it is reframed as evidence of capability. I have not read those chapters, and the titles are what I can report.

## The Architecture

Three parts and seven chapters, plus a long introduction: by the library's own collation, thirty-four pages of front matter and 217 pages of text, against the publisher's 264-page and 272-page listings of the same edition ([Wake County](https://catalog.wake.gov/Record/771441)). The book is shorter than its reputation suggests, and a reader can finish it in an afternoon.

| Part | Chapters |
|---|---|
| Get out of the Gap | Wants not needs; self-determined criteria; the compound effect of either meter |
| Always measure backward | Measuring backward for hope and resilience; measuring three wins daily |
| Transform every experience into a gain | Taking ownership of the past; improving your success criteria |

The three-part sequence is a progression rather than a menu. The first part establishes that the Gap exists and that it is a choice of instrument, the second installs the counter-practice, and the third extends the practice backward into the past and forward into the standard itself. Hay House Business published the hardcover on 19 October 2021.

## A Respectful Disagreement

**The Gain has the same weakness as the Gap, and the book does not answer it.** The Gap fails because it compares a real result with an unattainable reference. The Gain compares a real result with a chosen starting point, and the choice of starting point is where motivated reasoning has the most room. A company that has fallen forty percent from its peak can report a gain from last quarter's trough; a runner returning from injury can choose the week he could not walk. Both statements are arithmetically true and both are consoling, and neither tells the reader whether the trajectory is sustainable. The book's rule is that you measure to where you started, which is a rule about direction and not about which start. Without a discipline for choosing the baseline, the Gain is a frame that can be applied to any number, and the reader most in need of a warning is the one most able to produce a flattering one.

**Sometimes the Gap is telling the truth.** If the ideal is a horizon, then the distance to it is not information. But some ideals are not horizons; they are forecasts with dates, and a cash runway that ends in four months is a fact rather than a mindset. The book's answer to this is that living in the Gain does not excuse mediocrity, and Sullivan makes that point explicitly in the article version, where he also admits to entering the Gap "several times a day" ([Strategic Coach](https://www.strategiccoach.com/resources/the-multiplier-mindset-blog/the-best-goal-measurement-strategy-for-entrepreneurs)). That admission is the most useful sentence in the corpus around this book, because it reframes the method as a correction rather than a cure. A reader who takes the concept as a permanent state rather than a returning practice has misunderstood it, and the book's confidence of tone does not always prevent that misunderstanding.

**The psychological claim is larger than the evidence underneath it.** Hardy writes in the publisher's description that "everything that psychologists know about how to create a high-functioning and successful person can be achieved using The GAP and the GAIN" ([Hay House Business](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/679143/the-gap-and-the-gain-by-dan-sullivan-and-dr-benjamin-hardy/)). That is the co-author's own copy, and it is an overclaim: the evidence offered in the book is the testimony of coaching clients, and testimony arranged by outcome cannot establish a mechanism. The concept is a good reframe; the universal psychology in that sentence is marketing, and the reader should keep the two apart.

**The examples assume countable gains.** Revenue, deals, projects, and clients. The book says the concept applies to family, health, and community, and it probably does, but the worked examples are commercial and the arithmetic is easiest where a number exists. A reader whose year included a death, an illness, or a caregiving load may find that measuring backward shows a loss, honestly and not as a failure of mindset. The book has less to say to that reader than its subtitle, addressed to high achievers, implies it should.

---

## Bottom Line

*The Gap and the Gain* is one idea delivered at the length the idea deserves, which is short. The distinction between an ideal that inspires and a goal that measures is the part worth keeping, and it survives the book's weaknesses because it does not depend on them.

What stays with you is the arithmetic of attention. A result compared with a horizon is always a failure; the same result compared with your own starting point is usually a gain, and the second comparison is the one that leaves you able to act.
