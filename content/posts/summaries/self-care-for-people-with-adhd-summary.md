# *Self-Care for People with ADHD*

### 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Prioritize You!

### Sasha Hamdani, MD, 2023

---

## Executive Summary

The book's premise is stated in its own jacket copy: it "can help you engage in some neurodiverse self-care—without pretending to be neurotypical" ([Simon & Schuster](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Self-Care-for-People-with-ADHD/Sasha-Hamdani/9781507219430)). Two commitments follow from that sentence, and they separate this book from the shelf around it.

First, the failing is located in the instruction rather than in the reader. Most self-care advice assumes a nervous system that will hold a routine once the routine is chosen, and an ADHD reader usually cannot. Second, care is broken into units small enough to survive a bad day. The book is not an argument that runs from a first premise to a conclusion. It is a catalogue of **more than a hundred discrete actions**, sorted into six domains, written to be entered at any point.

Hamdani writes from both sides of the desk. She is a board-certified psychiatrist and ADHD clinical specialist, and she has ADHD herself, which she states plainly on [her own site](https://www.drhamdanimd.com/about-me) and in the publisher's author note. The book appeared through Adams Media on January 3, 2023; *Too Sensitive* followed from Flatiron in 2026.

---

## The Six Domains

The table of contents divides care into six chapters, and the division is the book's real intellectual content ([Perlego edition listing](https://www.perlego.com/book/3260304/selfcare-for-people-with-adhd-100-ways-to-recharge-destress-and-prioritize-you-pdf)):

1. **Emotional self-care**
2. **Physical self-care**
3. **Mental self-care**
4. **Social self-care**
5. **Career and professional self-care**
6. **Practical self-care**

A preface and an introduction precede the chapters, and an index follows them. The taxonomy is worth reading as a claim. "Practical" is a separate domain from "mental," and that distinction is where the book is most useful to the reader it was written for: the unpaid bill, the unopened post, and the drawer without batteries in it are not failures of mindset and cannot be fixed by reframing. In most books of this kind they are not a domain at all.

---

## The Format Is the Argument

The arithmetic is the clearest statement in the book. More than a hundred entries occupy roughly 190 pages, with six chapter openings, a preface, an introduction, and an index inside that count, which puts the average entry near a page and a half ([Adams Media](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Self-Care-for-People-with-ADHD/Sasha-Hamdani/9781507219430)).

That shape answers a question a longer book cannot. A reader who cannot finish a chapter is not served by an argument that requires finishing it, and a reader in the state the book is written for will not remember a system described across two hundred pages. Entry-per-page means no single failure costs more than a page, and the index means the book can be consulted by symptom rather than read in sequence.

The cost of the shape is that nothing in it can be argued at length. An entry can name an action; it cannot show the trial or the sample behind the action. The reader gets the intervention without the evidence, which makes the book a prompt to test rather than a finding to accept.

---

## A Respectful Disagreement

A hundred-item catalogue can reproduce the problem it names. The reader most likely to buy this book is the reader who has already abandoned several routines, and the book hands that reader a list of a hundred more things to not finish. The shame of walking away from an itemised book is not obviously lighter than the shame of walking away from a morning routine, and the format offers no ritual for the entries a reader will skip. The book says repeatedly that the reader is not lazy; it does not say what to do with the page count once it becomes another unfinished project.

The commercial shape constrains the depth further. A short, portable, gift-shaped book is a reasonable product and a poor container for a clinical argument. A psychiatrist who has treated this population for years has material for a book that makes one claim and defends it; this one spends its pages on coverage instead.

The largest omission is material rather than clinical. Self-care advice of any kind assumes discretionary time, a room of one's own, and the money for the drawer full of batteries. The book addresses the neurology of the obstacle and says little about the economics of it, and for a reader whose problem is a second job rather than a dysregulated evening, no entry in any of the six domains will close the gap.

---

## Bottom Line

*Self-Care for People with ADHD* is a reference manual disguised as a self-help book, and the disguise is the most useful thing about it. It refuses the premise that a person should be able to meet a neurotypical baseline first, and it organises care by domain so that a reader can find the one that is failing today.

Read it as a menu, not as a plan. The value is in the entry a reader can start within a minute of opening the book, and the six-domain split is the part worth keeping when the individual entries are forgotten.
