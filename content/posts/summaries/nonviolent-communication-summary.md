# *Nonviolent Communication*

### A Language of Life

### Marshall B. Rosenberg, 2015

---

## Executive Summary

Rosenberg's claim is that most of us were taught to speak in a way that produces conflict and then blamed for the conflict that follows. He calls it life-alienating communication and gives it four recognizable forms: moralistic judgments that imply wrongness in whoever disagrees with us, comparisons, language that hides our responsibility for our own feelings and actions, and demands. None of that is unusual speech. It is the ordinary register of a meeting, a marriage, or a news broadcast, which is why the diagnosis lands before the method begins.

What he proposes instead is a four-part process, and each part is a chapter: observe without evaluating, identify and express the feeling, take responsibility for the need behind it, and make a request rather than a demand ([library contents note](https://library.mlc-wels.edu/Record/422119)). The publisher describes the result as the integration of four things, consciousness, language, communication, and a means of influence, and it names the last of those as the whole point: "sharing 'power with others' rather than using 'power over others'" ([PuddleDancer](https://nonviolentcommunication.com/product/nvc/)).

The book's sharpest single sentence is not about technique. It is Rosenberg's explanation of what criticism actually is: "It is my belief that all such analyses of other human beings are tragic expressions of our own values and needs" ([chapter two sample](https://nonviolentcommunication.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/NVC-3e10p-web-sample-060619.pdf)). A judgment is a report on the speaker wearing the costume of a report on the listener, and the book's method is largely an exercise in taking the costume off.

## 5 Core Arguments

**1. Judgment is the mechanism, not the symptom.** Rosenberg locates the root of violence in a habit of thought rather than in an emotion: "At the root of much, if not all, violence—whether verbal, psychological, or physical, whether among family members, tribes, or nations—is a kind of thinking that attributes the cause of conflict to wrongness in one's adversaries." The consequence is that the argument about who is right is the violence, not merely a prelude to it, which is why the book never asks the reader to win one.

**2. The language of obligation hides the choice.** "Have to" is the tell. Rosenberg's teacher, told to grade because district policy requires it, is walked through the translation and arrives at "I choose to give grades because I want to keep my job," then objects that saying it that way makes her feel responsible ([chapter two sample](https://nonviolentcommunication.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/NVC-3e10p-web-sample-060619.pdf)). That objection is the whole difficulty. Responsibility is uncomfortable, and the passive constructions we use instead, policy, orders, role, circumstance, are what make it possible to obey something and disown it. His example of where that road ends is Eichmann's *Amtssprache*, the office talk in which no one is ever the agent.

**3. Feelings are evidence of needs, and needs are universal.** The move that makes the method practicable is the reinterpretation of emotion as information about a condition rather than a verdict on a person. Anger, in this account, points at an unmet need, and needs such as respect, safety, meaning, and freedom are ones every reader shares. That universality is what makes empathy possible in a conflict: if the other party is also trying to meet needs, then a solution which meets neither side's is simply a bad solution rather than a defeat. The Center for Nonviolent Communication states the assumption without hedging: "We believe that all our actions — anything anyone ever does — are attempts to meet needs" ([CNVC](https://www.cnvc.org/about/purpose-of-nvc)).

**4. A request can be refused; a demand cannot.** Rosenberg's test is operational rather than moral. "A demand explicitly or implicitly threatens listeners with blame or punishment if they fail to comply," and the way to tell the difference in your own speech is to notice what you do when the answer is no. If the refusal produces a penalty, the request was a demand. His own lesson in this came from parenting, where he concluded that "we can never make people do anything," only make them wish they had complied, and that the wishing has a cost he eventually paid.

**5. Empathy is the other half, and the harder one.** The method as usually remembered is speaking in four steps. Rosenberg's account of listening is more demanding: "Empathy is a respectful understanding of what others are experiencing" and that it "calls upon us to empty our mind and listen to others with our whole being" ([CNVC](https://www.cnvc.org/learn/what-is-nvc)). The obstacle is that advice, reassurance, and our own story arrive faster than understanding does, and every one of them is a way of not listening. The book treats empathic presence as a discipline with its own failure modes, including the need to give oneself empathy after failing at it.

## The Architecture

Fourteen chapters, xix plus 244 pages, in the third edition of 2015 from PuddleDancer Press, with a foreword by Deepak Chopra and a bibliography at pages 220 to 222 ([library record](https://library.mlc-wels.edu/Record/422119)). The publisher reports more than eight million copies in more than forty languages ([PuddleDancer](https://nonviolentcommunication.com/product/nvc/)).

| Chapters | Subject |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Giving from the heart; the four forms of communication that block compassion |
| 3–6 | The four components: observation, feeling, need, request |
| 7–9 | Receiving empathically; the power of empathy; compassion toward oneself |
| 10–12 | Expressing anger fully; mediation; the protective use of force |
| 13–14 | Liberating oneself and counseling others; expressing appreciation |

The division is worth noticing. Six chapters build the process, three apply it inward, and three handle the cases that a purely gentle method would prefer to avoid: anger, third-party conflict, and force. The chapter on protective force is the one that keeps the book from being a posture, because it admits that some situations call for stopping someone rather than persuading them.

## A Respectful Disagreement

**The hardest step is placed first, and the book is lighter on it than on the others.** Separating an observation from an evaluation sounds like a grammatical instruction and is closer to a spiritual discipline. "You were late three times this week" is an observation. "You don't respect my time" is a judgment, and most speakers cannot feel the difference until they have been caught in it a few times. Rosenberg's examples are clean because he is the one constructing them; a reader in a real argument has to perform the separation while angry, which is where the book's confidence in its own sequence is least helpful.

**The method cannot detect its own misuse.** Four steps are easy to say insincerely, and a speaker who learns the vocabulary without the consciousness produces something worse than plain speech: a judgment wearing the uniform of a need. "I feel unsafe when you disagree with me in meetings" is not an observation, a feeling, or a need, but it scans as all three. The book's defense is that the consciousness comes first and the language second, which is honest and also unenforceable, since the reader is the only auditor of their own sincerity.

**"Never make demands" collides with legitimate authority.** A parent, a manager, and a police officer all hold positions in which a request that can be refused is not always available. Rosenberg's answer is the protective use of force in chapter twelve, and his distinction between protective and punitive force is genuinely useful. But the boundary is drawn by the person using the force, in the moment, under pressure, which is precisely the moment at which the actor's judgment is least reliable. The framework's remedy for unilateral judgment is a unilateral judgment about whether the force is protective.

**The universal-needs claim carries more weight than the evidence in the book.** Treating needs as universal is what makes the method work across a conflict, and the book supports it with Rosenberg's own decades of mediation and with research he cites, including O.J. Harvey's finding that cultures which classify people as good or bad show more violence than cultures which think in terms of needs. That is a correlation reported in a chapter, not a body of trials, and it cannot establish that the list of needs is complete or that it is the same list everywhere. The method may well work; the book shows that it has worked for its author.

---

## Bottom Line

*Nonviolent Communication* is a method with a metaphysics underneath it, and the metaphysics is the part that lasts: people do what they do to meet needs, judgment is a report on the judge, and force is for protection rather than instruction. The four steps are the trainable surface of those claims.

What stays with you is the test of a demand. Watch what you do when someone says no. If the no costs them something, you were not asking, and the whole book is an argument for learning the difference.
