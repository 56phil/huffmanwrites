Yesterday I wrote that the order which stopped every departure in the United States carried no reason, and that the blank field was the most accurate thing either document said.[^1] That is still true of the orders. It is no longer true of the agency.

At some point on Sunday, the Federal Aviation Administration posted a page titled "Nationwide Ground Stop: Questions and Answers," dated November 15, 2026, and answered sixteen questions in writing.[^2] It is the first document in this affair that says where.

The answer is two places, and one of them is three weeks old.

## Two locations

The FAA's own sentence:

> "The initial investigation indicates that two potential locations as the reason for the excessive data flooding the systems: a radar site in Amarillo, Texas and a radio communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas."[^2]

El Dorado is where the work was happening. From the same answer: "L3 Harris, the FAA's current network service provider, had been doing work at the El Dorado site, including deployment of a new router."[^2]

Amarillo is where the network had been changed already. Secretary Sean Duffy, on ABC's "This Week" on Sunday, described the sequence: "When the router in that area was turned on, we saw that in Amarillo, Texas, a system we had replaced about three weeks earlier, that started to flood our system as well."[^3]

Read that carefully, because it is a different shape from the story everyone was told on Saturday. On Saturday the account was one bad device at one remote site. On Sunday it is two sites, in two states, three weeks apart, and the second one only became a problem when the first one came online. A site that had been running quietly since late October began flooding the network at the moment a new router was switched on in Arkansas. Neither machine was sufficient on its own. The fault was in the relationship.

The local reporting supplies the physical detail the agency's answer omits. Amarillo's KVII, which had a camera at the site, identifies it as a Remote Center Air/Ground Communication facility next to Highland Park High School, one of the unmanned radio outposts that a Center operates remotely to extend voice coverage to pilots, in this case the Albuquerque Center.[^4] The agency's word for it is "radar site"; the local account describes an RCAG. Both descriptions may be of the same property. What matters for the argument is that it is unmanned, that it is remote, and that somebody replaced it three weeks before the outage without anything going wrong, until something did.

## The number that is the news

The question that produced the most consequential answer was the last one. How will the FAA prevent a recurrence?

> "We believe we have addressed the initial cause of the disruption by immediately disabling the functionality of the originating routers. As a precaution, we disabled the same functionality on approximately 60 similar routers in the system."[^2]

Sixty. The Secretary put the fleet slightly higher on Sunday: "we have 69 of these routers in the national airspace. We've stopped their ability to broadcast."[^3] The two counts may describe the same fleet with the originating machines included or excluded; nobody has published the list. Either way the arithmetic is the story. A failure that needed two sites to express itself was answered by changing the behaviour of sixty-odd machines across the country.

That is not a repair. It is a concession, written in the passive voice of an agency doing the right thing. If one router in El Dorado could destabilise the network through a function it shared with its siblings, then the exposure was never the router. It was the function, and the function was on every one of them. The FAA has now disabled a behaviour on sixty machines whose purpose it has not described, and no document in the record says what capability the airspace gave up to regain stability. It may be nothing anyone needs. It is not nothing, and it is not stated.

Duffy was asked whether this was sabotage. His answer is worth keeping whole, because it is the most honest sentence either official has said:

> "We don't think that someone tried to sabotage the network on the install at all. But we don't actually have the root cause, which is why we do the investigation."[^3]

He also said the router is in an L3Harris laboratory in Florida with FAA engineers on site, that the FBI has offered assistance, and that the agency sees "no indication that this was a hack."[^3]

## The sentence that is true of the victims

One answer in the Q&A will be quoted for months, and it deserves to be read twice:

> "No, this is due to the legacy TDM system causing instabilities. None of the upgraded towers that received modifications from the down payment in the Working Families Tax Cuts went down. This type of work has been going on for years to transition from old copper wires that are being disconnected due to their obsolescence. It is important to distinguish routine equipment upgrades from the FAA's broader modernization effort."[^2]

The question was whether the modernisation caused the outage. The answer says no, and then describes, in the answer immediately before it, a legacy network destabilised by data flooding from a newly deployed router.

Both statements are true, and they are about different objects. The towers that received the fiber upgrade did not fail. What failed was equipment the agency itself classifies as legacy, and what provoked the failure was a machine that had just been installed. The sentence defends the towers. It does not address the router. An agency asked whether its modernisation caused an outage answered with a fact about the equipment that suffered it.

The distinction the answer draws, between "routine equipment upgrades" and "the FAA's broader modernization effort," is doing a great deal of work for one sentence. The router deployment at El Dorado is not an unrelated act of housekeeping. It is a step in the copper-to-fiber conversion the same page says "has been going on for years," the programme Congress funded with $4.75 billion for telecommunications modernization and that the administration told Congress stood at 68 percent complete the day before the outage.[^2][^5] Calling the individual installation routine is fair. Treating it as separable from the programme that schedules it is the claim that has not been established.

## Why a Friday afternoon

One question in the Q&A was evidently anticipated, and it is the one I would have asked:

> "Maintenance at major air traffic control facilities is typically conducted at night, when there's less traffic. This disruption was a result of a routine replacement at a remote facility, which has regularly been done for decades, without any impacts to the systems at these times. However, based on what happened Friday, we will reevaluate when we do replacement/maintenance work."[^2]

That last sentence is the first commitment of its kind in the record: an admission that the timing of this work is now a question the agency has to answer. It is also, read beside the union's statement from the day before, a description of a decision made without the people who normally make it. The Professional Aviation Safety Specialists said on November 14 that "PASS-represented technicians were not involved in the upgrades that led to the ground stop yesterday," under a protocol in which FAA employees ordinarily conduct the risk assessment and coordinate the maintenance.[^6] The FAA has not confirmed that, and the Q&A does not address it. What the agency now says, in its own words, is that it will reconsider when it does this work. A risk assessment is the mechanism by which such a reconsideration would ordinarily be carried out, and by the union's account it did not happen here.

## The counts

The Q&A settles two numbers that had been running loose.

> "We began experiencing instability at 17 of our 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers, and it spread into several Terminal Radar Approach Controls."[^2]

Seventeen was, until Sunday, a figure attributed to ABC's Australian service. It is now the agency's own, with the additions that the instability crossed into terminal facilities and that approximately 3,500 aircraft were in the air at the time.[^2]

The second is the cancellation count, and it does not agree with the flight tracker.

> "On Friday, Nov. 13, following a telecommunications failure that prompted a nationwide ground stop, operators canceled approximately 12,462 flights, including 9,082 cancellations by major airlines, out of an abundance of caution. While not all cancellations were directly caused by the nationwide ground stop, all flights landed safely."[^2]

FlightAware's settled figure for Friday, as recorded in the morning pass on November 15, was 9,201 cancellations among flights to, from or within the United States, and 9,938 worldwide.[^7] The agency's 12,462 is larger than the tracker's worldwide total, which means the two are not counting the same population. The agency counts what operators cancelled. The tracker counts flights it tracks. Neither is wrong and neither is the airspace's bill.

The more interesting phrase is the one in the middle: "While not all cancellations were directly caused by the nationwide ground stop." That is the agency reserving a distinction it did not need before the number became large.

## The money, in both directions

Duffy spent Sunday making an argument that has been building since Thursday, and it now has a shape.

> "They gave me money for tower -- for radios, money for radar, money for systems. But I don't have any money in that $12.5 billion for a new architecture for telecom. I don't have any money given by Congress to build a new software system to manage the airspace."[^3]

He said AT&T, Verizon and Lumen are "ready to start working tomorrow" on a new architecture, and that it "would be done by the end of 2028 or possibly the first quarter of 2029."[^3] That is a concrete claim with a date, which is more than most of this week's statements offer, and it is falsifiable in a way that matters: a network built from a standing start in 24 to 30 months would be the fastest such programme in the record this series has assembled.

The counter-argument arrived the same weekend, from the two ranking Democrats on the committees that fund the agency:

> "Congress approved $12.5 billion for the Department of Transportation to make necessary safety upgrades to our air traffic control system. Fifteen months later, the administration's obligated $4.3 billion but only spent $1 billion, leaving $8 billion unobligated and $11 billion unspent. Explain to Congress, again, why they need more money?"[^8]

Both claims can be true. "Obligated" and "spent" are different stages of the same pipeline, and a statute that names line items can bind an agency without giving it a general telecom account. What the two statements together establish is the shape of the dispute: the agency says the money it has cannot be used for the thing that failed, and the appropriators say most of what they gave has not left the Treasury. Neither claim has been tested against the other by any document I can cite.

## What is still unanswered

Five questions were open when the Q&A was published. Monday morning closes one of them and leaves the rest.

The advisory index for today, read at 5:30 this morning, carried 21 rows and no national restriction: an operations plan, launch-window and route notices, a volcanic-activity bulletin from Popocatépetl, a ground delay program at San Francisco, and a ground stop at Las Vegas that the Command Center cancelled fourteen minutes later.[^9] That is what an ordinary morning looks like in that index. It is not a clean bill of health, because the index never carried the national stop as an entry either. It is the state of the operation, and the operation is open.

The Inspector General of the Department of Transportation has published nothing on the outage as of this morning; the newest items on its FAA page are audits initiated, not reported.[^10] No root-cause report exists. The cutover's resumption is undated: the agency says it "will reevaluate when we do replacement/maintenance work," and nothing published since says when that reevaluation will conclude.[^2]

Three questions have no owner at all. Nobody has said whether the same cutover method ran at the other sites already converted, where an incident of the same kind might have been absorbed without anyone noticing. Nobody has said what the broadcast function was for, or what the airspace loses with it disabled on sixty machines. And nobody has said whether the Amarillo site's three-week-old replacement was itself a fault waiting for a peer, or an ordinary site that behaved properly until another machine addressed it.

The first piece promised a fuller accounting in early December, once the findings were in hand. This is the interval, not the accounting. What has changed in three days is that the blank field is gone: the agency is answering questions now, in writing, with a date on the page, and the answers are better than their reputation will be if the root cause never arrives. Two sites named. Seventeen centers named. Sixty routers named. The questions left are the ones the answers raise, and an investigation exists to answer them.

I would rather have this page than the silence before it. I would rather have it, too, than the press conference that preceded it, which explained nothing on Friday and asserted a cause on Saturday that the Sunday document declines to confirm. The written answer is a form of restraint. It is also the only document that states the size of the remedy.

The airspace this morning is being carried by equipment that has had a function removed from it, by an agency that says it does not yet know why, at a cost to the operation nobody has quantified, on a modernization schedule the Government Accountability Office described two months ago as 11,389 project schedules that are not integrated, with the Thanksgiving travel period opening on Friday.[^5] Everything about that sentence is either in the documents cited here or in the ones cited in the piece before this one. None of it is reassuring, and all of it is the record.

The stop was the safety action. What remains avoidable is the next one: whichever site comes next, somebody will switch on a machine during a replacement the agency calls routine, and the exception will either announce itself on the spot or wait three weeks for a peer in another state to wake it.

Think clearly. Live intentionally. Love deeply.
