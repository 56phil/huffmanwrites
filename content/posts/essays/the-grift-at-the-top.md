I have written about corruption at the summit before. [*Corruption at the Summit*](/posts/civics/corruption-at-the-summit/) argued the constitutional case: that when public power serves private gain, the republic corrodes. [*The Corruption Tax*](/posts/civics/the-corruption-tax/) argued the economic case: that graft misallocates capital, raises the cost of borrowing, and falls hardest on those with no access. Both essays used the word *corruption*, and both treated it as a moral and institutional failing.

This essay is about something different, or something worse. It is about **grift**.

The word matters. *Grift* entered American underworld slang around 1906, probably as a variation of *graft*, and it carries a specific meaning: a confidence game, a swindle, the systematic extraction of money through deception rather than force ([Etymonline](#sources)). The grifter does not steal. The grifter persuades. The mark believes he is getting something for nothing, or something for loyalty, or something because he is special. The grift is the con at scale, and the con at scale is what the highest levels of government have become.

## What a grift looks like from the inside

A grift has a structure, and the structure is worth naming because it repeats.

First, there is the **hook**: a promise, a persona, a product that seems too good to refuse. Second, there is the **lever**: a position of power, an office, a relationship that makes the promise credible. Third, there is the **harvest**: the moment when belief converts into money, and the money flows to the grifter. Fourth, there is the **exit**: the grift ends, or transforms, or the grifter moves to the next con while the marks are left holding the bag.

Corruption in the old sense was transactional. An envelope changed hands, a contract was steered, a pardon was sold. It was hidden because it was illegal, and it was shameful because it was recognized as wrong. The grift is different in three ways that matter.

It is **open**. The modern grift does not hide. It is announced on social media, defended on television, celebrated by its beneficiaries. The Qatar jet was not a back-channel bribe; it was a gift that the president described as "a very public and transparent transaction" ([ABC News, 2025a](#sources)). The meme coin dinner was not a secret fundraiser; it was a contest with a public leaderboard, advertised as "the most EXCLUSIVE INVITATION in the world" ([Reuters, 2025c](#sources)).

It is **systemic**. A bribe corrupts one transaction. A grift corrupts the relationship between the citizen and the state itself. When the president's family launches a cryptocurrency and the top buyers dine with the president while the administration writes the rules for cryptocurrency, the transaction and the policy have become the same event. There is no longer a line between public office and private enrichment because the office has been converted into the product.

It is **defended as virtue**. The corrupt official hides. The grifter boasts. He tells you that he is smart for doing it, that everyone else is stupid for not doing it, that the rules are for losers. This is not shamelessness. It is a marketing strategy, and it works on an audience that has been taught to expect betrayal.

## The anatomy, in public

Let us look at the record, because the record is not ambiguous.

**The jet.** In May 2025, the United States accepted a Boeing 747-8 from the government of Qatar, valued by aviation experts at roughly $400 million, to serve as a temporary Air Force One while Boeing's replacement aircraft are delayed ([ABC News, 2025a](#sources); [ABC News, 2025b](#sources)). The arrangement, as first reported, called for the aircraft to be transferred to the Donald J. Trump Presidential Library Foundation shortly before the president leaves office. The president defended the gift by calling it "a gift to the Department of Defense," and when asked what he would say to people who viewed it as a personal gift, he answered: "I would never be one to turn down that kind of an offer" ([ABC News, 2025a](#sources)). Senator Chuck Schumer called it "not just bribery, it's premium foreign influence with extra legroom" ([ABC News, 2025a](#sources)).

**The coin.** In January 2025, days before the inauguration, a meme coin bearing the president's name launched. Two Trump-affiliated entities control 80 percent of the supply, and the coin's code directs a fee from every transaction to wallets tied to the project's creators ([NBC News, 2025](#sources)). In May, the top 220 holders were invited to a black-tie dinner at the president's golf club in Virginia. The buyers spent an estimated $148 million to attend, with an average of $1.8 million per person ([Reuters, 2025c](#sources); [NBC News, 2025](#sources)). The top buyer was Justin Sun, a Chinese-born crypto entrepreneur who had invested $75 million in the Trump family's other crypto venture, World Liberty Financial; one month after that investment, the SEC under the Trump administration moved to pause a fraud case against him ([ABC News, 2025c](#sources); [CNBC, 2025](#sources)). The top 25 holders received a tour of the White House the following day. Senator Chris Murphy called the dinner "maybe the most corrupt of all of the corruption" ([CNBC, 2025](#sources)). Senator Elizabeth Warren called it "an orgy of corruption" ([CNBC, 2025](#sources)).

**The stablecoin.** Also in May 2025, the Trump family's World Liberty Financial announced that its USD1 stablecoin had been selected to close a $2 billion investment by the Abu Dhabi firm MGX into Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange ([Reuters, 2025d](#sources); [ABC News, 2025c](#sources)). The exchange's founder, Changpeng Zhao, had been pardoned by the president in October 2025 after pleading guilty to money-laundering violations and serving four months in prison ([Reuters, 2025a](#sources); [AP News, 2025](#sources)). Senator Warren described the pardon in a statement: "First, Changpeng Zhao pleaded guilty to a criminal money laundering charge. Then he boosted one of Donald Trump's crypto ventures and lobbied for a pardon. Today, Donald Trump did his part and pardoned him" ([CNBC, 2025](#sources)).

**The pardons, plural.** Zhao was not alone. In January 2025, the president pardoned Ross Ulbricht, the founder of Silk Road, who was serving two life sentences ([Reuters, 2025b](#sources); [U.S. Department of Justice, 2025](#sources)). In March, he pardoned the three founders of the BitMEX exchange, who had pleaded guilty to Bank Secrecy Act violations ([Decrypt, 2026](#sources)). Each pardon cleared the way for a crypto figure whose companies had business before the administration, or business with an administration that was simultaneously writing the rules for the industry in which those figures operated.

**The son-in-law.** Jared Kushner left the White House in January 2021. Within months, he had raised $2 billion from Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund for his new private equity firm, Affinity Partners. The fund's own investment committee had objected, citing Kushner's lack of private equity experience, and the board, led by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, overruled the objection ([Senator Ron Wyden, 2024](#sources)). By March 2025, Affinity's assets had grown to $4.8 billion, almost entirely from Gulf sovereign wealth funds ([Reuters, 2025e](#sources)). In September 2025, Kushner was back at the center of negotiations, working alongside envoy Steve Witkoff on the Gaza peace deal, while his fund was simultaneously closing a $55 billion takeover bid for Electronic Arts together with Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund ([ABC News Australia, 2025](#sources)). The White House press secretary defended his role: "Jared is donating his energy and his time to our government, to the president of the United States, to secure world peace, and that is a very noble thing" ([ABC News Australia, 2025](#sources)).

**The documentary.** In January 2025, Amazon agreed to pay a reported $40 million to license a documentary about the First Lady, outbidding Disney and Paramount. Follow-up reporting indicated that Melania Trump would receive at least $28 million, more than 70 percent of the deal's proceeds, and that she had separately sought sponsorships from CEOs who attended her husband's inauguration, with acknowledgments in the film's credits in exchange for contributions of at least $10 million ([Decider, 2025](#sources); [New York Post, 2025](#sources)).

**The stock.** Trump Media & Technology Group, the company that operates Truth Social and is majority-controlled by a trust managed by the president, went public in March 2024. On its first day of trading, the company closed at nearly $8 billion in market value, with the president's stake worth more than $4.5 billion ([Forbes, 2024](#sources)). For fiscal year 2025, the company reported revenue of $3.68 million and a net loss of $712.3 million ([Trump Media & Technology Group, 2026](#sources); [StockAnalysis.com, 2026](#sources)). A company with single-digit millions in revenue, valued in the billions: the valuation is not a measure of the business. It is a measure of the name on the building, the platform behind it, and the expectation that political power will keep the price where the owner needs it to be.

**The reversal.** In 2020, the president signed an executive order seeking to ban TikTok, citing national security concerns about the Chinese-owned platform. In 2024, as a candidate, he joined TikTok and opposed the ban. After taking office, he signed an executive order delaying the ban's enforcement and floated the idea of the United States taking partial ownership of the company ([BBC, 2025](#sources); [PolitiFact, 2025](#sources)). Asked why he had changed his position, he answered: "Because I got to use it" ([BBC, 2025](#sources)). He also said, at a signing ceremony: "Every rich person has called me about TikTok" ([BBC, 2025](#sources)).

Each of these is a separate story. Together they are a pattern, and the pattern is the point.

## The con and the country

Here is what the grift understands, and what its critics do not: **the audience is not stupid, and the audience is not innocent.**

The people buying $TRUMP coins are not victims of a sophisticated fraud in the classic sense. They know the coin has no utility. They know it is a bet on access, on influence, on the possibility that proximity to power will pay off. They are not being deceived about the product. They are being sold the one thing the grift has always sold: the feeling of being inside, of being smart, of being on the winning side of a game that everyone else is too stupid to see.

This is why the grift is more durable than corruption. A bribe can be prosecuted. A grift gets votes. The grifter's supporters do not want the con to end. They want to be the ones running it next time, or at least the ones invited to the dinner. The cynicism that corruption produces ("they're all crooks") is replaced by a worse cynicism: "they're all crooks, so I might as well be one, or follow one, or buy the coin."

The grift also understands something about the modern presidency that the norms of the old republic did not anticipate. The president is no longer just a policymaker. He is a brand, a media property, a licensing opportunity. Every appearance is content. Every decision is a signal to the market. Every foreign leader who wants something knows that the fastest route to the Oval Office runs through a Trump hotel, a Trump golf club, a Trump coin, or a Trump family investment fund. The office has become a funnel, and the funnel points down.

## What the grift costs

The costs are not abstract, and they are not only moral.

Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index scored the United States 64 out of 100, its lowest-ever ranking, and attributed the decline in part to the misuse of enforcement discretion and the selective loosening of rules for politically connected actors ([Transparency International, 2026](#sources)). The International Monetary Fund has estimated that bribery alone moves $1.5 to $2 trillion through the global economy each year, roughly 2 percent of world GDP ([IMF, 2017](#sources)). Those figures describe the world the grift produces: a world in which the rules are negotiable, the enforcement is selective, and the price of both is paid by everyone who cannot negotiate.

The grift also prices into markets. When the president's family earns more than $1.4 billion from crypto ventures in a single year while the government writes crypto law ([Reuters, 2026](#sources)), investors learn that the rules are not rules. They learn that the referee has a stake in the game. The risk premium on American assets rises, not because of any single policy but because of the demonstration that the game is fixed. That premium is paid by every American who holds a Treasury bond, a mortgage, a retirement account.

And the grift costs the republic something that no index measures: the belief that public service is a public trust. When the president's son-in-law negotiates peace in Gaza while taking billions from the governments whose interests are at stake in Gaza, the peace process becomes a business negotiation, and the Palestinians and Israelis become counterparties in someone else's deal. When the president pardons a crypto founder who lobbied for the pardon while his family's stablecoin processes billions for the founder's exchange, the pardon power becomes a closing argument. When the First Lady sells a documentary while her husband occupies the office she is documenting, the office becomes a set.

## Why the old answers do not work

The standard remedies for corruption do not fit the grift, because the grift is designed to evade them.

**Prosecution** requires a quid pro quo: this money for that act. The grift is structured to avoid it. The jet was given to the Department of Defense, not to the president. The pardon was an exercise of constitutional authority, not a sale. The coin was bought by volunteers, not solicited. The documentary was a private contract, not an official act. Each step is legal. The grift is the sum of legal steps, and no single step is the crime.

**Disclosure** requires the public to care. Financial disclosure forms list the income from crypto ventures, the fees from the investment fund, the licensing deal. The forms are filed. The voters who support the grifter do not read them, and the voters who read them already knew. Disclosure without shame is not accountability. It is advertising.

**Elections** require the alternative to be better. The grift does not run on policy. It runs on the promise to keep running the con, but for you this time. The voter who buys in is not choosing a platform. He is choosing a team.

**Norms** require both sides to enforce them. The grift's genius is that it dares the other side to object. When a senator calls the pardon corruption, the response is not denial but escalation: you are the corrupt one, you are the partisan one, you are the one who hates America. The norm is broken, and the breaker is rewarded with the attention that breaking it produces. Every outrage cycle is a fundraising cycle. Every scandal is content.

## What would actually work

The remedies are structural, and they are known, because other countries have built them and the American republic once believed in them.

**Divestiture, real and blind.** A president who enters office with business holdings should be required to divest them into a blind trust managed by an independent trustee, with no communication, no report, and no return of the assets until the office is left. Not a trust managed by the president's children. Not a trust that the president can describe in an interview. A blind trust, enforced by law, with criminal penalties for violation. The current arrangement, in which the president's assets are held in a trust managed by his children while he makes decisions that affect their value, is not a firewall. It is a window.

**A pardon power with a record.** The Constitution gives the president the power to pardon, and the power is nearly absolute. But the exercise of the power can be made transparent in ways it currently is not: a written statement of reasons, published before the pardon takes effect, subject to congressional override by a supermajority. Not a limit on the power. A requirement that the power be exercised in daylight, with the reasoning on the record. The pardon of a convicted money launderer who lobbied for the pardon, issued without explanation, is not an exercise of mercy. It is an exercise of the con, and the con requires no explanation because explanation would end it.

**Enforcement that follows the money.** The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act made it a crime for American companies to bribe foreign officials. Its enforcement was paused in 2025, and its resources were reduced ([Transparency International U.S., 2026](#sources)). The same administration that paused the FCPA was accepting gifts from foreign governments, licensing its brand to foreign-funded ventures, and writing rules for an industry in which foreign capital flows through the president's family. The enforcement apparatus does not need to be politicized to be captured. It only needs to be starved, and the starving is announced as deregulation.

**A Congress that remembers it is a branch.** The Constitution gives Congress the power of the purse, the power of impeachment, and the power to set the rules for the executive. A Congress that will not use those powers is not a check. It is an audience. The senators who called the dinner "an orgy of corruption" and then voted for the GENIUS Act anyway were not being hypocritical in any way that matters to the grift. They were being exactly what the grift needs: critics who provide the outrage that fuels the base, and votes that enable the system.

## The honest word

I keep returning to the word *grift* because the other words have been spent. *Corruption* sounds like a scandal from another era, something that happens in other countries, something that resignations fix. *Conflict of interest* sounds like a technicality, a form to be filed. *Self-dealing* sounds like a civil complaint. The grift is none of these. It is a confidence game run from the highest office in the land, and its practitioners are not embarrassed because they have convinced themselves, and half the country, that the game is the whole point.

An earlier essay asked what high-level corruption costs a republic. It costs trust, it costs law, it costs the moral claim on obedience. The grift costs the same things, plus one more: it costs the shared belief that the con is not the system. When the president is the grifter, the citizen cannot look away, because the grift is performed in the citizen's name, with the citizen's power, and at the citizen's expense. The mark in this con is not the fool who bought the coin. The mark is everyone who still believes that the office means something.

The grift ends in one of two ways. Either the mark wises up, or the grifter runs out of marks. A republic that has been grifted does not automatically recover. It has to decide, deliberately, that it will not be grifted again: that the next president will divest, that the next pardon will carry a reason, that the next coin will be regulated like the security it is, and that the next dinner will be held somewhere the president is not selling.

That decision is not the president's to make. It is ours. The con works only as long as we keep buying.
