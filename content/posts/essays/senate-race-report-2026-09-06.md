*Note: I will repeat this report weekly, beginning September 6, 2026.*

**Question:** Nine weeks out, is the fight for the Senate real, or is this the usual mirage that evaporates by November?

**Answer:** It is real, and it is moving. Every rating change this week favored the Democrats: Sabato's Crystal Ball moved Texas to a toss-up and Kansas to "likely Republican," and the consensus forecast nudged Mississippi out of the safe column. The two big prediction markets sit within a few points of even, the generic ballot runs six points for the Democrats, and the president's approval has fallen to the lowest levels of his term. Democrats still need a net gain of four, and the path runs through North Carolina, two coin flips in Ohio and Texas, and a bench of races from Maine to Alaska, with Kansas as the margin seat.

---

## What Changed This Week

The week's story is the national environment. President Trump's approval fell to 33% in the Reuters/Ipsos poll, the third straight survey at that level, with the Iran war and inflation driving the decline; the UMass Amherst poll put it at 32%.[^1] The generic ballot favors the Democrats by roughly six points.[^2] Cook Political Report's Jessica Taylor wrote on September 1 that the Senate map has already expanded to nine competitive seats and asked how much bigger it could get, answering "not much," while WIRED's Hugo Lowell drew the 2006 parallel: an unpopular war, sub-40% approval, and gas prices.[^3] Decision Desk HQ's forecast calls the Senate a toss-up, with a 50-50 split the most common simulated outcome.[^4]

The ratings moved with the environment. On August 26, Sabato's Crystal Ball moved Texas from "leans Republican" to "toss-up," the third forecaster to move the race toward the Democrats this month, and moved Kansas from "safe" to "likely Republican."[^5] On August 31, the 270toWin consensus forecast moved Mississippi out of the safe column.[^6]

Texas is now the most-watched race in the country. The University of Texas poll released August 25 showed Talarico ahead 42 to 39, and on August 31 he aired his first attack ad, "Most Corrupt," built on Paxton's 2023 impeachment.[^7] A pro-Paxton super PAC answered September 1 with a $15 million ad campaign, and Talarico's fundraising edge is roughly seven to one.[^8]

Kansas moved too. Sabato's shift to "likely Republican" followed a Democratic poll showing a statistical tie, Marshall 46 to 45, and Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer has added the state to the Democratic battleground map.[^9] Hamilton and Marshall are scheduled to debate at the Kansas State Fair on September 12.[^10]

Ohio's week was defined by Republican anxiety. NBC News reported August 31 that GOP operatives worry Husted "has been getting thrashed," and Husted asked the NRSC to pull an ad built on a grieving family's story, saying they had "suffered enough."[^11] PolitiFact rated the NRSC's central claim about Brown's immigration votes "False" on September 1.[^12]

Maine's race keeps tightening. Troy Jackson has led in every poll since becoming the nominee, including an Abacus Data survey released August 29 showing him up 52 to 43, and the prediction markets now make him a two-to-one favorite.[^13] Collins agreed to four televised debates on August 27; Jackson held out over format, preferring town halls with direct voter questions.[^14]

Alaska's ballot got stranger. David Leslie, the fourth-place finisher in the August 18 primary, withdrew on September 1, elevating a Republican to the general ballot and leaving Mary Peltola as the only Democrat facing three Republican-identified candidates under ranked-choice voting.[^15] The same-name spoiler, Dan J. Sullivan Jr., remains on the ballot.[^16]

New Hampshire's primary is September 8, and the first poll to show John E. Sununu ahead of Chris Pappas, 45 to 43, landed August 26.[^17] Kamala Harris endorsed Pappas on August 31.[^18]

Michigan's week was a debate feud and an apology. El-Sayed led Rogers 47 to 43 in the EPIC-MRA poll released August 31, and Jim Clyburn endorsed him on August 30, but the campaign's attention went to Rogers' refusal to confirm a September 15 debate, which earned him the nickname "Mike Dodgers," and to El-Sayed's apology to the Michigan Democratic Jewish Caucus for March comments about a synagogue attack.[^19]

Nebraska's first Senate debate in six years, at the state fair on September 1, was a brawl: Ricketts called Osborn "Democrat Dan" roughly 25 times, and Osborn said he would not caucus with either party.[^20]

Iowa's Suffolk poll, released August 26, showed a dead heat, Hinson 45 to 41, with the race tied among very likely voters.[^21]

Florida's week was the Hope Florida grand jury report, published August 26, which found the DeSantis administration misappropriated $10 million from a Medicaid settlement and said Moody, then attorney general, "authorized" the settlement. Nixon and all 12 Florida Senate Democrats called for her resignation.[^22]

South Carolina's first independent poll, released September 1, showed Darline Graham up 55 to 42, but the Republican nominee's own primary rival, Ralph Norman, refused to endorse her, telling supporters to write in his name.[^23]

## The Defense

Michigan is the most competitive Democratic seat in the country, and the one that could break the whole path. El-Sayed leads in every poll released this month, the markets give him 65%, and the ratings houses call it a toss-up, but the race is close enough that a single bad week could flip it.[^24]

Georgia is the second. Ossoff leads Collins in every recent poll and holds a roughly twenty-to-one fundraising edge, and the week's news cut against the challenger: new allegations of antisemitism in Collins' orbit on August 27, and three Republican mayors endorsing Ossoff on September 1.[^25]

Minnesota is an open seat in a blue-leaning state. Flanagan leads Tafoya, the former NFL sideline reporter who won the August 11 primary, by about five points in the only recent poll, and the markets give her 92%.[^26]

New Hampshire is the open seat where the primary has not yet happened. Sununu is the heavy favorite for the September 8 Republican primary, and the general election is closer than the ratings suggest: Cook, Sabato, and Inside Elections all call it "lean" or "tilt" Democratic, but the UNH poll shows Sununu ahead.[^27]

The other nine Democratic seats, Colorado, Delaware, Illinois, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, Rhode Island, and Virginia, are not in play.[^28]

## The Offense, Tiered

North Carolina remains the anchor of every path to 51. Cooper leads Whatley by five to thirteen points in every poll, the markets give him 92%, and the week's most striking development was the father of a murder victim saying he was "swindled" into appearing in an ad that falsely implied Cooper released his son's killer early.[^29] There is still no debate agreed, which would make this the first North Carolina Senate race without one since 1998.[^30]

Ohio and Texas are the two coin flips. Brown leads Husted in most polls and the markets give him 53 to 55%, but Husted holds the structural advantage of an appointed incumbent in a state Trump won twice.[^31] Texas is the surprise of the cycle: a scandal-plagued nominee, a seven-to-one money gap, and a state that has not elected a Democrat to the Senate since 1988.[^32]

Maine, Alaska, Iowa, and Nebraska are the bench. Jackson is favored in Maine, Peltola in Alaska, and the two races are the most likely flips after the top three.[^33] Iowa is a dead heat with Hinson narrowly ahead, and Nebraska is the wildcard: Osborn, the independent endorsed by the state Democratic Party, has about a 30% chance on the markets, but he tied Ricketts in a recent poll.[^34]

Kansas is the margin seat. Hamilton trails Marshall by about 80 to 20 on the markets, but the ratings have moved twice this month, the only public poll shows a tie, and the race is on the Democratic battleground map.[^35]

Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi, Montana, and South Carolina are Republican-favored. Moody leads Nixon comfortably despite the grand jury report; Letlow leads Davis in Louisiana despite a controversy over undisclosed stock trades tied to a Meta data-center deal; Hyde-Smith leads Colom in Mississippi, where the consensus forecast moved the race out of the safe column; Alme leads a three-way field in Montana, the first open Senate race there in fifty years, after Steve Daines withdrew in March; and Darline Graham leads Andrews in South Carolina, the first all-women major-party Senate matchup in the state's history.[^36]

The nine safe Republican seats, Alabama, Arkansas, Idaho, Kentucky, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Tennessee, West Virginia, and Wyoming, are not in play.[^37]

## The Math

The consensus forecasters see a 47-47 Senate with six toss-ups: Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Michigan, Alaska, and Maine.[^38] The prediction markets are split: Kalshi prices a Republican Senate at 53%, Polymarket prices a Democratic Senate at 51%.[^39] Decision Desk HQ's model calls the whole thing a toss-up with a 50-50 split the most common outcome.[^40]

The market path is North Carolina, Ohio, and Texas, plus one of Maine, Alaska, Iowa, or Nebraska. That is four flips, and it is what the markets are pricing.

The consensus path is North Carolina, Ohio, Maine, and Alaska, or Iowa. It requires winning the top four, which is harder but not out of reach.

The nightmare is any Democratic loss, most likely Michigan, which would require five flips and a sweep of everything competitive.

The Kansas connection is worth stating plainly. If the Democrats sweep the top three, Kansas is not needed. But if any of those three slips, Kansas, or Nebraska through Osborn, becomes the margin seat. A 51-49 Senate is decided by exactly the kind of long shot Hamilton is running.

## The Close

Nine weeks is a long time in a race this fluid. The environment is the story: a president at 33% approval, a generic ballot running six points for the Democrats, and a war and a trade war that are not going away. The map has expanded to the point where the forecasters are arguing about whether it can expand further, and the markets are within a few points of even.

I have written before about why Kansas matters, and I will not repeat the whole case here. The point for this week is simpler: the race is real. A pastor who has spent 35 years holding a divided congregation together is within a point of an incumbent in a state that has not elected a Democratic senator since 1932, and the forecasters have noticed. That is the margin seat, and it is the reason this report exists.

Election Day is November 3. Between now and then, the debates will happen, the ads will land, and the polls will move. The math is narrow but real: hold the defense, flip North Carolina, win two of the coin flips, and find a fourth seat somewhere in the long tail. That is what 51 looks like, and it runs through places like Kansas.
