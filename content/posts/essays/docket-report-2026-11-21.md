**Question:** What changes when a court stops accepting assurances and asks for the documents?

**Answer:** The government produces them, or it appeals. This week it did both.

Last week's page was written before the ruling it awaited. Judge Ana C. Reyes granted a temporary restraining order on November 14 that keeps East Potomac Park and its golf course open through November 28, and her opinion says plainly what she thinks of the reasons the government gave for closing them. Within four days the government appealed. In the Kennedy Center case, Judge Christopher R. Cooper refused the plaintiff's request for live testimony and ordered the government to hand him the communications it had withheld under the presidential-communications privilege. He heard argument on Thursday and took the question under advisement. In the Epstein files case, the Justice Department filed the certified list of the administrative record Judge Emmet G. Sullivan had ordered and, the same day, appealed the order requiring it to publish the handwritten FBI interview notes. The D.C. Circuit, asked to hurry, moved the final briefs up by three weeks and set argument for March.

## The week's filings

Forty-two entries landed across the five dockets. Thirty are in the golf case, six in the Kennedy Center case, five in the Epstein files case, one at the circuit, and none in the media case. Seventeen of the forty-two are documents. The rest are minute orders, and several of those are only labels for one another, which is what a docket looks like when a judge is issuing orders faster than the clerk can index them.

The golf docket is where the week happened, and it moved in two directions at once. The plaintiffs asked for discovery into how the government ended its lease with the course's operator. The government asked the court to decide the whole question on the papers before the restraining order runs out. The court, in between, put the government on a schedule to brief whether a special master is necessary, which is a different question from the one the government answered a week earlier, when it said a special master was impermissible.

The Epstein files docket produced the week's quietest and most consequential pair of filings. On the same day, the government complied with one order and appealed another.

## The golf course: *D.C. Preservation League v. Department of the Interior*

Where the case stands: the League and two regular players sued in February over the redevelopment of East Potomac. Judge Reyes granted a preliminary injunction on October 26 that bars cutting, topping or substantially pruning any tree in the park absent a court order, with a narrow safety exception, and requires ten business days' written notice before any closure of the course or irreversible modification of the park. The government appealed on October 28 and moved to stay the injunction. Judge Reyes denied the stay on November 9. On November 14 she granted a temporary restraining order against the closure itself.

The restraining order is thirty pages, and its first paragraph is the one to read. "The Department of the Interior has progressed from removing the trees to removing the people from East Potomac Park." The court then does what the government had spent months avoiding: it compares the reasons in the Park Service's Record of Determination with the chronology in the record.

The two reasons were the absence of a golf operator and the condition of the seawall. On the operator, the court found that the Park Service ended National Links Trust's holdover operation as of November 15 and then cited the absence of an operator as of November 15. "But that reverses cause and effect—by design." On the seawall, the court noted that the agency had identified the hazard since 2019, had fenced unsafe sections rather than closing the park, and three days before the government announced the closure had permitted more than thirty thousand runners to circle that same seawall. The court's summary: "The Court, based on the current record, readily finds that these explanations are pretextual."

Pretext alone would not carry the motion. What carried it was the court's finding that "in its haste to meet an arbitrary deadline, the Government has likely violated at least the National Environmental Policy Act and the Administrative Procedure Act," because the Park Service's own regulations required notice and comment before closing a national park and none occurred. The court adds a sentence about the government's own competence: "The Court, hopefully not naively, has more faith in the federal government than that."

The order itself is narrower than the reasoning. The course must remain open and accessible, but the court does not require the government to operate it or to contract with a concessioner. What the government may not do is "use the absence of golf operations as a reason to exclude the public from the Course." The rest of the park south of Buckeye Drive, including Ohio Drive, stays open except in the immediate vicinity of active seawall repair and except for articulated, imminent safety threats, which require notice within 24 hours and a sworn declaration from a senior Park Service employee. The order runs through November 28. It required distribution to Interior and Park Service employees, officers, agents and contractors likely to be affected by noon on November 15, a $1.00 bond by November 20, and it closed by telling the parties that the judge was available on Sunday, November 15, for emergency clarification. The bond was deposited on November 16.

Tuesday's joint status report is where the week's real fight is, and it is a fight about time rather than about law. The parties met on November 17 and could not agree. The plaintiffs want to file a preliminary injunction motion on November 25, take discovery from National Links Trust first, brief through December 7, and hold a hearing on December 8, with testimony from Acting Park Service Director Jessica Bowron and Superintendent Griess. They ask the court to extend the restraining order to December 12, observing that "the 14-day period covered by the Court's November 14 temporary restraining order straddles the Thanksgiving holiday."

The government wants the opposite: a preliminary injunction motion on November 19, a combined opposition and stay motion on November 23, the plaintiffs' combined reply and opposition on November 25, no hearing, and a ruling before the order lapses on November 28. Its brief says the court already has what it needs, and that "any additional witness testimony in this APA case on the subject closure decision, even with a prior finding of bad faith (which Defendants continue to dispute), would be without basis."

No order adopting either schedule appears in the feed. That matters more than it looks. The restraining order expires on November 28, and if the court has not extended it or converted it into a preliminary injunction by then, the bar on closing the park lapses while the case continues.

The plaintiffs spent the week trying to make that conversion harder to win on the existing record. Their motion at ECF 166 asks for leave to subpoena National Links Trust, which held a fifty-year lease from 2020 until 2025 and then stayed on in a holdover capacity until November 15. The theory is stated without hedging: the government "ousted the National Links Trust in November 2026 as a step in implementing the Washington National Plan and to engineer grounds to close the golf course and East Potomac Park by leaving East Potomac Golf Course without an operator."

The government's opposition, filed Friday, is the week's bluntest brief. It argues that discovery in an APA case is "the exception, not the rule," that the record is adequate for the preliminary injunction question, and that the bad-faith finding it traces to the October 19 hearing does not open discovery into a different decision. It closes with Justice Thomas's partial concurrence in *Department of Commerce v. New York*: "It is not difficult for political opponents of executive actions to generate controversy with accusations of pretext, deceit, and illicit motives." It also records something the docket alone does not show: the government says it consented to converting the restraining order motion into a preliminary injunction motion. The plaintiffs' own proposal still contemplates filing one. No order resolves the difference.

Two smaller filings show the restraining order already doing work. On Tuesday the government gave notice of closures under the order's safety exception: a section of Ohio Drive around an open manhole for about four hours on November 16, the Field House while National Links Trust removed its property, and the upper floor of the driving range for about a day. None of them was the seawall. On Friday it gave notice of four permitted road races that will close Ohio Drive from 5:00 to 9:30 a.m. on November 21, November 29, December 6 and December 19, and described the closures as routine. The November 29 race falls after the order expires.

Then there is the inquiry into the government's lawyers, which changed shape this week. On November 13 the government had said a special master was impermissible and that Rule 53 did not allow one over its objection. On November 18 the court recorded that the government "informed the Court it wishes to brief whether a special master is necessary in this case," and set a schedule. The government's joint report proposes a brief by December 10 arguing that "an ethics investigation, including one conducted by a Special Master, is not necessary in this case." That is a narrower position than the one it took a week earlier, and it is the first time the government has offered to argue the merits of the inquiry rather than its legality.

The case is also getting more expensive to run. The plaintiffs' request for a 21-day extension of their response to the summary judgment motion says the parties are "currently engaged in a fourth round of preliminary relief-briefing" and that "proceedings appear likely to multiply in the court of appeals, where two appeals are now pending." Judge Reyes granted it, moving the response to December 21.

Outside the docket, the Commission of Fine Arts met on Thursday and asked the Interior Department for a revised proposal. WTOP reported that the commission received almost 700 comments, only three of them in support, and that it wants the revision at its January 21 meeting. The department's deputy chief of staff, Gregory Wischer, described the concept as one that "would deliver a safe, affordable, world-class public golf course built to championship standards," with Hains Point incorporated into the course. That matters to the litigation because the government's defense rests on the premise that no final plan exists, and a commission asking for a revision is evidence for both sides.

## The Kennedy Center: *Beatty v. Trump*

Where the case stands: Judge Cooper's opinion of May 29 declared the Board's December 2025 renaming void and preliminarily enjoined the March 16 closure decision. The Board voted to close the main building for two years on August 13 and again on September 15, and moved to dissolve the injunction under Rule 60(b)(5) at ECF 88. Discovery closed October 23. Beatty opposed the motion on October 30 in a 51-page brief, and the government replied on November 6. A hearing was set for 2:00 p.m. on November 19 in Courtroom 27A.

The week moved the question last week's page posed, and it moved against the government's preference for a decision on the log alone.

On Tuesday the court denied Beatty's request to put the Center's executive director on the stand, so the hearing would be argument rather than testimony. In the same order it did something more consequential. It directed the government to provide the court with copies of the documents on its privilege log "that have been withheld exclusively on grounds of the presidential-communications privilege to allow for in camera review to assess the validity of Defendants' qualified privilege assertions." It cited *Karnoski v. Trump* for the proposition that a district court "must conduct in camera review before any materials are turned over to Plaintiffs," and *Zolin* for the practice of requiring the party seeking to avoid disclosure to produce the documents for inspection.

The hearing on Thursday took the motion under advisement. No transcript appears in the feed and no order has issued, so this page can report the outcome and not the argument. What the record does show is that the court chose to look at the material rather than accept the characterization of it, which is the same move Judge Sullivan made when he had the handwritten notes brought to him.

Friday's lodging is the follow-through, and it is careful. The government delivered the documents withheld solely on presidential-communications grounds, retaining redactions made under attorney-client privilege. It describes them as "otherwise unmodified email and text-message threads" from May 29, 2026 through the service of the plaintiff's written and document discovery requests on September 23. And it reserves a second fight: not every message in a thread is within the scope of discovery, so the government "reserve[s] the right to seek further redaction of these records on that basis should the Court determine that public disclosure of the documents is warranted."

That reservation is where the next round begins. The court has the documents, and the question is now what it does with the parts it finds unprivileged.

One loose end from last week remains loose. Beatty moved on November 9 for leave to file a three-page sur-reply, arguing that the government's reply raised new evidence and new arguments. The government opposed on November 10. No order on ECF 116 appears in the feed, and the hearing went forward without one.

## The Epstein files: *Phang v. Blanche*

Where the case stands: Judge Sullivan granted a preliminary injunction on June 25, holding that the Epstein Files Transparency Act required disclosure. On September 16 he granted Phang's motion to compel an answer and set the administrative record. The government appealed, and on October 28 the D.C. Circuit denied a stay and left the district court's orders in place. On November 9 Judge Sullivan issued three orders: publish the handwritten FBI interview notes behind three interview reports within fourteen days, produce a certified list of the administrative record by November 17, and report on the foreign-language review by December 10.

Tuesday produced the week's cleanest illustration of how the government is fighting this case. It complied with one order and appealed another, on the same day.

The compliance is ECF 68, the certified list of the administrative record, filed with the certification and the list of contents attached. The order at ECF 65 had rejected the government's argument that a Rule 12(c) motion does not engage the record, and had given the government until November 17 to identify the records on which it relied. It did. The record itself is due to Phang by December 1.

The appeal is ECF 67, a one-paragraph notice that the government appeals the November 9 order at ECF 64. The district court assigned it D.C. Circuit No. 26-5389 on Thursday. That is a separate appeal from the consolidated 26-5299 and 26-5334, and it is not the appeal this page watches.

The notes order has a clock attached, and the clock is what to watch. ECF 64 declined to stay itself, and instead built the stay into the deadline: "if Defendant moves for a stay of this Order and expedited review from the D.C. Circuit prior to the expiration of the fourteen-day period, the Order is TEMPORARILY STAYED pending the D.C. Circuit's resolution of any such motion." The fourteen days run out on Monday, November 23. A notice of appeal is not a motion for a stay and expedited review. Whether the government has filed one in No. 26-5389 is not visible in the dockets this page reads, and so this page does not assert it either way. What is visible is that no notice of compliance appears on the docket as of Friday, and that the order's own terms make the motion, not the appeal, the thing that pauses the deadline.

## The Epstein files: *MSW Media v. DOJ*

Where the case stands: MSW Media sued in August 2025 over two FOIA requests, one for grand jury material and one for the FBI's videos on how to review Epstein records. The grand jury request was dropped; the case now concerns nine training videos, withheld in full under four exemptions. Judge Howell denied expedited processing on July 1, 2026, and the government moved for summary judgment on October 2. The plaintiff's opposition and cross-motion were filed on November 7, and the court granted a nunc pro tunc enlargement on November 10.

Nothing moved this week. That is the finding, and it is worth stating plainly: the quietest of the five dockets stayed quiet. The next deadline falls on dates the feed does not carry. The amended schedule the November 2 minute order issued sets the government's reply and opposition and the plaintiff's reply, and ECF 23 had proposed November 24 and December 10, but the order's text is truncated at its first colon, so this page does not state the dates as the court's. The question before the case remains narrow: whether a disclosure statute's list of permitted withholdings displaces the exemptions an agency would otherwise cite.

## The Epstein files on appeal

Where the appeal stands: the government moved on September 22 to stay the June 25 and September 16 orders and to expedite. On October 28 a panel denied the stay and referred Phang's motion to dismiss the appeal of the September 16 order to the merits panel. On November 5 the clerk set a briefing schedule running from December 15 to final briefs on February 25. On November 6 the government moved to expedite the case and oral argument.

The government got most of what it sought. Friday's per curiam order grants the motion and replaces the clerk's schedule: the appellant's brief is due December 4, the appellee's January 8, the reply January 25, the deferred appendix January 29, and final briefs February 3. The clerk is directed to calendar the cases "for oral argument on the first appropriate date in March 2027." The panel is Millett, Katsas and Rao.

The acceleration is real but modest: the final briefs move up by three weeks, and argument is more than three months away. In the meantime the district court's orders remain in force, because the circuit declined to stay them in October. The government's opening brief will therefore be filed eleven days after the notes deadline and three days after the administrative record is due, which means the circuit will read the government's merits argument alongside the district court's compliance record.

## What the coverage showed

Two accounts are worth citing this week, and both add something the dockets cannot.

NBC News reported the restraining order on Saturday, November 14, and carried the Justice Department's response: "Nothing in the Court's order precludes planning for the development of a world class golf course to continue." It also reported the spokesperson's narrower claim, that the court "has acknowledged that the National Park Service can limit access to its facilities where the public is at risk of harm." Both statements are accurate as far as they go. The order does leave planning and limited safety closures alone. What it forbids is the closure itself.

WTOP reported Thursday's Commission of Fine Arts meeting, including the nearly 700 comments, the three in support, and the request for a revised proposal in January. That meeting is the one place this week where the redevelopment was discussed in public by the agency proposing it, and the docket records none of it.

Several other accounts appeared in search results, including the Associated Press, The Washington Post, The Hill, WUSA9, Courthouse News and BroadwayWorld. None of them served this page's automated fetch, so this page did not read them and does not characterize them. That is a statement about the checker, not about the reporting.
