The Republican Party has been called many things this year, and almost none of them by its own members. A cult of personality. A revenge machine. A rubber stamp. A party that lost its way. The metaphors are all attempts to describe the same spectacle: a governing majority that has become, in practice, a single man's instrument, and that defends the arrangement with the vocabulary of loyalty rather than the vocabulary of policy.

I want to look at the mess directly, and then at the harder question, which is whether it can be cleaned up. Because the two are related in a way the metaphors hide. If the party were merely afraid of one man, it would snap back the day he left. If it is not merely afraid, the problem survives him.

## The mess, named plainly

Begin with the primaries, because primaries are where a party tells you what it actually values.

In May, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton unseated four-term Senator John Cornyn. Paxton had been impeached by a Republican-controlled state House on bribery and corruption charges in 2023 and acquitted by the Republican-controlled Senate; he had carried a years-long cloud of legal and personal scandal; and he won the nomination of the party that once ran on law and order. The Texas Tribune called it, accurately, "a watershed moment for GOP politics in Texas," and noted that Cornyn became "the first senator in Texas history to lose to a member of his own party" since 1970 ([Texas Tribune, 2026](#sources)).

In Louisiana, Senator Bill Cassidy, who had voted to convict Donald Trump at his 2021 impeachment trial, finished third and failed even to reach a runoff. In Kentucky, Representative Thomas Massie, a libertarian who had voted against the president's tax bill and led the push to release the Epstein files, lost his primary to a challenger the president recruited. Afterward, Massie offered the cleanest description of the new rule that anyone has given. He told the BBC that the administration and its allies "want 100% compliance" ([BBC, 2026](#sources)).

Two sitting senators lost their seats to their own party's voters in a single cycle. Before this year, only two had done so in the previous fifteen years.

Now the retirements. More than fifty House members have announced they will not seek reelection, the largest cohort in over three decades; roughly two-thirds of them are Republicans, and the average retiring Republican has served only five terms, meaning the people leaving are not tired elders but mid-career members who have concluded the job is no longer worth having ([Brookings, 2026](#sources)). A party that cannot retain even its mid-career members is not a party that is winning an argument. It is one that is losing its bench.

And then the floor votes, in which the majority has spent its power on things the old party would have rejected on principle.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, passed in 2025, is the clearest case. Presented as fiscal discipline, it is projected to add $3.4 trillion to the deficit over the 2025–2034 period and to leave ten million more people uninsured by 2034 ([AHA News, 2025](#sources)). A party that spent the Obama years waving the debt clock produced a multi-trillion-dollar expansion of the deficit without a serious revolt.

The Iran war, which began on February 28, 2026, produced a second. In June the Senate adopted a war powers resolution directing the president to withdraw from hostilities, 50–48, which CNBC called "an escalation in Congress's effort to reassert its role" over the conflict ([CNBC, 2026](#sources)). Four Republicans broke with their leadership to vote for it. The measure was symbolic, and it still took a war the president had started without asking to produce four defections.

The anti-weaponization fund is the third. The Justice Department proposed a $1.776 billion fund, drawn from a settlement of the president's own lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service, to compensate people who claimed political persecution, potentially including the January 6 defendants the president had pardoned. When the Senate minority leader moved to prohibit it, forty-nine senators voted to kill the fund and fifty voted to keep it alive ([Spectrum News, 2026](#sources)). The party that ran for a decade against slush funds voted to preserve one.

And the Epstein files, whose release Congress ordered by a vote of 427–1 ([ABC News, 2025](#sources)), remain a case study in the distance between what the majority votes and what the executive does.

## What it actually is

The reflex is to explain all of this by fear. Members are afraid of a primary challenge, and so they comply. There is truth in that, but it is an incomplete account, and the incompleteness matters for anyone who wants the party to recover.

Two explanations deserve to be taken seriously. The first is personal: the president disciplines dissent and the base rewards him for it, so the rational member defers. The second, argued bluntly this year by the historian Allan Lichtman, is that the members are not afraid at all. They "agree with him" ([MS NOW, 2026](#sources)); the old language of free markets and small government was always more slogan than conviction, and the president has simply stopped pretending. Lichtman's line is worth sitting with: "Trump and today's GOP majorities are not a betrayal of that tradition," he writes. "They are its culmination."

A third explanation, from political science rather than punditry, is the most unsettling of the three. Jeffrey Stonecash's 2026 study in *PS: Political Science & Politics* tracks the geographical realignment of the two parties from 1952 to 2024 and finds that the president now has something no Republican since Eisenhower has had: a presidential electoral base and a congressional electoral base drawn from the same places. Republican members are elected by the same voters who elected him, in states and districts that now vote the same way at both levels. The deference is not merely imposed from above. It is the predictable behavior of a party whose representatives and whose president answer to one electorate ([Stonecash, 2026](#sources)).

If that analysis is right, it changes the question with which I began. A party that is only afraid of a man will heal when the man is gone. A party whose factions have fused into a single base, and whose officeholders agree with that base, will not. The mess is structural, and the repair has to be structural too.

## The bill, arriving

Structure does have a way of presenting its own invoice, and this autumn the invoice is coming due.

Three-in-ten Americans approve of the president's job performance; sixty-eight percent disapprove. Among the voters who chose him in 2024, approval has fallen from ninety-five percent in early 2025 to sixty-seven percent ([Pew Research Center, 2026](#sources)). In Morning Consult's October tracking, his approval has reached the lowest point of his second term, with Republicans themselves accounting for the decline; in twelve states he remains above water, down from thirty-one at the start of the term ([Morning Consult, 2026](#sources)).

The midterm math points the same direction, and Republican strategists are saying so out loud. That is the honest signal, and it is worth separating from the noise: the people closest to the machinery, the ones who read the same polls and knock the same doors, have stopped pretending the House is safe.

A defeat in November would not by itself fix the party. But a defeat is how parties of the past have rediscovered the will to reform. The internal argument that cannot win in a year of victory can win in a year of loss, because losing changes who is willing to say the unsayable.

## Can it be improved?

Here I want to be careful, because "how can it be fixed" is a question that invites either cynicism or fantasy, and both are useless. So let me name only levers with evidence behind them.

**First, change the rules that select the candidates.** The single most important fact about a modern congressional majority is that most of its members are chosen by a small share of the electorate. As the political philosopher Danielle Allen puts it, "most members of Congress are elected by only 5 to 8% of the electorate in their districts," because gerrymandering and low-turnout primaries mean a candidate who satisfies the most intense partisans can sail into office ([Allen, 2026](#sources)). Allen's proposal is to "abolish taxpayer-funded party primaries" and replace them with an all-party primary, the system Louisiana, California, Washington, and Alaska already use, in which every candidate runs on one ballot and the top finishers advance. The effect is not to favor one party. It is to make every incumbent campaign to the whole electorate, which is the audience a party has to please if it wants to govern rather than posture.

**Second, rebuild the party as an organization, not just a brand.** In late 2025, New America convened forty-two political scientists and practitioners to ask what a healthy party system would even look like. Their findings describe the Republican Party's problem precisely: state and local organizations that appear during election season and vanish afterward; a talent pipeline that no longer runs from local activism to national leadership; and a fundraising apparatus that treats supporters as marks. "It is a grift," one former party activist said of the small-dollar machinery, "borrowed from elder fraud schemes" ([New America, 2026](#sources)). A party that raises money by deception has nothing to offer the people it needs to rebuild, and no institution with which to rebuild.

**Third, and hardest, produce a faction with a program.** Parties have reformed from within before, and almost always the same way: a wing of the party offers a coherent alternative and fights for the name. The Republicans who wrote the modern party out of the ashes of the Whigs in the 1850s did it with a program. The Democrats who rewrote their own nomination rules after 1968 did it after a defeat made the old leadership indefensible. Neither happened because anyone was polite about it, and neither happened during a winning streak.

What is missing today is not courage in the abstract. It is a faction that stands for something. Massie's rebellion was against a war, a spending bill, and a cover-up, all of them things to oppose. A party is rebuilt by people who can state its purpose. On the available evidence, that argument has not yet been made inside the Republican Party, and until it is, the party will keep choosing the loudest voice over the better program.

## What the citizen can do

I hold no brief for the Republican Party, and I am not writing to save it. I am writing because a republic needs two parties that are capable of governing, and it does not have them if one of them has become a machine for validating a single person.

The Stoic part of this is not resignation. It is the recognition of what is and is not mine to control. I cannot talk the party out of its loyalty test, and neither can any essay. What is mine is the honesty to call the arrangement what it is while it is happening, rather than after the history books have agreed, and the discipline to insist on the same standard for whichever party holds power next. That is not a small thing. A democracy survives on the willingness of citizens to keep the record straight in real time.

The party will decide what to do about itself. The voters will decide in November. But the question of whether a party can be pulled back from a personality cult is not really a question about the party. It is a question about whether enough of its members, and enough of its voters, still want a party at all. The mess will resolve when the answer changes.

The gold in the fissure is what people mistake for the thing itself. It is only the light that shows where the break is.
