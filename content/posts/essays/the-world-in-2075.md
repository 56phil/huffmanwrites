The year 2075 is forty-nine years away. That is the same distance as 1977 is from today, and it is worth pausing on what that means. A person born in 1977 has lived through the end of the Cold War, the rise of the internet, the fall of the Berlin Wall, the invention of the smartphone, the first decade of artificial intelligence, and a pandemic. Forty-nine years is a lifetime, and it is also the span in which the world's population will grow by two billion people, the global temperature will rise past thresholds that have never been crossed in human history, and the political order that emerged after 1945 will either be repaired or replaced. This essay is an attempt to look at that year soberly, without panic and without comfort, and to set down the two paths that are actually available. They are not predictions. They are the two futures that the evidence says are possible, and the difference between them is being decided now.

## The Baseline: What Is Already Locked In

Before either path can be described, the shared baseline must be acknowledged, because much of 2075 is already written. The people who will live in it are mostly alive. The climate that will prevail in it is largely determined by the greenhouse gases already in the atmosphere and the emissions trajectory of the next decade. The demographic shape of the century is already visible in the birth rates of today.

The population of the world in 2075 will be roughly ten billion people, up from eight billion today, and it will be close to its peak. The United Nations projects that global population will top out around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s and then begin a slow decline ([UN, 2024](#sources)). The growth is not evenly distributed. All of it will come from the less developed regions, more than half of it from sub-Saharan Africa, whose share of the world's people will rise from 17 percent today to roughly a third by 2075 ([UN, 2024](#sources)). Nigeria will have passed the United States in population. China will have lost a third of its people. Europe and Japan will be older and smaller. The world of 2075 will be a world whose center of gravity has moved south, and no political choice can change that. It is arithmetic.

The climate of 2075 is also largely committed. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change concluded that the world will very likely pass 1.5 degrees Celsius of warming above pre-industrial levels before 2040, no matter what is done now ([IPCC, 2023](#sources)). The question is not whether 1.5 degrees is reached. It is whether the world stops at 1.5, or drifts to 2.7, the median estimate for current policies, or approaches 3 degrees, the central estimate of the IPCC's scenario range ([Hausfather, 2025](#sources); [IPCC, 2023](#sources)). Sea levels will rise by half a meter to a meter by 2100 on current trajectories, and the IPCC does not rule out two to five meters if ice sheet instability accelerates ([IPCC, 2021](#sources)). A billion people live in coastal zones that will face flooding. By 2070, between one and three billion people will live in areas where the average annual temperature exceeds 29 degrees Celsius, a level at which sustained outdoor labor becomes dangerous and agriculture becomes marginal ([IPCC, 2022](#sources)). None of this is speculation. It is the committed future, and both paths begin from it.

The third element of the baseline is technological, and it is the one genuine wild card. Artificial intelligence is already reshaping labor, information, and war. Fusion power, as the prediction markets price it, has a real chance of delivering its first commercial plants in the 2030s and meaningful scale in the 2040s ([Manifold, 2026](#sources)). Biotechnology is advancing at a pace that makes the last century's medical progress look slow. Whether these tools are used to repair the world or to entrench its divisions is not a technological question. It is a political one, and it is the hinge on which the two paths turn.

## The Pessimistic Path: The Fortress World

The pessimistic path is not a catastrophe in the sense of a single event. It is a slow, compounding failure, the kind that is hard to see from inside because each year is only slightly worse than the last. It is the path on which the world drifts to three degrees of warming, the political order fragments, and the gains of the last century are quietly reversed for most of the world's people.

On this path, the climate does not break all at once. It breaks region by region. The tropics become too hot for reliable agriculture, and the breadbaskets of the subtropics dry out. The IPCC's assessment is that on current emissions paths, Africa will lose 30 percent of its maize-growing territory and half of its bean-growing land ([IPCC, 2022](#sources)). The coastal megacities of Asia, home to hundreds of millions, face chronic flooding that no seawall can fully answer. The result is migration on a scale the world has never managed: the contested estimates of climate displacement run to hundreds of millions of people by mid-century, and the small island states, home to 65 million people across 39 countries, are already losing their habitability ([Myers, 2005](#sources); [IOM, 2024](#sources)). On the pessimistic path, this migration is not managed. It is militarized. Borders harden, walls go up, and the countries that caused the least emissions spend their wealth defending themselves from the people who suffer the most from them.

The politics of this path are already visible in miniature. The Varieties of Democracy Institute reports that nearly a quarter of the world's countries were undergoing autocratization in 2025, and that the share of the global population living in autocratizing countries rose from 6 percent in 2009 to 34 percent in 2019 ([V-Dem, 2026](#sources); [V-Dem, 2020](#sources)). The third wave of autocratization that began around 2010 has touched long-established democracies, not just fragile ones. On the pessimistic path, this trend does not reverse. It consolidates. The tools of the century, artificial intelligence for surveillance and control, biotechnology for identity and exclusion, fusion power for the few who own it, are used to make authoritarianism more efficient rather than less. The great powers retreat into spheres of influence, the international institutions that managed the postwar order atrophy, and the world returns to the pattern that prevailed for most of human history: a few wealthy, fortified regions and a large, unstable periphery. The historian's name for this is not dystopia. It is the normal condition of human civilization, and the twentieth century's experiment in something better is the exception.

The human cost is not evenly distributed, and that is the defining moral fact of this path. The IPCC estimates that 3.3 billion people, about 40 percent of the world's population, are already highly vulnerable to climate impacts, with the worst effects concentrated in the developing world ([IPCC, 2022](#sources)). On the pessimistic path, the rich adapt and the poor do not. The wealthy world builds sea walls and air conditioning and indoor agriculture, and the rest of the world absorbs the losses. The inequality that already defines the global order becomes a permanent feature of it, enforced by technology and defended by walls. This is not a prediction that humanity fails. It is a prediction that humanity succeeds for a minority, and that the majority is left to pay the cost of a century it did not choose.

## The Optimistic Path: The Managed Transition

The optimistic path is not a utopia. It is a world that manages its problems badly but in time, the way the twentieth century managed its problems badly but in time. It is the path on which the world stops at two degrees of warming, the political order is repaired rather than replaced, and the technological wild cards are played in the direction of human flourishing. It is harder to describe than the pessimistic path, because it is less dramatic, and that is precisely why it is the more likely of the two if the world behaves as it usually does.

On this path, the climate transition is real but late. The world overshoots 1.5 degrees, as it is already committed to do, but the combination of rapidly falling renewable costs, fusion power arriving in the 2030s and 2040s, and carbon removal at scale brings warming back toward two degrees by the end of the century ([IPCC, 2023](#sources)). The IPCC's own assessment is that all pathways that limit warming to 1.5 or 2 degrees require rapid, deep, and immediate emission reductions, and that carbon dioxide removal is necessary to counterbalance residual emissions ([IPCC, 2022](#sources)). This is not a free lunch. It is a managed transition, with costs, and the costs are paid by everyone. But the alternative, on the evidence, is worse, and the world has a demonstrated capacity to do hard things when the alternative is clear. It ended slavery. It defeated fascism. It vaccinated the planet. The claim that it cannot decarbonize is a claim about will, not about capacity.

On this path, the demographic transition becomes an opportunity rather than a crisis. The world's population peaks and begins to decline, which relieves pressure on resources and climate. The aging societies of the rich world, which on the pessimistic path become stagnant and fearful, instead become the destination for the young populations of Africa and South Asia, and the migration that the pessimistic path militarizes becomes the engine of the optimistic one. The evidence for this is not hypothetical. The countries that have managed migration well, and there are some, have found that it solves the demographic problem that no amount of domestic fertility policy can solve. The world of 2075 on this path is a world in which the movement of people is treated as what it has always been, the oldest and most reliable engine of human progress, rather than as a threat to be stopped.

The politics of this path rest on a fact that the pessimists underweight: democratic backsliding has historically been reversible. The Varieties of Democracy data shows that more than half of all autocratization episodes between 1900 and 2023 took a U-turn shape, in which the slide toward authoritarianism was followed by a return to democracy ([V-Dem, 2026](#sources)). The third wave of democratization in the late twentieth century was itself a recovery from the authoritarian waves of the 1930s and the 1970s. The same data shows that pro-democracy mass mobilization has been rising, with the share of countries experiencing substantial pro-democracy protests reaching 44 percent in 2019, up from 27 percent a decade earlier ([V-Dem, 2020](#sources)). The optimistic path is not a prediction that authoritarianism will fail. It is a recognition that it usually does, eventually, and that the institutions of the postwar order, courts, elections, free presses, alliances, are more durable than their current strain suggests. The republics of 2075 will not look like the republics of 2026. They will be messier, more diverse, and harder to govern. But the alternative to them is not a clean authoritarianism. It is a permanent crisis, and the evidence of the last century is that people eventually choose the mess.

## The Hinge: The Next Fifteen Years

The two paths diverge in the next fifteen years, and this is the most important fact about them. The climate is not decided by 2075. It is decided by the emissions of the 2030s, which are being set by the policies of the next few years. The political order is not decided by 2075. It is decided by whether the current wave of autocratization is reversed in the countries where it is happening now, or whether it is allowed to consolidate. The technology is not decided by 2075. It is decided by whether the coming decade's breakthroughs in artificial intelligence and fusion are governed in the direction of diffusion and access, or in the direction of concentration and control.

This is the sobering part, and it deserves to be stated plainly. The people who will live in 2075 are mostly alive, but they are not the ones who will decide what 2075 looks like. The decisions will be made by the people in power in the next fifteen years, and by the citizens who hold them accountable. A child born today will be forty-nine in 2075, old enough to live with the consequences of decisions made before they could vote. The generation that decides the shape of the century is the generation now alive, and it is deciding badly in some places and well in others. The two paths are not abstract. They are the aggregate of the choices being made this year, next year, and the year after.

There is a temptation, in an essay like this, to end with a call to action, and there is a temptation to end with a warning. Both are warranted, and neither is sufficient. The honest conclusion is that the future is not a prediction and it is not a fate. It is a set of commitments, made by real people, in real time, under conditions of uncertainty. The pessimistic path is the path of drift, the path on which the world does what it is already doing and lets the compounding failures accumulate. The optimistic path is the path of deliberate effort, the path on which the world treats the next fifteen years as what they are, the hinge of the century.

The Stoics had a word for the discipline this requires: prohairesis, the faculty of choice, the one thing that is always in our power even when everything else is not. The world of 2075 will be a world of ten billion people, a warmer planet, and a different distribution of power. That much is written. Whether it is a fortress or a commonwealth is not written. It is being written now, by the choices of the living, and the living includes us.
