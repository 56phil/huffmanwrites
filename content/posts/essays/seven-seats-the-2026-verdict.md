**Question:** Who won the Senate, by how many seats, and what changes on Monday morning for a family that voted because groceries cost more than they did two years ago?

**Answer:** The Democrats won it, with fifty-four seats, having entered the night with forty-seven and lost none. Seven Republican-held seats fell: North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, Ohio, Kansas, Iowa and Maine. They also won the House and a majority of governorships, the first time since 2010 that they have held more statehouses than the Republicans. One Senate race, Alaska, has not been called, and it no longer decides anything.[^1] What changes on Monday is smaller than the numbers suggest and more concrete than the commentary will admit: subpoenas that a committee chairman signs and a minority cannot block, nominations that a floor majority can confirm, and a veto that can now be sustained or overridden depending on which chamber the fight runs through. The prices do not change on Monday. The power to investigate why they are high does.

---

## The Result

The Senate arithmetic is worth stating once, plainly, because so much of what follows rests on it. Forty-seven Democratic seats were not up for election and none of those forty-seven changed hands in a way that cost the party a seat. The thirteen Democratic-held seats on the ballot all stayed Democratic: Georgia, New Hampshire, Michigan and Minnesota were the four that could have gone the other way, and all four were called for the incumbents or their party's nominee.[^1] The Republicans were defending twenty-two seats. Seven of them are now Democratic.

Fifty-four seats is not a working majority that governs by itself, and it is not the sixty that would end a filibuster. It is enough to chair every committee, set every agenda, issue every subpoena that a chairman can issue alone, and confirm judges and agency heads on a party-line vote. It is also enough to lose. Four defections in a fifty-four seat caucus produce a tie that the vice president breaks, which is the arithmetic every Democratic whip will carry into January.

The House matters more to a reader who is not following whip counts, because the House is where investigations begin and where spending starts. The Associated Press declared that the Democrats had won it, and with it unified control of Congress, in the small hours of Wednesday morning.[^1] Hakeem Jeffries is in line to become the first Black speaker of the House when the new Congress organizes on January 3.[^1] The practical meaning for a household is not a bill. It is a hearing: the Oversight Committee can compel documents and testimony from an administration that has faced a Republican Congress for two years, and Representative Robert Garcia, the committee's ranking member, said the two words his party intends to use. "Lawyer up."[^1]

Then there are the governorships. Democrats will hold a majority of governors' offices for the first time since 2010, the AP reported, after flipping governorships in Texas, Ohio, Georgia and Iowa and holding open seats in Maine and Wisconsin.[^1] Governors do not vote on judges. They appoint the officials who run elections, sign or veto state budgets, and decide whether a state cooperates with a federal directive. For a reader in one of those states, that result will touch their life sooner than anything that happens in Washington.

## The Defense

The Democratic path to fifty-one required holding four seats that were not safe: Georgia, New Hampshire, Michigan and Minnesota. All four held.

**Georgia.** Jon Ossoff won re-election over Representative Mike Collins.[^1] He told a crowd in Atlanta that the result was a rebuke of "the depravity, corruption and incompetence of a failed lame duck President Donald J. Trump," and the same evening his party's candidate for governor, Keisha Lance Bottoms, won that office too, making her the country's first Black female governor.[^1] A state that Trump carried in 2024 elected a Democratic senator and a Democratic governor on the same ballot.

**New Hampshire.** Chris Pappas defeated former Senator John Sununu, holding the seat Jeanne Shaheen is leaving, and Sununu conceded before nine o'clock. "This has been an incredible campaign, but it is not our night," he said.[^1]

**Michigan.** The most contested Democratic-held seat on the map went to Abdul El-Sayed over Mike Rogers, called at 2:27 a.m.[^1] El-Sayed will be the first Muslim senator in the country's history, and he won after a primary in which the state's Democratic establishment warned he was a risky nominee and one of his primary rivals, Representative Haley Stevens, endorsed his Republican opponent on the Sunday before the election.[^1] He won anyway, and the primary objection to him was a thing about which the general electorate turned out not to care.

**Minnesota.** Peggy Flanagan held the seat Tina Smith is leaving, defeating Michele Tafoya, and will become the first Native American woman to serve in the Senate.[^1]

## The Offense

Seven seats fell, and they did not fall in the order the forecasters expected.

**North Carolina** was the one everybody saw coming. Roy Cooper, the former governor, defeated Michael Whatley for the seat Thom Tillis left open, and Tillis congratulated him the same night. A state that has not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 2008 sent one on Tuesday.

**South Carolina** was the one nobody saw coming. Annie Andrews, a pediatrician who had never held office, defeated Senator Darline Graham, who was appointed in July after her brother Lindsey Graham died and who had the president's endorsement, two campaign visits and heavy outside support in the closing weeks.[^1] Andrews is the first woman elected to represent South Carolina in the Senate and the first Democrat elected to that seat since 1998, in a state Trump carried by nearly twenty points two years ago.[^1] Her victory speech was not about the party. "The Senate seat does not belong to one family, one party or to Washington, D.C.," she said.[^1]

**Texas** went to James Talarico over Ken Paxton, the first Democratic senator from Texas since Lloyd Bentsen in 1988 and the first Democrat elected to any statewide office there since 1994.[^1] The same ballot elected Gina Hinojosa governor over Greg Abbott, making her the state's first Latina governor and the first Democrat to hold that office since 1990.[^1] Two statewide offices that had been Republican for a generation changed hands in one night.

**Ohio** returned Sherrod Brown to the Senate he left in 2024, defeating Jon Husted, who had been appointed to the seat when JD Vance became vice president.[^1] Brown won about two-thirds of moderates and roughly seven in ten voters under thirty, according to AP VoteCast, against about half of that youngest group two years ago.[^1] A state that has moved toward the Republicans in every recent cycle elected a Democrat to the Senate for the fourth time in twenty years.

**Kansas** and **Iowa** are the two that tell you what kind of night it was. Both are states Trump won by double digits, both were rated Republican-leaning or better for most of the year, and both elected Democrats to the Senate, Iowa for the first time in more than a decade and Kansas for the first time since 1932.[^1] Iowa's Josh Turek defeated Ashley Hinson for the seat Joni Ernst is leaving.[^1] Kansas gets its own section below, because Kansas is the story.

**Maine** ended Susan Collins. Troy Jackson, a late entry to the race, defeated a senator first elected in 1996 and re-elected through every previous wave that was supposed to end her.[^1] The issue mix in Maine was not abstract: heating oil, electricity, groceries, and a trade war with Canada that pressed on tourism, lobstering and logging, which is what the reporting from the state says the campaign concerned.[^6]

**Alaska** remains uncalled, and it is the one place where the night's arithmetic does not reach. Mary Peltola spoke to supporters, the race against Senator Dan Sullivan is not called, ranked-choice tabulation runs after the first count, and the state's own reporting schedule has the bulk of mail ballots arriving no earlier than November 18.[^1] Nothing about control depends on it. It depends on whether a Democrat can win a seat in a state where the last two weeks were consumed by an allegation that her campaign recruited a second candidate named Dan Sullivan.

## Kansas

The series that ends with this piece called Kansas the margin seat. If the top of the map held, Kansas or Nebraska would decide the majority. That is not what happened. Kansas did not decide the majority; it announced it.

Adam Hamilton, the founding pastor of the largest United Methodist congregation in the country, defeated Senator Roger Marshall, and Kansas elected a Democratic senator for the first time since 1932.[^2] He had 51.7 percent of the vote when the AP called it at 10:24 p.m. Central.[^3] Marshall's campaign had the president twice, the vice president twice, and the endorsements that usually follow. It also had a story that would not go away: the New York Times reported that Marshall, an obstetrician before he went to Congress, had sued hundreds of his former patients over medical debt, and Marshall spent the final day of the campaign answering for it.[^2]

What the voters said is more useful than what the analysts said. Andi Welch, of Johnson County, told KCUR she does not always vote for the Democrat on the ballot. "I think he's going to be fair," she said. "I think he's running as a Democrat for the reasons he states, and I think he really is going to be looking out for Kansas citizens."[^2]

Hamilton ran as what he called an independent-minded Democrat, took money from a committee tied to the Senate Democratic leader and then said he would not support that leader for another term, campaigned on costs and term limits, and was endorsed by the state's two most recent Democratic governors along with most living former governors of both parties.[^2] His victory speech was not a policy speech. "This election is a rebuke of unkindness and cruelty in Washington, D.C.," he said.[^3] He told the crowd, "Tonight we made history. The real work starts Jan. 3."[^3]

Marshall's concession was shorter. "I don't have any answers," he told supporters in Wichita. "It's been the great honor of my life to serve the state of Kansas for 10 years."[^2] He called the result "very humbling."[^2]

The same ballot in the same metropolitan area carried two more results that belong in this piece because they are the same story told in a different key. Missouri voters rejected a constitutional amendment that would have reimposed an abortion ban, two years after they became the first state to undo a ban at the ballot box, and they rejected a congressional map drawn at the president's urging, by about 62 percent to 38 percent.[^4] A metro area that spans two states and two electorates voted the same way on the same night, and neither vote was about Washington.

## The Math That Materialized

The forecasters were right about the direction and wrong about the size, in the same direction.

Cook Political Report's closing analysis, published October 30, called the Democrats "slight favorites" and put their expected gain at two to six seats.[^5] The result was seven, a seat beyond the top of that range. Inside Elections had moved nine races toward the Democrats in a single update on October 29, which looked at the time like a rater hedging late; it was a rater catching up.[^5] The markets were worse. Kalshi had Marshall at 82.8 percent in Kansas on September 5 and Hamilton at 51 percent by October 30.[^5] It had Darline Graham at 75 percent in South Carolina on October 28, four days before she lost.[^5] It had Susan Collins at 36 percent in Maine on October 29, which was the one market call that pointed the right way.

This series made its own error and it is worth naming. On November 1 it argued that the signal was where the raters disagreed, and that the disagreement was Kansas, Iowa, Nebraska and South Carolina. Three of those four were right, and the fourth, South Carolina, was not a disagreement between raters so much as a state none of them could see. The piece also said the majority would likely be decided by Kansas or Nebraska. Kansas was not the margin. It was called at 10:24 p.m. Central, by which time the majority was already a question of how large, not whether.

One number deserves skepticism rather than celebration. Seven flips is the top of every published range, in a year when the final polls were inside the margin of error in two of those states and in single digits in every one. That is not evidence the polls were rigged or the models broken. It is evidence that a national swing of a few points moves a dozen states at once, and that ratings built one race at a time understate what happens when the same few points land everywhere. A wave is not a forecast anyone can make state by state. It is what happens when the same few points land in every place at once.

## The Close

The president's own account of the night is the clearest summary of what his party now faces. He told the New York Times shortly before 10:30 that he was "absolutely willing to accept those results" and would work with the Democrats.[^1] Within the hour he had posted that he "wasn't on the Ballot and if I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE," and he spent the rest of the night blaming Republican candidates for their losses while allowing that Democrats "did a good job."[^1] A man can accept a result and still explain it as somebody else's fault. Both statements are on the record, and the reader can hold them side by side and weigh both without an argument between them.

What the voters said, in the AP's own polling, is less complicated than either statement. High costs weighed heavily, most voters blamed the administration for them, and most wanted Congress to impose more checks on the president.[^1] That is not a mandate for a program. It is an instruction to a new majority to do something about the price of things, from a country that has stopped believing the argument about whose fault it is.

The new majority has two years and a narrow margin. It can hold hearings, block appointments it dislikes, and force votes that the Senate has avoided for a decade. It cannot pass a budget without the president's signature and cannot override a veto without sixty-seven votes in a Senate where it holds fifty-four. The likeliest outcome is not legislation. It is a two-year argument conducted with subpoenas, in which the administration defends and the Congress investigates, and the country watches to see whether either side can make groceries cheaper.

For the reader who voted because of the price of groceries, there is one honest thing to say. The election changed who can ask why they cost what they cost, and who must answer. It did not change the price. That work is slower, it is less satisfying than a wave, and it starts on January 3 with a gavel and a calendar.

Kansas waited ninety-four years for a senator who was not a Republican, and then elected a pastor who told the crowd that the real work starts after the history. That is the right order. The history is the easy part. What the voters did on Tuesday was open a door. What happens Monday is whether anyone walks through it.
