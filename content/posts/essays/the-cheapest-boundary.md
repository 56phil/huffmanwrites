The question arrives as an accusation, so the first thing to do is check the numbers.

In May 2025, Gallup found that 68 percent of Americans said marriages between same-sex couples should be recognized by law as valid, with the same rights as traditional marriages. That figure has held between 68 and 71 percent since 2021. Support among Republicans, however, has fallen from a peak of 55 percent in 2021 and 2022 to 41 percent, the lowest reading since 2016, and the resulting 47-point gap between the parties is the largest Gallup has measured in 29 years of asking. On the companion question, 64 percent of Americans called gay or lesbian relations morally acceptable, down from a high of 71 percent in 2022, and the Republican share had fallen to 38 percent, its lowest since 2012.[^1]

So the first thing to say is that the country did not have a change of heart. The country has been remarkably steady. What moved is a minority inside one party, and the movement is real, measurable, and recent.

The second number to hold beside it is the legislative output. The American Civil Liberties Union counted at least 510 bills targeting LGBTQ people in state legislatures in 2023, nearly three times the number introduced in 2022, with education and health care the heaviest categories.[^2]

That pair is the thing to explain. Not "America is hostile," which two-thirds of the public contradicts, and not "nothing is happening," which a record wave of bills contradicts. The thing to explain is a divergence: a coalition that has made one subject central out of proportion to any policy it might deliver, inside a country that has moved the other way.

## What a boundary is for

Start with cost, because cost is what decides which symbols a coalition uses.

A position that is nearly free to hold and expensive to disown is the most efficient loyalty test available. It marks who is inside the group without requiring agreement on anything that actually costs money. Trade, taxes, entitlements, the size of the deficit: those split a coalition every time. A moral boundary splits nothing, because nobody in the group is competing for the thing it protects.

The test of that reading is not what the position says. It is what the position does over time.

In November 2004, eleven states put constitutional amendments banning same-sex marriage on the ballot and all eleven passed, several by margins of three to one.[^3] The New York Times reported the following day that the measures "increased the turnout of socially conservative voters in many of the 11 states where the measures appeared on the ballot," providing "crucial assistance to Republican candidates including President Bush in Ohio and Senator Jim Bunning in Kentucky."[^4] That is a boundary doing load-bearing political work, and doing it cheaply.

Twenty-one years later, Gallup has marriage support at 68 percent, and the ballot energy has moved almost entirely. The 2023 wave was dominated by school curricula, sports participation, and health care for minors. The specific objection changed; the position did not.

If the objection were to a particular act, the object would stay fixed, because acts do not migrate on a political calendar. Boundaries migrate, because the boundary is the point and the territory on either side is interchangeable. This is the strongest single piece of evidence available, and it requires no psychology at all.

## The gender part, stated honestly

There is a psychological account here, and it is better than the version usually offered, but it is not as strong as its citation count suggests.

The precarious manhood thesis holds that manhood, unlike womanhood, is understood as an achieved status that "must be earned and maintained through publicly verifiable actions," and that men therefore experience more anxiety about their gender status, especially when it is challenged. The 2013 review by Joseph Vandello and Jennifer Bosson lists the ways men prove manhood in public, and among them it names "public displays of homophobia."[^5] That is the theory's own claim, not an inference from it, and it fits the shape of the rhetoric: the anxiety is about hierarchy, and sexuality is the instrument, not the subject.

Now the correction, which most versions of this argument omit.

A 2026 credibility review of the experimental precarious manhood literature, covering 188 independent samples and 452 hypothesis tests, found "substantial selection for significance at the conventional 5% threshold and moderate expected replicability." The same review reports a 2026 meta-analysis that put the average effect of a masculinity threat at d = 0.45, small to medium, and reports a preregistered replication of the field's most-cited masculine-overcompensation experiments, using a large nationally representative sample, which found "no consistent evidence that masculinity threat altered political attitudes," homophobia among them.[^6]

Take that seriously, because it cuts against the tidier story. The description survives: the rhetoric of this politics is anxious about manhood, and it treats femininity as contamination. The causal claim that follows it around does not survive intact. Threatening a man's masculinity does not reliably make him homophobic in the published record, once the record is corrected for the file drawer.

What remains is enough for the argument here, and it is narrower. The gender account explains why the language sounds the way it does. It does not explain the bills, and it was never going to.

## The study everybody reaches for

The finding that gets quoted most in this conversation is older and weaker than its fame.

In 1996, Henry Adams, Lester Wright, and Bethany Lohr recruited 35 men who scored high on a homophobia index and 29 who scored low, showed them erotic films, and measured penile circumference. Both groups responded to the heterosexual and lesbian films. "Only the homophobic men showed an increase in penile erection to male homosexual stimuli."[^7] The sample was 64 college men, the finding has never been replicated at scale, and the literature built on top of it is mixed.

It is also irresistible, for a reason worth naming. It converts a political opponent into a patient. If the man across the table is not making an argument but displaying a symptom, then no argument is owed to him, and the person holding the diagnosis never has to answer the case he made.

That is a bad trade even when the diagnosis is correct. It is a worse one when the diagnosis rests on 64 undergraduates.

The better-supported version of the same insight is unglamorous: threatened status produces defensive reactions, and status threat, not economic hardship, best explains the 2016 shift. Diana Mutz's panel study tracking the same respondents from 2012 to 2016 found that changes in personal financial wellbeing "had little impact on candidate preference," while changes in preferences tracked anxiety about dominant group status.[^8] That is a claim about groups defending a hierarchy. It is not a claim about anybody's unconscious.

## The inheritance

None of this rhetoric is new, and the continuity is the most useful thing about it.

In 1977, Anita Bryant formed Save Our Children to repeal a Dade County ordinance that barred discrimination against gay and lesbian residents in housing and employment. The campaign's central charge was that "known practicing homosexuals" were converting children to homosexuality. The ordinance was repealed that June by a margin of 69 to 31.[^9]

The charge has since been compressed into a single word, and it does the same work it did in 1977: it moves the argument from what adults may do to what children must be protected from, where no counterargument is permitted. A frame that has survived fifty years is not surviving on its merits. It is surviving because it converts a policy disagreement into an emergency, and emergencies do not require evidence.

## What the numbers do not license

Two things should not be flattened, and both cut against the more satisfying conclusion.

The first is sincerity. Thirty-eight percent of Republicans calling gay or lesbian relations morally acceptable leaves 62 percent who do not, which is tens of millions of people holding an actual conviction rather than performing one. Among Americans who attend religious services weekly, Gallup finds one-third supporting same-sex marriage and 24 percent calling same-sex relations morally acceptable.[^1] That is not a boundary. That is a belief, held in public, by people who are not pretending.

The second is that "MAGA" is a media artifact rather than a sampled population. There is no survey instrument that returns a group with the psychology the phrase implies. What exists is a messaging layer optimized for attention, a donor and activist infrastructure with material incentives, and a mass layer that is mostly sorted rather than obsessed: people who have been told which team holds which position and have lined up accordingly, without much interest in the subject either way.

The obsession is real at the top and performative in the middle. Most of what looks like conviction in the middle is alignment, and alignment is cheap because it has to be.

## What follows from the boundary reading

If the position is a boundary rather than a doctrine, then two conclusions fall out, and they are not comforting.

The first is that argument does not dissolve it. A boundary cannot be refuted, because it does not assert anything that a fact could settle. Every refutation lands on the territory, and the boundary simply moves to new territory, which is exactly what happened between 2004 and 2023. This is why a decade of documented debunking has produced no measurable movement among the people for whom the boundary was built.

The second is that what does move a boundary is cost. A position becomes expensive when it starts losing elections, when it costs a business money, when the abstraction acquires a face at a family table. Those are the levers that have worked, and none of them is a debate.

The rotation is therefore both the bad news and the good. The bad news is that the coalition will always have a boundary somewhere, because a boundary is what it runs on. The good news is that a boundary can be made expensive without first winning an argument about which territory it should enclose.

Sixty-eight percent is not a verdict. It is a majority that a boundary exists to keep from behaving like one, and the whole of the political question is whether it will.

Think clearly. Live intentionally. Love deeply.
