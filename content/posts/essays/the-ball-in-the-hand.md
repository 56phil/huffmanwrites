A ping pong ball resting in the hand is a small sphere and nothing else. It has no direction, no score, no history. The table stands there. The net divides an emptiness that has not yet become a game. Everything that will happen happens after the ball leaves the palm.

A vote has the same shape. An opinion you hold is not a vote. An intention you announce is not a vote. A preference that never reaches a ballot box, a mail tray, or a canvassing board is a ball in a closed hand: real, round, and worth nothing to the score.

The comparison is not a joke about small margins, though small margins are part of it. It is a claim about where a political act begins. Table tennis is unusually good at stating that claim, because its rules spend more words on the serve than on almost anything else in the sport.

## The most regulated act in the game

The International Table Tennis Federation devotes seven numbered clauses to getting the ball into play. The first is almost domestic in its care. "Service shall start with the ball resting freely on the open palm of the server's stationary free hand," Law 2.6.1 says. The second adds the geometry: the server must project the ball "near vertically upwards, without imparting spin, so that it rises at least 16cm after leaving the palm of the free hand and then falls without touching anything before being struck." The fourth fixes the position, requiring that until the strike the ball be "above the level of the playing surface and behind the server's end line," and that it not be hidden from the receiver by the server's body, clothing, or partner. The fifth requires the free arm and hand to leave the space between the ball and the net as soon as the ball is projected. ([ITTF Statutes 2026, Laws of Table Tennis 2.6](https://documents.ittf.sport/sites/default/files/public/2026-02/2026_Statutes_v1_consolidated_tc.pdf))

None of these rules is about the ball. The ball is the same ball either way. The rules are about whether anyone else can see what happened, and they place the cost of visibility on the person who serves. Law 2.6.6 states it without decoration: "It is the responsibility of the player to serve so that the umpire or the assistant umpire can be satisfied that he or she complies with the requirements of the Laws."

That sentence deserves a place on the refrigerator of every citizen. The burden of making the act legible does not belong to the umpire. It does not belong to the opponent. It belongs to the person serving. A legal serve that no one could see is still a fault, and the server does not get credit for sincerity.

## The civic serve

A vote passes through the same sequence, and each stage has a rule that decides whether the act counts. Registration is the open palm: the voter's name resting where election officials and neighbors can find it, in a precinct and at an address. Casting is the toss: a marked ballot delivered at a documented time, in person or by mail, into a system that will record its arrival. The eligibility rules are the end line: the right precinct, the right deadline, the right signature, the right envelope. The canvass is the net: the moment the ballot crosses from private intention into public count. Certification is the score.

The numbers show how much of this machinery is doing real work. In the 2022 general election, the Election Assistance Commission counted more than 112 million ballots, which was 46.8 percent of the citizen voting age population. ([EAC, *2022 Election Administration and Voting Survey*](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2024-11/2022_EAVS_Report_508c.pdf)) Those ballots were not the electorate's opinions. They were the opinions that got served.

The serve fails often enough to matter. Of the mail ballots returned in 2022, 1.5 percent were rejected, roughly 550,000 ballots, and the rate varied enormously by state: Delaware rejected 13.2 percent of the mail ballots it received, while Idaho rejected essentially none. ([Ballotpedia, on the EAC data](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2023/08/14/1-5-of-all-absentee-mail-in-ballots-were-rejected-in-2022-roughly-equal-to-2018/)) A rejected ballot is not a voter without an opinion. It is a player who served and lost the point on a technicality that the rules anticipated.

Which is why the cure exists. Forty-one states allowed voters to correct a missing signature or a defective envelope in 2022, and about 170,000 mail ballots were successfully cured. ([EAC](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/2024-11/2022_EAVS_Report_508c.pdf)) The cure is a second serve, granted because the first one failed in a way that said nothing about whether the player meant to play.

## Two ways to lose a point

Once you see the vote as a serve, the ways it goes wrong stop looking alike.

The first is the ball that never leaves the hand. More than half of the citizen voting age population did not vote in 2022, by the EAC's own count, and the largest bloc in American politics is not a party. It is the people who held the ball. The usual explanations are familiar and partly true: no compelling candidate, no close race, no time, no faith that it matters. But the structure of the failure is the same in every case. An opinion existed and never entered the count, so the scoreboard never registered it. A ping pong player who decides the match is rigged and refuses to serve has not protested the score. He has forfeited the game and left the table looking the same.

The second is the serve that lands nowhere. A ballot cast in the wrong precinct, mailed without enough time, signed in a way a scanner cannot read, or returned in an envelope the voter sealed wrong is an intention that reached the table and failed the rules. This is where the civic version of Law 2.6.6 bites. Some of that failure belongs to the voter, who must serve so that the count can see the serve. Some of it belongs to the state, which writes the geometry and then decides how forgiving to be. A jurisdiction that rejects 13 percent of its mail ballots is not running a strict game. It is running a game whose serve rule is badly designed, and then blaming the players for the faults.

## The score moves by one

The margins justify the fuss. Table tennis awards a game to the first player to reach eleven points, "unless both players or pairs score 10 points, when the game shall be won by the first player or pair subsequently gaining a lead of 2 points." ([ITTF, Law 2.11.1](https://documents.ittf.sport/sites/default/files/public/2026-02/2026_Statutes_v1_consolidated_tc.pdf)) At deuce, no single point decides the game, and every single point is required. That is a better description of a close election than most political science produces.

Virginia supplied the illustration in 2017. In the 94th House of Delegates district, the recount first gave the Democrat Shelly Simonds a one-vote win. A three-judge panel then counted a disputed ballot for the Republican incumbent, David Yancey, and the race tied at 11,607 votes each. State law resolved the tie "by lot," so the board of elections placed each candidate's name in a film canister, put both canisters in a blue stoneware bowl, and drew one. Yancey won the seat, and with it Republican control of the chamber. ([NPR, January 4, 2018](https://www.npr.org/2018/01/04/573504079/virginia-republican-david-yancey-wins-tie-breaking-drawing))

Two thousand miles and sixteen years earlier, Florida certified a presidential margin of 537 votes, and a national election turned on a count that stopped before it finished. ([Supreme Court of Florida, *Gore v. Harris*](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/2000-12-08-FL-SC-opinion.pdf)) In both cases the scoreboard moved because ballots had been served and counted, and in both cases the argument afterward was about the serve rule: which marks count, which deadlines hold, which ballots enter the tally. Nobody argued about whether the voters had opinions. The opinions were never in question. The serves were.

## The rule, not the sermon

Here is what the serve rule teaches that a civics sermon cannot. Law 2.6 does not ask the player to want to serve legally. It specifies the palm, the height, the line, the visibility, and then assigns responsibility for demonstrating compliance. The rule works because it is written as geometry rather than as virtue.

The civic equivalents are equally concrete, and they are the reforms that actually change outcomes: registration deadlines published in plain language, mail ballots that can be tracked, curing processes with a phone call attached, postmarks that count, paper records that can be recounted, audits that happen before certification rather than after a complaint. Each is a serve rule. Each decides whether an intention becomes a point. None of them requires the voter to be better than he is. They require the table to be arranged so that an ordinary person, serving as well as he can, gets the ball into play.

The reverse is also true. A state can write a serve rule so exacting that only the practiced can comply, and then report low turnout as though it had discovered something about apathy. That is not a measurement of the electorate. It is a description of the table.

## Put it down and serve

There is a reason the ball is not the interesting object. It is identical for every player, cheap, and inert. What decides the game is the act of putting it into play, under rules that everyone can see, in a sequence that the other side can follow.

A vote is the same. The opinion is cheap and inert until someone serves it. The ballot in the drawer, the conviction held at dinner, the certainty that the country is going in the wrong direction: none of it registers. The scoreboard has no column for what you meant.

So the instruction is not to think harder, though thinking clearly is where it starts. It is to serve: register, mark, return, and then watch the count, because the count is where the point is awarded. A ball in the hand is decoration. A ball in play is the game.
