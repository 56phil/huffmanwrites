**Question:** What happens when a federal court finds, as a fact, that the plan the government told it did not exist does exist, and that the reason offered for the work was not the real one?

**Answer:** The government files a notice confirming the plan, with a date for the closure and the name of the designer. On Monday, October 26, Judge Ana C. Reyes granted a preliminary injunction in the East Potomac golf case, and the opinion released with it makes two findings. The first is that "the Washington National Plan exists," defined as "a plan to destroy the current East Potomac Golf Course and to replace it with a modern, championship-style course designed by Tom Fazio." The second is that the government's stated justification for the tree removal, compliance with the 2019 Cultural Landscape Report, is "pretextual," and that the tree work "is part of the Washington National Plan." On Wednesday the government filed a notice under the injunction disclosing that it will submit a Tom Fazio rendering to the National Capital Planning Commission on Friday, that the Commission will take it up on December 3, and that the Park Service will close East Potomac Park south of Buckeye Drive, including the course, on November 15, "consistent with the President's directive." The same notice reports a new lease, signed October 28, under which the course's operator stops running it on or before that date. On Thursday the government moved to stay the injunction pending appeal and asked the court to rule by November 4.

Three dockets moved this week, and in two of them the government argued that a decision had not been made. In the District it had. At the D.C. Circuit, in the Epstein files case, a panel denied the government's motion to stay the district court's orders and sent the jurisdictional fight to the merits, which means review proceeds rather than waits. In the Kennedy Center case the week produced the closest look yet at what the government calls privileged: a log listing roughly 180 text messages between the Center's executive director and the Commerce Secretary, and a brief arguing that the board was told a phased renovation would cost twice as much while a consultant's analysis putting the premium at $41 million sat in management's hands.

## The golf course: *D.C. Preservation League v. Department of the Interior*

Where the case stands: the D.C. Preservation League and two regular players at East Potomac sued on February 13, 2026, challenging the redevelopment under the National Environmental Policy Act through the Administrative Procedure Act. The government moved to dismiss in April on the theory that no final agency action existed, and in March and again in April the court declined to intervene because the agency was still deciding what it wanted to do. On October 9 the Park Service gave notice that it would begin removing trees the next morning, and on October 10 it felled hundreds. The plaintiffs sought a temporary restraining order, which Judge Reyes granted on October 13. Their motion for a preliminary injunction was heard on October 19 and taken under advisement. It was granted on Monday.

The opinion opens with a sentence that deserves a pause, because it is a trial judge describing what a court does when the executive branch asks it to disbelieve its own principal: "Rarely, if ever, has the United States Department of Justice urged a federal court not to believe the sitting President." It then sets the record against the assurances. The Deputy Chief of Staff to the Secretary of the Interior, asked whether the course would close on November 15, answered that "the Truth Social post is the only place where I see that." The Acting Director of the National Park Service, asked whether any plans existed, said "None that I've been made aware of."

The findings that follow are the substance. The Plan exists, and the court's definition of it is specific: a plan, designed by Tom Fazio, to replace the course with a championship layout. The evidence is a list, and each item is a fact of record rather than an inference: the President's statements on five occasions, the Secretary's statements in May and his silence after them, final designs from the architect the Secretary and the President had named, 30,000 cubic yards of soil dumped on the course in preparation, the termination of the operator's lease, a June tour of the site, and the tree removal itself. On the tree work the court is blunter. The justification offered was compliance with a 2019 landscape report, and the court found that justification "pretextual," the work "part of the Washington National Plan," and the plaintiffs likely to succeed on their claim that dividing the project into pieces violated NEPA.

The injunction is broad, and the government's objection to its breadth is the heart of its appeal. It bars cutting, topping or substantially pruning any tree in East Potomac Park without a court order, except to meet an imminent threat to public safety, which requires notice within 24 hours and a sworn declaration from a senior Park Service employee. It requires ten business days' written notice before any closure of the course or irreversible modification of the park, with seven particulars: the action's nature and scope, its start date and why then, everyone who authorized it, everyone who will carry it out, the agency's reason, its impact, and whether it relates to the Washington National Plan or a Fazio design. It orders the department to notify its employees and the Fazio firms. It requires a dollar bond.

Wednesday's notice was the first thing the injunction required, and it answers the question at the center of the case. The government will submit a Fazio rendering to the National Capital Planning Commission on Friday for a "World Class, championship-level golf course" capable of hosting "the U.S. Open, the PGA Championship, the Ryder Cup, and the President's Cup," for the Commission's December 3 meeting. It will close the park south of Buckeye Drive, including the course, on November 15. National Links Trust, which has operated the course, signed a new lease on October 28 under which it stops operating East Potomac by that date, and the notice says the closure follows from that: "there will be no company to operate that course." A crumbling sea wall is offered as an independent reason, one that would require closure whatever the design.

Thursday's filings put the President's own account beside the notice. The plaintiffs filed a notice quoting his October 19 post about work beginning "IMMEDIATELY!", then superseded it within hours with an amended one reporting a post from the night before in which he called the course "highly dangerous, dilapidated, and absolutely disgusting," described the sea wall and the driving range in detail, and wrote: "We will hold the judge, Ana C. Reyes, fully responsible for anybody who is hurt, or killed, on this very dangerous, dilapidated, and unsightly Golf Course." The safety rationale in Wednesday's notice and the conditions described in Wednesday night's post are the same conditions, described hours apart.

Read against the opinion, the notice is a concession with a caveat. The plan the government told the court did not exist is now a submission to the Commission with a date. The closure the government told the court was not planned is now a lease term and a calendar entry. What the notice preserves is the argument that the closure would happen anyway, for safety. What it cannot preserve is the premise of the stay motion filed the next day, which argues that "redesigning an existing public golf course into a different public golf course is not agency action at all," and that any proposal "is not yet final because the Park Service has not settled on or approved any such plan." The Park Service settled on a rendering, a submission date and a designer, and said so in a filing under the injunction.

The stay motion is otherwise a conventional four-factor argument, and its strongest point is the one the opinion leaves open. It says the injunction reaches all of East Potomac Park rather than the ground the course occupies, bars even maintenance pruning, and substitutes the court for the agency as manager, which it says *Monsanto* forbids. It also argues standing, leaning on the Supreme Court's recent decision in the National Trust case that aesthetic and historic interests are not injuries. The court answered both below. On standing it found that the individual plaintiffs have it, and that the League lacks organizational standing but has associational standing through a member. On finality it held that the plaintiffs are likely to succeed, because their "characterization" that the plan is final agency action "finds ample support in the record." Against that, the motion says the government is only "acting as proprietor, changing the design of the product it is offering the public," which it argues falls outside the APA. Whether the D.C. Circuit sees the boundary the same way is now the question.

Friday's hearing was on the government's response to the court's order to show cause, and it produced a schedule that runs past the closure date. The government must identify everyone who worked on the NCPC submission, brief the deliberative-process question and disclose any Fazio contract by November 2; the parties must propose a preservation order the same day and a revised notice requirement by Tuesday; and the plaintiffs must file any motion about the NCPC submission by November 5. They must also meet in person by 5:00 p.m. Saturday to discuss how far the notice requirement should reach.

The last filing of the week is the one that shows the purpose of the schedule. ECF 116 says the plaintiffs cannot respond to the NCPC submission by November 5 without the submission, that defense counsel declined to provide it, that counsel said the material is "usually provided in the normal course (up to 10–14 days)" and might be public on November 3, and that counsel had no authority either to produce it early or to join a call with the court. So the plaintiffs asked the court to compel production, or an explanation, by 10:00 a.m. Saturday. The document the government told the court about on Wednesday is the document it would not give the plaintiffs on Friday.

## The Kennedy Center: *Beatty v. Trump*

Where the case stands: Judge Cooper's opinion of May 29 declared the board's December 2025 renaming void and preliminarily enjoined the March 16 closure decision; his order of September 15 enjoined the August 13 inscription and the "President Donald J. Trump Plaza" renaming. The Board voted to close the main building for two years on August 13 and again on September 15, and moved to dissolve the injunction under Rule 60(b)(5) (ECF 88) and for partial summary judgment (ECF 89). Discovery closed October 23. The building is still closed, and there is still no reopening date.

The privilege log is the week's most revealing document, and it is five pages. Defendants served it on the last day of the extended discovery period, and it lists nine categories of withheld emails and text threads, all dated between April 23 and September 15, 2026. Two entries carry most of the weight. The eighth is nineteen text messages between Matthew Floca, the Center's executive director, and Natalie Harp, eleven of them on September 15, the day of the third closure vote and the day before the building shut. The ninth is a thread of roughly 180 messages between Floca and Howard Lutnick, the Commerce Secretary, running from May 30 to September 15, described by the defendants as about "operations, renovation, and other planning related to Kennedy Center Board of Trustees and management matters." Lutnick is not an officer, employee or trustee of the Kennedy Center, and the notice says so, adding that voluntary disclosure to a third party waives the privilege.

The rest of the log traces a relationship. A May 29 email, sent the day the injunction issued, attaching a memorandum on "operations, statutory duties, and litigation risks" and the Center's "financial outlook." A July 30 meeting with the President, followed the same day by a summary document sent to two Commerce Department advisers and an employee of the Executive Office of the President. A seventeen-email thread about Honors planning. The notice's argument is that the descriptions concede the point the government has been resisting: communications about the "operations" and "management" of the Center, logged as presidential, are the Chairman acting as Chairman, and they bear on whether the September vote was independent or a ratification.

The opposition filed Friday is fifty-one pages and makes four factual claims that would each be enough for a hearing. The first is that Floca hired Delta Consulting in July, told it it would have 60 days, then demanded a recommendation within days, and did not give the trustees Delta's written report until August 31, eighteen days after the vote it was written to support. The second is an error with a date attached: Delta told the board that closure had to finish within two years because appropriated funds had to be spent by September 2029, and "those funds can be spent through September 2034." The board, the brief says, never sought legal analysis of that question.

The third is the number this page treated last week as the government's withdrawn error, and it has changed sides. Delta put a phased renovation at $560 million, and the board was told that phasing "will cost twice as much." JLL's updated plan, delivered to management on August 20 and withheld from the board, put the additional cost of phasing at $41 million and found phased renovation "technically feasible." Last week the government withdrew a sentence quoting the $41 million figure because it came from a report the Board never received. This week the plaintiffs use the same report to argue that the Board was told the opposite of what its own consultant had concluded. The fourth claim is about motive, and its evidence is a text message: "we need to get shut" because "we are burning money."

The relief the brief asks for is graded. Deny the motion to dissolve. Hear Floca live, for up to 90 minutes, because the documents that contradict his deposition arrived after the deposition ended. And if the court ever concludes that the Center's finances are as dire as management told the board, appoint an independent monitor or receiver rather than let insolvency excuse the conduct that produced it. The brief's footnote puts the choice more sharply: closure is "a decision to save the patient by killing him."

The live-testimony request is four pages and quotes the government's opposition in full. Defendants say no hearing is needed at all, that any testimony would be "duplicative of extensive record evidence," and that the burden on Floca, "who is responsible for leading the Center," would outweigh its value. The request answers with the record: Floca testified at the preliminary injunction hearing in the East Potomac case, Beatty's counsel cross-examined him there, and the court later relied on that testimony in assessing whether a phased renovation was feasible.

One filing is sealed: ECF 113, a motion for leave accompanied by a document the docket identifies as the August 2026 JLL plan. So the report that the brief says was withheld from the board is now being filed under seal in the case about whether it was withheld, and the public can read the argument about it but not the report.

The week's one order is a scheduling one, and it is easy to misread. The status conference on November 3 is on the motion to compel, ECF 96. It is not a hearing on the motion to dissolve, which has no hearing set and will be decided on the papers unless the court grants the live-testimony request.

## The Epstein files on appeal

Where the appeal stands: the government moved on September 22 to stay the June 25 and September 16 orders and to expedite. Phang opposed the stay and cross-moved to dismiss the appeal of the September 16 order as not appealable. Briefing was deferred pending further order, and the stay motion had been fully briefed since September 29.

On Wednesday the panel decided it. The order is two pages and it does two things. It denies the stay: "The government has not satisfied the stringent requirements for a stay pending appeal." The reason is narrow and worth quoting, because it turns on timing rather than merits. The government "has failed to show that it is irreparably harmed by merely initiating review of foreign-language documents," and "production does not appear to be imminent." Where the government does not dispute its obligation, it would "only produce documents that" it "concludes must be disclosed by law," and any dispute about redactions "will need to be resolved through further litigation," which is not present harm. Then it refers Phang's motion to dismiss to the merits panel, with an instruction to brief those issues rather than incorporate them by reference.

Read plainly, the ruling says the government may keep litigating, and that nothing about the litigation relieves it of the review it was ordered to start. It is not an administrative stay and it does not pause the district court. It also answers a question this page has left open since September: the stay motion was not a placeholder for a ruling that would never come. It was decided, twenty-nine days after the last brief, and it was denied.

What comes next is merits briefing, and the circuit has set no date for it. The motion to dismiss now travels with the appeal rather than ahead of it, so the question whether the September 16 order is appealable at all will be answered by the same panel that would hear the merits.

## The Epstein files: *Phang v. Blanche*

Where the case stands: Judge Sullivan granted a preliminary injunction on June 25, holding that the Epstein Files Transparency Act required disclosure. On September 16 he granted Phang's motion to compel an answer, denied the government's motion to stay his deadlines, and set the administrative record: a certified list by October 1, the record to Phang by October 15. The government answered on September 24 and appealed. On October 1 it filed ECF 51, asking the court to postpone the record until its Rule 12(c) motion was resolved, and ECF 52, the motion that would make the record unnecessary. On October 9 the court ordered redacted versions of the handwritten notes behind three FD-302 reports, and the government delivered them by hand on October 16.

The district docket is empty for the second week running. The October 15 deadline for the administrative record passed without an entry recording production. Three decisions have waited since September: the motion to postpone the record, the Rule 12(c) motion that would end the case, and Phang's request to move her response to it. Nothing in Wednesday's circuit order changes any of that, and the circuit did not remand or direct the district court to act. So the same asymmetry the appeal produced is now visible from the other side: the appellate court has ruled, and the trial court has said nothing about the record it ordered.

## The Epstein files: *MSW Media v. DOJ*

Where the case stands: MSW Media sued in August 2025 over two FOIA requests, one for grand jury material and one for the FBI's videos on how to review Epstein records. The grand jury request was dropped; the case now concerns the training videos, which the FBI withheld in full under four exemptions. Judge Howell denied expedited processing on July 1, 2026, which was a ruling about speed rather than about the videos. The government moved for summary judgment on October 2, asking the court to uphold the search and the withholding.

This week the plaintiff asked for four more days. The motion is two pages, it is consented to, and its reason is the ordinary one: counsel has three deadlines and a status conference on the Monday the brief is due. What it proposes is the useful part, because it would set the whole remaining schedule: the opposition and cross-motion on November 6, the government's reply and opposition on November 24, and the plaintiff's reply on December 10. The docket does not show a ruling, so those dates are a proposal and not an order.

That is the quietest docket of the five, and it is the one whose merits question is the most concrete: nine videos, made to instruct agents how to handle the files, withheld as internal guidance. Whether an exemption written for deliberative advice covers training material is the kind of question a court answers in a sentence, and it will have the chance in December.

The thing to hold on to when reading next week's version of this page: in three cases this week, the government's position was that a decision had not been made, or could not be reviewed, or should wait. One judge found the decision had been made and said so in seventy pages. Another court declined to pause the review it had ordered. The third now has a log of roughly 200 messages it has been asked to release. The pattern is not defiance of any one order. It is a preference for the position that nothing is ripe, maintained until a document arrives that says otherwise.
