# Mike Johnson and the Problem of Bad Faith: A Scholarly Assessment

**Question:** Is the man stupid, evil, both, or something else?

**Thesis:** He is not stupid. He is not evil in the sense of personal malevolence. He is something more dangerous and more explicable: a **true believer of above-average intelligence who has substituted a closed theological epistemology for empirical reasoning, and who has proven willing to abandon democratic norms and institutional integrity when they conflict with his eschatological politics.** In short, he is a **competent ideologue operating in bad faith** — not because he is incapable of recognizing truth, but because he has trained himself to reject it when it threatens his worldview.

---

## I. The "Stupid" Thesis: Why It Fails

James Michael Johnson is, by conventional metrics, an intelligent man. He earned a J.D. from the Paul M. Hebert Law Center at Louisiana State University.[^1] He spent two decades as a practicing constitutional litigator and argued cases in federal appellate courts. He founded and served as dean of a law school — the ill-fated Judge Paul Pressler School of Law at Louisiana College, which never opened its doors, a failure attributable to administrative chaos and donor fraud rather than to Johnson's legal acumen.[^2] He rose through the ranks of the Republican House caucus with sufficient tactical skill to become Speaker after Kevin McCarthy's ouster, surviving a motion to vacate from Marjorie Taylor Greene and navigating a fractured conference that had consumed three other would-be speakers.[^3]

But "stupid" must be defined before it can be dismissed. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing from a Nazi prison in 1943, distinguished stupidity from intellectual deficiency. "Against stupidity we have no defense," he wrote. "Neither protests nor the use of force accomplish anything here; reasons fall on deaf ears; facts that contradict one's prejudgment simply need not be believed — in such moments the stupid person even becomes critical — and when facts are irrefutable, they are just pushed aside as inconsequential, as incidental." For Bonhoeffer, stupidity is not low intelligence; it is the **surrender of independent judgment to an ideological system**, a protective sociological adaptation that allows the individual to act with "the unshakable assurance of the sleepwalker."

In this sense, Johnson exhibits a strain of Bonhoefferian stupidity. He has exempted his theological convictions from empirical testing. Facts that contradict his worldview — election results, climate data, constitutional history — are not engaged with; they are "pushed aside as inconsequential." Yet Bonhoeffer's stupid person is typically a follower, swept up in a movement he does not direct. Johnson is not following. He is architecting. He retains the instrumental rationality to manipulate the very system he claims to serve purely by faith. He files amicus briefs with surgical legal precision while denying the empirical basis of the cases he argues. He recesses the House with procedural cunning while claiming moral incapacity to negotiate. This is not the unshakable assurance of the sleepwalker. It is the **calculated compartmentalization** of a man who knows exactly what he is doing. Bonhoeffer's stupid person cannot be reasoned with because he has surrendered his reason; Johnson cannot be reasoned with because he has **weaponized** his reason. That is a different category of moral failure entirely.

This is not the résumé of a stupid man. It is the résumé of a man who is **selectively ignorant** — who applies rigorous legal reasoning to arguments that serve his priors and dismisses evidence that does not. His 2020 election denialism was not born of intellectual incapacity; it was born of **willful refusal to engage with evidence** that contradicted his desired outcome. When he filed amicus briefs supporting Texas's lawsuit to overturn the election — a lawsuit rejected by the Supreme Court for lack of standing — he was not misunderstanding constitutional law. He was **weaponizing** it.[^4]

The "stupid" frame is comforting because it lets us off the hook. If Johnson were merely a dullard, his ascent would be an accident, a failure of vetting. It is not. It is the logical endpoint of a party that has decided theological certainty is a sufficient qualification for governing a pluralistic democracy.

---

## II. The "Evil" Thesis: Why It Is Incomplete

If Johnson is not stupid, is he evil? The word is loaded. Let us use the precise formulation: does he act with **malice aforethought**, knowingly harming others for personal or ideological gain?

The evidence is mixed. Johnson's policy record is cruelty-adjacent by design: he supports a nationwide abortion ban, opposed same-sex marriage, defended Louisiana's "religious freedom" law that would have licensed discrimination against LGBTQ citizens, and has consistently opposed labor protections, environmental regulation, and social welfare expansion.[^5] But there is little evidence that he *revels* in suffering. He does not have the sadistic theatricality of a Marjorie Taylor Greene or the cynical nihilism of a Matt Gaetz.

What Johnson has is something subtler and, in some ways, more disturbing: **indifference to consequences that fall outside his moral universe.** He is not tormented by the women who will die from abortion bans because he genuinely believes embryos have souls and that God will sort out the rest. He is not lying awake over transgender teenagers suffering because his theological framework does not grant them full personhood. This is not personal malice. It is **structural cruelty** — the inevitable result of a worldview that privileges the eternal soul over the material body, and the "biblical family" over the actual family in front of him.[^6]

His recent conduct as Speaker, however, pushes closer to the "evil" threshold. In June 2025, he said California Governor Gavin Newsom "ought to be tarred and feathered" for his response to protests — a phrase invoking extralegal mob violence.[^7] In July 2025, he shut down the House and sent members into recess to avoid a vote on releasing Jeffrey Epstein's client files, a maneuver of pure political cowardice.[^8] During the 2025 government shutdown, he extended the House recess rather than negotiate, leaving federal workers unpaid while the chamber sat empty.[^9]

These are not the actions of a man who believes he is doing God's will. They are the actions of a man who believes his **political survival** is the highest good, and who will sacrifice transparency, governance, and the dignity of his office to preserve it.[^10] This is not theological conviction. It is **bad faith** — the deliberate subordination of truth and duty to power.

---

## III. The "Something Else" Thesis: Christian Nationalist Institutionalist

The most accurate descriptor is the third option: something else. Johnson is best understood as a **Christian nationalist who has mastered the language and forms of constitutional governance while rejecting its substantive premises.** His formative professional years were spent at the Alliance Defending Freedom (ADF), a conservative Christian legal organization that litigates to establish biblical principles as binding law. He sat on the Ethics and Religious Liberty Commission of the Southern Baptist Convention.[^11] He has openly stated that his worldview is derived from the Bible and that the Constitution was founded upon Christian principles — a historical claim rejected by most constitutional scholars but central to the Christian nationalist project.[^12]

This is not mere religiosity. It is a **totalizing epistemology.** In Johnson's framework, there is no distinction between biblical truth and legal truth, between spiritual warfare and political warfare. He described his own election as Speaker as the result of divine intervention — "God is not done with America" — and has consistently framed political opposition in moral and eschatological terms.[^13]

The danger of this worldview is not that it is irrational (though it is). The danger is that it is **non-falsifiable.** When empirical evidence contradicts theological conviction — on climate change, on election results, on the efficacy of abstinence-only education, on the outcomes of tax cuts — the evidence must be wrong, because the Bible cannot be. This creates a politician who is capable of sophisticated legal argumentation in service of propositions that are factually untrue. He is not stupid. He is **epistemologically closed**.[^14]

---

## IV. The Speaker as Institutional Saboteur

Johnson's speakership has been defined by a paradox: he is simultaneously **the least experienced Speaker in 140 years**[^15] and one of the most consequential for what he has *prevented* rather than achieved. The 118th Congress under his leadership was one of the least productive in decades.[^16] He has used the Speaker's gavel not to legislate but to **obstruct** — to block votes on Ukraine aid (until a tactical reversal in April 2024), to shelve immigration reform, to shut down the chamber to avoid uncomfortable votes on Epstein files and ethics.[^17]

This is not incompetence. It is **strategic nihilism.** Johnson understands that in a polarized environment, his base rewards obstruction more than governance. He has learned from McCarthy's fate that compromise is fatal, and that the only way to survive as Speaker of a Republican conference is to serve as a **human shield for Donald Trump** while the Freedom Caucus fires from behind him.[^18]

His institutional vandalism is particularly striking given his legal background. A man who spent twenty years arguing constitutional cases now treats the Constitution as a menu of options — citing its text when convenient (e.g., the Electoral Count Act challenges) and ignoring its spirit when necessary (e.g., recessing the House to avoid accountability). This is not ignorance of constitutional principle. It is **contempt for it** when it conflicts with partisan advantage.[^19]

---

## V. Synthesis: The Banality of Certainty

To return to the original question: Is Mike Johnson stupid, evil, both, or something else?

He is **not stupid.** His legal career, his political ascent, and his tactical maneuvering in the Speaker's chair demonstrate above-average intelligence applied with discipline.

He is **not evil** in the narrow sense of personal malevolence. He does not appear to take pleasure in suffering. He believes, sincerely, that he is doing God's work.

He is **something else** — and that something is, in the context of democratic governance, arguably worse than either stupidity or evil alone. He is a **true believer who has learned to lie, an institutionalist who has learned to destroy institutions, and a constitutional lawyer who has concluded that the Constitution is only binding when it produces the right outcomes.** He is Hannah Arendt's banality of certainty: a man whose sincere faith has become an engine of democratic corrosion, not because he intends harm, but because he has exempted himself from the obligation to care about harm that falls outside his theological frame.

The most accurate epitaph for his career to date is this: **Mike Johnson is a man smart enough to know better, sincere enough to believe his own lies, and powerful enough to make the rest of us suffer for both.** He is not the villain of a morality play. He is the protagonist of a tragedy — one in which the American experiment in pluralistic self-governance is the collateral damage of his personal redemption arc.
