On the morning after the midterms, EA WorldView's front page carried two clocks. One read "Ukraine War, Days 1,713-1,714" ([4 November 2026](https://eaworldview.com/2026/11/ukraine-war-russia-26-civilians-murdered-in-strike-on-aid-center-and-shelter/)). The other read "US War on Iran, Day 251" ([same morning](https://eaworldview.com/2026/11/us-war-on-iran-strikes-qeshm-island-in-strait-of-hormuz/)).

That is the whole method, sitting there in the headline. Donald Trump speaks in durations that do not exist, "two weeks" and "very soon" and "the regime will fall," and Scott Lucas answers with an integer that only goes up. Two wars, two counts, published every day by a volunteer newsroom and a professor who has been doing this long enough that the number is now larger than most people's memory of why it started.

## Who is doing the counting

Lucas holds a chair in International Politics at the Clinton Institute in University College Dublin, is Professor Emeritus at Birmingham, and edits EA WorldView, the site he founded in November 2008 ([author biography](https://eaworldview.com/author/eaworldview/)). The site is run entirely by volunteers; it made its name on Iran's 2009 protests, the Arab Spring, and the live coverage of Gaza, and major outlets cite it ([University Observer, 4 December 2025](https://universityobserver.ie/scott-lucas-on-journalism-curiosity-and-building-a-life-in-the-media/)).

The career before that was not a straight line, which matters to how he writes. He started at sixteen on a small paper in his Alabama hometown, spent a year at Vanderbilt, went to Leeds in 1981, and came back to Britain for postgraduate work because the alternative was law school. He drifted into journalism again through the New Statesman, whose editors published an essay arguing that American foreign policy could be understood through professional wrestling's narratives of heroes and villains.

The site's archive shows what became of that. His author page runs to **2,751 pages** of posts. Whatever else can be said about the volume, nobody produces it by accident.

## Bluster first, then the mechanism

The characteristic move is a stripping operation. "I cut through Trump's bluster to set out the political and military realities," he wrote in August, explaining a climbdown over strikes on Iran ([EA WorldView, 10 August 2026](https://eaworldview.com/2026/08/trump-tacos-over-us-strikes-on-iran/)). What follows is never a theory of Trump's psyche. It is a list of constraints: a shortage of air-defence interceptors, a Central Command running out of targets, roughly seventy per cent public opposition, and the price at the pump.

That last item recurs because it is the one that reaches voters. On the August pullback he put the causal chain in a single sentence: "If gasoline prices remain high ahead of the November elections, the Trump Administration will have little choice but to return to the negotiating table." The same entry has him telling a Polish interviewer that "Trump has found himself boxed in."

This is the opposite of the way a great deal of Trump commentary works. The dominant register treats the president as a psychological event, and each day's outrage as evidence of a character. Lucas treats him as a system with inputs: munitions inventories, oil markets, coalition partners who can say no. When Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman told Washington the Kingdom would not support an escalation it could not finish, that was, on Lucas's account, a reason for the retreat rather than a footnote to it.

The label he reached for in August was TACO, Trump Always Chickens Out. A shorthand like that can rot into a theory, the way "appeasement" and "failed state" did, until every observation fits and none can refute it. What keeps it usable in his hands is that he keeps attaching the mechanism. TACO is not offered as a claim about cowardice. It is offered as the visible result of a shortage of interceptors and a gallon of petrol.

## Why the number is the argument

The day counts look like housekeeping. They are the most consequential thing on the site.

A war with a running total is a war that cannot be memory-holed. Every presidential claim about duration has to be read against a figure that was published that morning and will be larger tomorrow. When officials brief that strikes may be "brief but intense" on the day after an election ([EA WorldView, 2 November 2026](https://eaworldview.com/2026/11/us-war-on-iran-trump-threatens-strikes-november-4/)), the reader has Day 251 in front of them, and the adjective has to survive the arithmetic. The war began on 28 February ([same entry](https://eaworldview.com/2026/11/us-war-on-iran-trump-threatens-strikes-november-4/)); nobody who reads the site daily can be talked into believing it started last month.

There is a second function, less obvious and more important. The number is a citation. "Ukraine War, Day 1,712" is a retrievable address for what was known on 2 November, which is what makes it possible to check a later claim against the earlier record. That is what a chronicle does when it is honest: it builds the file to which the argument will later have to answer.

The entries themselves are unsentimental. A Russian bombing of an aid centre and shelter in the Zaporizhzhia region killed at least thirty-three people on Monday, and it got a dated entry, the same way the hacked air-raid alert system got one the day before. The form does not permit a killing to be a mood. It is an entry, and the entry has a number.

## The cost of that volume

Five or six broadcast interviews a day is the figure the University Observer reports, across RTÉ and the BBC, Al Jazeera and France 24, NDTV and WION. Two thousand seven hundred and fifty-one pages of archive is the other figure.

An output that size has a structural price, and it is worth naming plainly. The unit of work becomes the confident sentence, because that is what the format rewards, and a man who gives six interviews before lunch cannot check six sets of claims the way a reporter checks one. The risk is not dishonesty. It is the drift from explaining to narrating, in which the analyst's fluency outruns the evidence and the viewer cannot tell the two apart.

Lucas manages most of that risk by staying close to inventories and prices, things that can be looked up. But the archive also contains the exceptions, and one is worth quoting because it is the same failure I described in a piece about Jeffrey Sachs this morning, in a smaller dose.

In July 2018, on CNN International, he said of the president's preference for Moscow over Brussels and London: "Donald Trump does not see the European Union as a partner. He does not see NATO as a partner. He sees Putin's Russia as a partner." Then the second sentence: "That may be because he has been compromised by the Russians" ([EA WorldView, 15 July 2018](https://eaworldview.com/2018/07/ea-on-cnn-realities-about-trump-us-allies-and-the-trump-russia-investigation/)).

Read it strictly and it is hedged, an inference explicitly marked as one. Read it as it aired, in the register of a man who usually deals in interceptors and prices, and the hedge is doing almost no work. It is the one moment in the record where a conclusion arrives ahead of its evidence, and the fact that it sounds like everything else he says is precisely the problem.

## What he gets right

The case for the method is not that it produces correct predictions. It is that it produces checkable ones, and that it refuses the two temptations of the moment.

The first temptation is to treat Trumpism as a personality that will pass. Lucas's framing, consistent for a decade, is that it is a political formation with a base, a media system, and an appetite for institutional damage, which is why his election-night verdict carried the title "A Victory for Decency, A Victory for Recovery," and why he spent the broadcast taking apart "Trump's attempt to retreat from his responsibility" ([EA WorldView, 4 November 2026](https://eaworldview.com/2026/11/trump-us-elections-a-victory-for-decency-a-victory-for-recovery/)). The moral language is not decoration. It is the claim that a system which lies about mines in a strait and files about its own citizens is a fact about the country's credit, not a matter of taste.

The second temptation is the opposite error, and the more common one among people who agree with him: to let contempt substitute for analysis, so that every development becomes confirmation. The counter to that is a date. On 4 November 2021 nobody could have known what Day 1,714 would look like. It looked like thirty-three people killed while collecting aid, and the entry says so, and the entry before it said something different.

## The file

There is a temptation, when a commentator is right about the thing you care about, to stop auditing him. The archive is the reason to keep going. It is also the reason he is worth reading, because it is a standing invitation: everything is dated, addressed, and still there to be contradicted.

A man who counts days is making a small promise every morning. The count will not skip, the entry will be published, and the number will not be adjusted to suit the argument. Lucas has kept that promise 1,714 times on one war and 251 on another, which is a longer record than most of the people currently explaining American politics to the world.

That is a lower and more durable achievement than being right.
