*This is satire. The wagers, the amounts, the payout figures, and every quotation below are real and sourced; the scene is imagined.*

The market had her at one percent.

That was the whole of it, at the start. On the prediction market Kalshi, Katie Zacharia, a conservative commentator who had spent a brief spell as a spokesperson at the Department of Homeland Security, was priced at roughly a one percent chance of becoming White House press secretary, which is the price of a thing nobody expects, and therefore the price of a thing nobody watches.

At 10:43 on a Thursday night, someone put down nineteen dollars.

There is no record of who, only that the wager was small, and that it sat there overnight, and that at some point in the dark it stopped being a lottery ticket and became a position. By Friday morning the price on Zacharia had not moved. It did not need to. The bettor had done the one thing the market could not price, which was nothing at all, and then waited for the news to arrive.

At 1:41 on Friday afternoon, two more wagers landed, about seventy-four dollars and about eighty dollars, placed within minutes of each other, less than an hour before reporters began announcing the appointment. The three tickets were expected to pay $1,896, $3,689, and $4,023. Kalshi opened an investigation into the trades. The company declined to say more. The market, being a market, had already said the important part: the one percent was not a prediction. It was a sign that the people who knew were not trading.

This is the strange new thing. A prediction market is supposed to convert belief into price, and price into information, and information into a kind of public weather. The wager on Zacharia did none of that. It took a ninety-dollar outlay and turned it into four thousand dollars, and it carried no information to anyone until after the appointment was already made, at which point the information was that someone had known, and that the knowing had a dollar amount, and that the dollar amount was small enough to hide and large enough to notice.

The White House, asked about the investigation, referred reporters to the president's Truth Social post announcing the hire, which praised Zacharia as a "devoted fan and member of MAGA." This is, in fairness, an answer. It is the answer you get when the question is procedural and the truth is atmospheric. Nobody in the press briefing that followed appeared to know whether the ninety dollars came from a friend, a donor, a staffer, a spouse, a phone, or a chair. They knew only that it came from somewhere inside the weather.

There is precedent, which is the part that makes it comedy. In July, the president's teleprompter operator, Gabriel Perez, was removed from his role after making more than one hundred thousand dollars on Kalshi bets about the president's speeches. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission fined him $172,000 in August and he settled. The White House had already warned staff not to use nonpublic information for personal gain, after suspicious wagers were linked to the Iran war. All of this was on the record before the ninety dollars was placed. A market that has already caught one insider, and been publicly reprimanded for it, and adjusted its prices, and then found itself pricing a press secretary at one percent and paying four thousand dollars to whoever read the signal correctly, is not a market that has failed. It is a market that has learned what its customers are for, and learned it well.

Here is the thing the market cannot do, and never could. It cannot distinguish a bet placed by a stranger who guessed from a bet placed by someone who was told. Both look identical on the tape. Both pay the same. Both are legal, until they are not, and the difference is not in the trade but in the person, and the person is the one thing the ledger does not hold. So the market did the only honest thing available to it: it paid out, and opened an investigation, and let the two of them sit next to each other in the same filing, unresolved, which is where they will stay.

The new press secretary will stand at the podium and take questions. Some of those questions will be about the trades. None of them will be answered, because the trade is not a fact about the administration. It is a fact about the weather the administration lives in, and the weather does not take questions.

One percent. Nineteen dollars. Two thousand dollars of return on a Thursday night, and four thousand more by Friday afternoon, and a job at the podium, and an investigation with no name in it. The market got everything right. That is the joke.
