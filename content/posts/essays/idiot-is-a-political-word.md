The brief for this piece arrived as an insult, the way most briefs for pieces like this one arrive: *angry idiots are ruining the nation.* I am not going to argue with the insult. I am going to argue that the word inside it is the more interesting half, and that it says something the anger does not.

Because **idiot is a political word.** It always has been. The Greeks did not mean by it what we mean. To an Athenian, the *idiōtēs* was not a man of low intelligence. He was a private person, a man who held no public office and took no part in public affairs, as opposed to the magistrate and the citizen who did ([Harper, 2026](#sources)). The word comes from *idios*: one's own, private, particular to oneself. The idiot was the man who stayed home. The idiot was the man who thought the city was somebody else's business.

The insult has migrated almost exactly into its opposite, and that migration is the whole story of this decade. The charge against the country now is not that too many people are staying home. It is that too many people have arrived in the public square carrying their private selves, and refuse to set them down. The new idiot is not the man who abstains. He is the man who participates, loudly and constantly, without ever leaving himself behind. He mistakes his own grievance for the public good and his own rage for a program, and he calls the resulting noise citizenship. The word never changed. The man did.

## The angry part, which is not the stupid part

It is worth separating the two words, because the phrase fuses them and the fusion is a mistake. Anger is not stupidity. Some of the most effective political anger in American history was the anger of people who had been lied to for a very long time and had finally read the documents. The anger of a parent who cannot afford an inhaler, of a farmer watching a tariff eat his margin, of a family that buried someone the state was not supposed to shoot: none of that is stupid, and none of it should be quieted. The country has real reasons for rage, and the people who feel it are frequently in possession of a better grasp of the facts than the people telling them to calm down.

So the problem is not that people are angry. The problem is what the anger has been attached to, and how easily it now detaches from any object that could ever be repaired.

There is a body of research on this, and it is unusually clear. A 2017 study in *Nature Human Behaviour* by the Yale psychologist Molly Crockett described what digital media does to moral outrage, and the findings have held up as the platforms grew. Outrage, she wrote, is "triggered by stimuli that call attention to moral norm violations," and expressing it can hold bad actors accountable and hold a community together ([Crockett, 2017](#sources)). But the architecture of the platforms changes the arithmetic. They "compete for our attention to generate advertising revenue," so their algorithms "promote content that is most likely to be shared, regardless of whether it benefits those who share it — or is even true" ([Crockett, 2017](#sources)). The result is a market that selects, generation after generation, for the most enraging version of every event. Crockett's central question has become the decade's own: "If moral outrage is a fire, is the internet like gasoline?" ([Crockett, 2017](#sources)).

The evidence that the fire spreads differently online is not a matter of opinion. A companion study in *PNAS*, summarized for a general audience a year later, found that political messages carrying moral and emotional language were roughly 20 percent more likely to be shared than comparable messages without it, and that these words captured attention even when a person was already paying attention to something else ([Gantman et al., 2019](#sources); [Brady et al., 2017](#sources)). The words did not have to be true, or fair, or useful. They had to be hot. A separate line of work that Crockett cites describes what follows: "by lowering the threshold for outrage expression, digital media may degrade the ability of outrage to distinguish the truly heinous from the merely disagreeable" ([Crockett, 2017](#sources)).

That sentence is the whole machine in one clause. When every provocation is met with the same volume, the volume stops carrying information. A senator who mistakes a basketball team for another basketball team, a school board that argues about a book, a reporter who gets a number wrong, and a government that shoots a citizen and then files a report about it all arrive at the same temperature. The instrument that once measured injustice now measures nothing, because it is pinned to its maximum. The angry man is not the problem. The man who is *equally* angry at everything is, because he has thrown away the one faculty the anger was supposed to serve: the ability to tell which thing matters most.

## The two idiots

It would be comfortable to place this on one side of the aisle. It would also be false, and the falseness would be the proof of the disease. The angry idiot has a left-hand version and a right-hand version, and they are the same man wearing different clothes.

The right-hand version runs on the scapegoat. The theory is that your pain has a face, and the face belongs to an immigrant, a trans person, a bureaucrat, a professor, a coastal elite. That this story has been enormously effective is not in dispute; it is the story Donald Trump told to win the White House twice. The problem with it is the same problem it has always had: the faces it names cannot, in fact, fix the thing that hurts. A man who loses a job because of automation is not made whole by a rule about who may use which bathroom. A family priced out of a house is not housed by a tariff on steel. The 2026 midterms are being framed in some quarters as a "midterms of rage," and the interesting turn is that the anger has swung around and pointed back at the people who harnessed it ([Williams, 2026](#sources)). As the Working Families Party's national director, Maurice Mitchell, told *The Guardian*, the scapegoating "playbook is falling flat after a decade." Once you have used the power to make those people's lives hell and the voter's life is no better, "then the jig is up" ([The Guardian, 2026](#sources)). Who, he asked, is left to be blamed?

The left-hand version runs on purity. It has no face for your pain, so it invents one in the mirror. Its engine is the moral-emotional post that proves the poster's virtue to a like-minded audience and never reaches a persuadable human being. The same *PNAS* study is quietly damning here: the moral-emotional messages spread furthest *within* ideological clusters and mostly fail to cross between them ([Brady et al., 2017](#sources)). The outrage ricochets inside the echo chamber and almost never escapes it. Which means the left's loudest product is not argument at all. It is reputational proof of belonging, sold to people who already belong. That is not persuasion. It is a loyalty oath with a share button, and its cost is the exact audience it needs: the unconvinced middle that no longer hears anything said in that register.

Both versions share the mechanism Crockett describes: outrage expression that has been decoupled from the feeling of outrage and from any consequence in the world ([Crockett, 2017](#sources)). The habit forms. The threshold drops. The person can now express rage he does not actually feel about a thing he cannot actually change, and receive the reward anyway, and be counted by the machine as having done something. *The Guardian*'s reporting from the Midwest this fall found a "pissed-off majority" whose anger is real and whose object is diffuse ([The Guardian, 2026](#sources)). One Iowa candidate, Rob Sand, has tried to give it one: he frames the election not as left versus right but as "well-fed versus the fed-up" ([The Guardian, 2026](#sources)). That reframing is an attempt to do the single hardest thing in politics, which is to take an emotion and give it a target that can be reached. It is also the exact thing the outrage economy is designed to prevent, because a target that can be reached is a target that can be satisfied, and satisfaction is not shareable content.

## What anger is for

None of this is an argument for calm. Calm is not a virtue, and the demand for it is usually a demand that the wronged person stop making the comfortable person uncomfortable. The Stoics, who are so often miscast as the philosophers of quiet, were not quiet. Seneca devoted three books to anger, and his aim was not to extinguish it but to subordinate it, because he understood exactly what a corrupted anger does: it "shows itself openly," he wrote, and "the greater it is, the more plainly it boils forth," until it "breaks itself to pieces upon the very thing which it crushes" ([Seneca, 1900](#sources)). His image for the angered man is not a coward. It is a falling rock.

Seneca is the right teacher here precisely because he keeps insisting that anger is *not* the same as courage, and that reason is the stronger instrument. "The sword of justice," he wrote, "is ill-placed in the hands of an angry man" ([Seneca, 1900](#sources)). He is not saying the sword is wrong. He is saying the hand must be steady. And he wrote this as a man who had watched emperors do precisely what an angry autocrat does: kill someone and then kill the men who reported the killing, and then kill the messenger who brought the prisoner back alive, "because one was proved to be innocent, two perished" ([Seneca, 1900](#sources)). Anger with a crown and anger without one produce the same thing. The difference is only how many people are inside the blast.

The American founders knew this from the other end. In the first of the Federalist Papers, Hamilton did not promise that the new Constitution would remove passion from public life. He predicted its opposite in the plainest possible terms: "A torrent of angry and malignant passions will be let loose" ([Hamilton, 1787](#sources)). His warning was about what such a torrent does to judgment. "History," he wrote, "will teach us" that the feigned zeal for the people's rights "has been found a much more certain road to the introduction of despotism," and that "of those men who have overturned the liberties of republics, the greatest number have begun their career by paying an obsequious court to the people; commencing demagogues, and ending tyrants" ([Hamilton, 1787](#sources)).

Read that last clause slowly, because it is the thesis of this essay written in 1787. The demagogue does not begin by attacking the people. He begins by flattering them, and by flattering their anger, and by telling them that the anger is the same thing as the cause. The tyrant is what the demagogue becomes when the anger has been used up and only the power remains.

## What the anger must not become

The other half of the founders' teaching is the one the angry forget, and Frederick Douglass stated it more plainly than any of them. "Power concedes nothing without a demand," he told an audience at Canandaigua in 1857. "It never did and it never will" ([Douglass, 1857](#sources)). This is the sentence that keeps the argument honest. Anger that makes a demand on power is not a disease. It is the only thing that has ever moved power at all, and the enslaved and the disenfranchised who have used it are the reason the republic is worth anything. Douglass knew the difference, and he drew the line himself: "Those who profess to favor freedom and yet deprecate agitation are men who want crops without plowing up the ground" ([Douglass, 1857](#sources)).

So the test is not whether anger exists. It is what the anger demands. Douglass demanded a specific thing of a specific power, and he named the terms on which he would be satisfied, which is why the demand could be met and has been, partially and too slowly, ever since. The corrupted anger demands nothing that can be granted. It demands recognition. It demands that the other side be made to feel it. It demands the continuation of the fight, because the fight is the point.

If anger is not the problem, then what is? The problem is that the two kinds of anger are indistinguishable from the inside and must be told apart by their fruit. There is a test, and it has four parts, and it is not difficult, and almost nobody applies it.

First: **does it name an object that can act?** Real anger aims at something that can change. A bill, a vote, a company, a court, an official with a name. The corrupted anger aims at a category: "them," the media, the establishment, the elites, the other party's voters. A category cannot act, so it can never be satisfied, so it is a perfect engine for a machine that needs to run forever.

Second: **does it end, or does it only recur?** The purpose of a fire is to change something and then be banked. The outrage economy needs the fire to keep burning the same fuel, so it teaches its users to relight the same grievance every morning. A cause that is genuinely advanced produces a result, and a result is boring, and boring is the one thing the machine cannot sell.

Third: **does it persuade anyone who did not already agree?** This is the question the moral-emotional study answers in the negative. If the effect of a message is confined to the people who already hold the view, the message has not made an argument. It has made a banner. There is a use for a banner, but a banner is not a movement, and confusing the two is how a majority is mistaken for a crowd.

Fourth, and hardest: **would you accept the repair if it arrived?** The moral test of political anger is whether the angry person wants the problem solved or wants the problem to remain available as a source of grievance. The second motive is more common than anyone admits, on both sides, and it is the reason so many fights in American politics have been going on for thirty years while nothing changes and the audiences grow.

Hamilton's friend James Madison supplied the deeper reason this test matters for a republic specifically. Faction, he argued, is "sown in the nature of man," and its causes "cannot be removed"; the only remedy is to control its effects ([Madison, 1787](#sources)). A republic is not a machine for eliminating anger. It is a machine for converting anger into decisions without letting the anger become the decision. Every institution the country has that is worth defending (the jury, the independent court, the election that the loser concedes, the press that prints what the government would rather it did not) exists for the single purpose of slowing the angry man down long enough for his better judgment to catch up. The outrage economy's entire business model is to defeat that delay. It is not selling politics. It is selling the removal of the one thing republican government cannot run without.

## The repair the anger is standing in for

There is a plainer way to say all of this, and Arthur Brooks, of all people, said it plainly at an event on the country's 250th anniversary this July. He was talking about courage, and he drew the distinction that the word "idiot" has been circling. "Nobody in history," he said, "has ever been persuaded by being told that they're stupid and evil. You can't insult anybody into agreement" ([Compart, 2026](#sources)). And then the sentence that ought to be taped to every screen that has a comment box: "When you hate, somebody's profiting—not you; you are the product. That's how they conscript you into their culture war. You need to be a conscientious objector" ([Compart, 2026](#sources)).

That is the whole thing. The angry idiot is not the man who is angry. He is the man who has been conscripted, and who mistakes his conscription for agency, and who will defend the arrangement because it has given him the one reward no other part of his life reliably provides: the feeling of having struck a blow when he has struck nothing at all.

The Pew Research Center measured the country's mood this year and found it sour in the ordinary way of a nation that has been disappointed by its leaders. In January, 69 percent of Americans said they were dissatisfied with the way things were going, against 29 percent satisfied. In December, 59 percent said the country's best days were behind it ([Pew Research Center, 2026](#sources)). But the same body of work found something the anger does not want to report: a majority, 68 percent, still said they felt hopeful about the future, and the share who believed the country would be less divided in twenty-five years had risen from 21 percent in 2023 to 33 percent in 2026 ([Pew Research Center, 2026](#sources); [Compart, 2026](#sources)). The country is not as far gone as its loudest voices require it to be. The loudest voices have a business interest in the opposite.

Which is where the word comes back, in its original sense, and inverts itself one more time. The *idiōtēs* was the man who took no part. He was the man who thought the public thing was not his affair. The remedy for the new idiot is not to go home and be quiet; that is the old disease wearing the mask of the cure. The remedy is the harder thing Brooks described: to participate in the public thing *as a public person*, which means to arrive with an argument that a stranger could accept, at a target a stranger could recognize, in a register a stranger could hear. To stand up to one's own side on behalf of the other, which he called the only real test of moral courage and which is the precise opposite of everything the outrage economy rewards.

The republic is not saved by whoever is angriest. It has never once been saved that way. It is saved by the people who are most clear about what hurts, who they can hold responsible for it, and what a fix would look like, and who are willing to be *satisfied* when the fix arrives, because a satisfied citizen is useless to a machine that has learned to run on his rage.

The bell still rings. It is the same bell it always was, the one that calls the town to assemble. It has simply been cracked, and the crack glows, and the light coming through it is beautiful enough that a great many people have decided the crack is the point. It is not. The crack is the damage. The bell is the instrument. The work is to mend the bell, and then to ring it, and then to listen.
