**Question:** What does a court do when the account it has been given stops holding?

**Answer:** It assembles a record of its own. Three rulings this week turned on the distance between what the government told a judge and what the documents showed. In the East Potomac case, Judge Ana C. Reyes denied the government's request to pause her injunction pending appeal. A week earlier she had given the parties notice that she will appoint a special master, Jessie K. Liu, to investigate whether counsel for the Justice Department and the Interior Department committed ethical violations in the case, and the government answered on Friday that it does not consent and that the rules do not permit it. On the same Monday, in the Epstein files case, Judge Emmet G. Sullivan ordered the Justice Department to publish the handwritten FBI interview notes behind three interview reports and refused to let it postpone the administrative record. By Friday afternoon Reyes had heard hours of argument about whether a park may close on Sunday, and as of this writing she had not ruled.

The golf week has four parts, and they arrived in an order that makes the last one harder to dismiss. On Monday the court denied the stay, in an opinion that says what the government's filings left out. On Tuesday it entered a preservation order that reaches a deputy chief of staff's personal cell phone and the National Garden of American Heroes Foundation. On Thursday it ordered the government to publish, unredacted, the names of the officials who prepared the submission to the National Capital Planning Commission. On Friday the government argued that the park must close because a seawall has been failing for years, and, as NPR reported from the courtroom, acknowledged that it has neither a seawall repair plan nor a contract to redevelop the course.

## The golf course: *D.C. Preservation League v. Department of the Interior*

Where the case stands: the League and two regular players sued on February 13, 2026, over the redevelopment of East Potomac, and Judge Reyes granted a preliminary injunction on October 26 after the Park Service felled hundreds of trees. The order bars cutting, topping or substantially pruning any tree in the park absent a court order, with an exception for an imminent threat to public safety that requires notice within 24 hours and a sworn declaration from a senior Park Service employee. It requires ten business days' written notice before any closure of the course or irreversible modification of the park. The government appealed on October 28, moved on October 29 to stay the injunction pending appeal, and said in a notice filed under the injunction that it would close the park south of Buckeye Drive, including the course, on November 15.

Monday's opinion denying the stay is the document to read this week, and its first page sets the tone. The government had complained of "intrusive judicial oversight" of "management of a golf course". The court answered that if park officials had planned nothing more than to manage a golf course, "the Court would have stayed in the gallery. As it had for months. The record, however, shows that during those same months DOI has been planning the opposite. Not to manage, but to destroy the historic East Potomac Golf Course."

What follows is a ground-by-ground answer, and its argument is cumulative rather than merely legal. On standing, the court says that whether prices will rise after renovation is speculative but that destroying three courses and turning them into one is plainly not. On agency action, it notes that the Park Service's acting director has already declared the redesign a "major [f]ederal action" requiring regulatory review, and that the government never explains why the court should discredit its own official. On finality, it holds that a sequence of discrete steps does not become reviewable only when the last one lands, or agencies could "avoid judicial review by doing what the Government attempts here". And on the balance of equities it observes that the President, the Secretary of the Interior, the acting director and the Associate Attorney General have each given the course considerable time, so "if some minimal manpower is needed to provide advance notice of certain permanent and irrevocable actions, the Government seems well-equipped to provide it."

The opinion's fifth section is titled "THE GOVERNMENT'S MOTION IS OUTDATED", and it is the part that reads as a finding about credibility rather than about law. It walks the chronology: the October 26 injunction, the President's Truth Social post three hours later saying the course would close "as scheduled", the October 28 notice conceding the President's "directives", and the October 29 stay motion that "said nothing of its U-turn on the November 15 closing date". Then the October 30 submission to the National Capital Planning Commission, which the court says "confirmed the Court's finding that the Washington National Plan exists" and is "impossible to square with the Government's prior representations".

The amended order at ECF 143 is the one thing the government won. It accepted five clarifications the parties worked out at ECF 129 and ECF 133: the 24-hour safety notice now runs from after the threat is addressed, the order's geographic reach is narrowed to the park southeast of Buckeye Drive, and two notice particulars are struck. The tree prohibition is unchanged, and the sixth particular still asks whether an action is taken in connection with the Washington National Plan or a Fazio design.

Tuesday's preservation order is broader than the dispute that produced it. It reaches any redesign of the course; every declaration and filing in the litigation; the Tree Management Plan and its compliance exhibits; the seawall renovation, the NCPC submission and the lease with National Links Trust, including the December 2025 termination of the original lease; the President's Truth Social posts of June 28 and October 18, 19, 26 and 28; and communications to or from any Interior or Park Service employee, "including but not limited to Deputy Chief of Staff Gregory Wischer on his personal cell phone", touching the course, the litigation or the National Garden of American Heroes Foundation. Privileged material must be preserved. By 6:00 p.m. on Monday the government must name each custodian who received the order and each who received an earlier litigation hold, and it may file that notice under seal.

Thursday's ruling on the sealed filing is the week's smallest and sharpest. The government had redacted three names from its list of everyone who worked on the NCPC submission. Applying the *Hubbard* factors, the court ordered the document refiled unredacted by 5:00 p.m. that day: "There is a strong presumption of public access in litigation. Two of the three redacted names have already been discussed in open court, significantly reducing any asserted privacy interests because their identities are substantially associated with this matter in the public domain. There is little possibility of prejudice from the disclosure of the names of government employees acting within the scope of their employment. And the public has a strong interest in knowing which government officials developed the NCPC proposal." The government complied and renewed its objection, arguing that ordering the names produced "is not a legitimate exercise of the Article III judicial authority and is an unconstitutional infringement on the Executive Branch's right to conduct its own affairs."

Then there is the inquiry into the government's own lawyers. On November 7 the court gave the parties notice that it intends to appoint Jessie K. Liu as special master to investigate whether Department of Justice or Interior Department counsel committed ethical violations in the matter through October 31, 2026, pointing to pages 70 to 74 of the October 30 hearing transcript. The plaintiffs took no position. The government answered on Friday that there is "no reasonable basis for conducting an ethics inquiry", that it does not consent to the appointment of "Ms. Jessie Liu or any Special Master", and that Rule 53 does not allow a court to appoint one over a party's objection absent consent, an exceptional condition, or the absence of an available judge. It notes that the court said at the October 30 hearing that it is "fully prepared to conduct this inquiry itself".

Friday's hearing is the part this page cannot report from the record, because the docket carries no transcript and no order. What the record does carry is the November 11 minute order setting out what the court wanted to hear: the new events since the last hearing, including the new lease, the anticipated seawall repair and the NCPC and CFA submissions; the likelihood of success on the merits; and whether the relief the plaintiffs seek reaches the golf course or the park. It also ordered government counsel to be ready to explain "what actions DOI or NPS plan to take at the seawall that require closure of the entire park", when those actions will occur, and whether any material action is scheduled to begin before NEPA review concludes.

NPR's Rachel Treisman reported from the courtroom that the government gave two reasons for closing almost the entire park: the lease with the current operator expires on the day the President named, and the seawall has been deteriorating for years. Treisman reported that the government "acknowledged it doesn't yet have a plan to repair the seawall or a contract to redevelop the golf course", that Reyes "suggested she'd rule on Saturday", and that she opened the hearing by raising recent CNN reporting that the Interior Department had destroyed documents related to the President's construction projects, which the department denies. Treisman reported that the government opposed the appointment and that, as she put it, Reyes "will probably lead that investigation herself".

The closure itself is now a published decision rather than a plan. The Park Service's [Record of Determination for the Temporary Closure of East Potomac Park South of Buckeye Drive](https://www.nps.gov/nama/learn/management/temporary-closure-of-east-potomac-park-south-of-buckeye-drive.htm), signed by Superintendent Kevin Griess on November 12, closes everything south of the drive, including the course, Ohio Drive, the seawall promenade and the fishing areas, effective November 15, with access restrictions beginning at 9:00 p.m. Its justification rests on the end of golf operations and on two condition assessments: a May 2026 assessment that recorded 110 deficiencies at the course, 69 rated critical or urgent and none of the 15 surveyed assets rated good, and a 2019 seawall scoping study finding the 18,807-foot wall "severely deteriorated in all areas and has failed in some". It also records the alternatives the Park Service considered and dismissed, and says the closure "will remain subject to review and may be modified or rescinded".

That document is where the week's arguments meet. It supplies the government's answer to the question the court ordered it to be ready for: what work requires closing the entire park. Whether the answer holds is what the ruling will say.

## The Kennedy Center: *Beatty v. Trump*

Where the case stands: Judge Cooper's opinion of May 29 declared the Board's December 2025 renaming void and preliminarily enjoined the March 16 closure decision; his order of September 15 enjoined the August 13 inscription and the "President Donald J. Trump Plaza" renaming. The Board voted to close the main building for two years on August 13 and again on September 15, and moved to dissolve the injunction under Rule 60(b)(5) at ECF 88. Discovery closed October 23. Beatty opposed the motion on October 30 in a 51-page brief and asked to put the executive director on the stand. The government replied on November 6. A hearing is set for 2:00 p.m. on November 19 in Courtroom 27A.

The week was about what that hearing will be allowed to decide. Beatty moved on Monday for leave to file a three-page sur-reply, arguing that the reply raised arguments and evidence for the first time: the motion to dissolve was filed before discovery, so the reply is where the government first addressed what the depositions produced. Defendants oppose. No order appears in the feed.

The November 4 order had asked for one joint submission identifying every disputed objection and privilege assertion. What arrived instead were two separate statements. Beatty filed hers on Thursday, explaining that both sides' counsel had been hit by an Outlook outage, that the government would be asking for a day, and that she wanted her position before the court ahead of the hearing. She then did something a plaintiff rarely does: she gave things up. She narrowed her challenge to the presidential-communications privilege alone, dropping attorney-client and work-product objections including to parts of log entries 4, 6 and 9, and said she would not seek further discovery on the motion. What she kept is specific: the nine log entries, the interrogatory and request-for-admission answers withheld on the same ground, and the deposition questions the government's counsel instructed the witness not to answer. The withheld material, she says, includes "180 messages between Howard Lutnick and Matthew Floca".

Her proposal is a sequence rather than a request for a broad ruling. Decide the logged documents before November 19, whether from the log or after in camera review; require Floca to testify at that hearing about anything the court finds unprivileged; then give guidance in advance about the oral communications, so that privilege objections at the hearing can be taken question by question. If the court cannot get enough information to do that in time, she asks it to let her reopen the depositions.

The government's statement, filed Friday, is shorter and harder. No further briefing is needed and no in camera review is required; the log is the record. It disputes the premise that anything about privilege has to be resolved for the court to grant the motion, and says Beatty's only argument tying withheld communications to the motion concerns the Secretary's influence on Delta, which the record already answers. In *In re Sealed Case*, it notes, "The presidential communications privilege applies to documents in their entirety."

It then does something unusual: it answers some of the deposition questions in a list, and refuses to answer others. On the Lutnick questions it volunteers detail, including that Floca recalls one conversation about being "brought in", roughly two between May and September about the Center's relationship with the Foundation, and none about the President's public statements. On the questions about the President it declines entirely, listing all of them, on the ground that "to confirm the absence of responsive communications with respect to some questions would be to confirm the presence of responsive communications with respect to others." A footnote closes with a sentence that no brief needs: "Plaintiff has subjected the Center and its management to extraordinary burdens through this litigation. Enough is enough."

The November 19 hearing will now turn on a question the plaintiff framed and the government rejected: whether the court can lift a preliminary injunction on a record that excludes the communications most likely to bear on why the Board voted as it did. Beatty says a court may deny the motion without them. The government says it may grant it without them. Only one of those can be right.

## The Epstein files: *Phang v. Blanche*

Where the case stands: Judge Sullivan granted a preliminary injunction on June 25, holding that the Epstein Files Transparency Act required disclosure. On September 16 he granted Phang's motion to compel an answer, denied the government's motion to stay his deadlines, and set the administrative record. The government answered on September 24 and appealed. On October 28 the D.C. Circuit denied a stay and left the district court's orders in place. The parties filed a joint notice on November 2.

Three orders issued on November 9, and together they amount to a ruling that the case will be decided on a record rather than on assurances. The first, ECF 64, orders the government to publish in the Epstein Library the underlying handwritten FBI interview notes behind the FD-302 reports bearing Bates numbers EFTA01245620, EFTA02858481 and EFTA02858491, redacted as the statute requires and consistent with the Department's own January 4, 2026 review protocol, within fourteen days and with a notice of compliance on the docket within 24 hours of publication.

The government had asked the court to stay any such order so it could seek appellate review. The court declined, and did something more useful: it built the stay into the deadline instead. Fourteen days give the government time to move in the D.C. Circuit, and if it does so before the period runs, "the Order is TEMPORARILY STAYED pending the D.C. Circuit's resolution of any such motion." The government's argument had been that the notes duplicate the typed reports and that the redaction done for the court "should not be taken as a representative sample of what could be done, or what would happen, if the Department were required to redact and produce handwritten notes on a larger scale." The court had the unredacted notes in camera and did not accept either point.

The second order, ECF 65, is the one with the longer shadow. The government had moved to postpone the certified list of the administrative record until its Rule 12(c) motion was resolved, on the theory that the documents it cited are already public and that the case turns on pure questions of law. The court rejected both halves. A Rule 12(c) motion, it held, does rely on the record, and it is "Defendant's obligation to identify the specific records upon which he relied" rather than the plaintiff's or the court's job "to comb through the FBI Vault". It gave two examples where the cited documents do not answer the question the government says they answer: the six-month gap between the February 15, 2026 report to Congress and its August 27 publication in the Federal Register, and the justification for concluding that all foreign-language documents were properly withheld. The certified list is due November 17, the record produced by December 1, and Phang's response to the Rule 12(c) motion by December 31.

The third order, ECF 66, is the government's one win, and it is a qualified one. Phang had asked the court to require the foreign-language review completed by November 27. The court declined: "At this time, and based on Defendant's representation, the Court will not order Defendant to complete the review within a certain timeframe." What it ordered instead is a status report on December 10 stating where the review stands and when publication would begin, and requiring that the report rest on "a written declaration made by a person with personal knowledge". The deadline is gone; the accounting is not.

Two of the minute entries that Monday are worth recording for what they show about the docket's temperature. The court denied the motion to alter judgment at ECF 53 as "patently insubstantial and frivolous". It found the motion of a non-party, Jane Doe, to intervene for the purpose of preserving victim redactions moot in light of its earlier orders, but left the door open to reconsideration for good cause shown by November 24. The next day it denied leave to file to a pro se non-party who had tried twice.

The circuit appeal, meanwhile, sits on a schedule that runs to late February. Whatever the panel holds, the record of compliance is being made in Judge Sullivan's courtroom first, and this week it got a date certain.

## The Epstein files: *MSW Media v. DOJ*

Where the case stands: MSW Media sued in August 2025 over two FOIA requests, one for grand jury material and one for the FBI's videos on how to review Epstein records. The grand jury request was dropped; the case now concerns nine training videos, withheld in full under four exemptions. Judge Howell denied expedited processing on July 1, 2026, a ruling about speed rather than about the videos, and the government moved for summary judgment on October 2. The plaintiff's opposition and cross-motion were filed on November 7.

This week's filing is a six-hour extension asked for after the fact. The opposition was due November 6. Counsel filed it at 6:00 a.m. the next morning and moved nunc pro tunc for the enlargement, explaining that final discussions with the client had removed several items from controversy, that this was the second extension request on the filing, and that he had written to opposing counsel on Friday night without a reply and so filed the motion as opposed rather than delay further. The government filed a notice of non-opposition the next day, and Judge Howell granted the motion nunc pro tunc on November 10.

The correction matters for the calendar rather than the merits. The registry behind this page recorded that no order on the plaintiff's earlier extension motion, ECF 23, appeared in the feed. One does: the minute order of November 2 granted it and issued an amended scheduling order, as last week's page reported. The proposal ECF 23 carried put the government's reply and opposition in late November and the plaintiff's reply in December, and the case will be fully briefed for a ruling on the cross motions after that. The feed truncates the order's own text at its first colon, so this page does not state the dates as the court's.

Nothing else moved. That is the quietest of the five dockets, and the question before it remains narrow: whether a disclosure statute's list of permitted withholdings displaces the exemptions an agency would otherwise cite.

## The Epstein files on appeal

Where the appeal stands: the government moved on September 22 to stay the June 25 and September 16 orders and to expedite. On October 28 a panel denied the stay and referred Phang's motion to dismiss the appeal of the September 16 order to the merits panel. On November 5 the clerk set the briefing schedule, running from the government's opening brief on December 15 to final briefs on February 25.

Nothing was filed at the circuit this week. The most recent entry on the docket is dated November 6, and it is a motion the previous installment of this page did not name: the government asked the court to expedite the case and to expedite oral argument, in 865 words. No order on it appears in the feed.

That motion is the tell. A party that has just been refused a stay, and that will not file its merits brief for another month, has asked the circuit to argue the appeal sooner than its own schedule provides. Whether the court grants that is worth watching, because the district court's orders are doing their work in the meantime: the record is due December 1, the notes are due November 23, and the review the circuit declined to pause is now reporting to Judge Sullivan with a declaration attached.

## What the coverage showed

One account of Friday's hearing could be retrieved and read. NPR's ["The government says it will close a beloved D.C. park Sunday. Will a judge step in?"](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/13/nx-s1-6039273/east-po-hearing-nov-13), reported by Rachel Treisman on November 13 for All Things Considered, is accurate on the things this page can check against the record: the two reasons the government gave, the lease expiring on the day the President named, the absence of a seawall repair plan and of a redevelopment contract, the judge's skepticism, and the special master fight ending with the government's objection.

What it adds, and the record cannot, is the courtroom itself: that the judge asked how many people were there because they care about the park and almost all of them raised their hands; that she said she has received death threats over the case; that she opened by raising the CNN reporting on destroyed documents and told the government's lawyer that if she learned otherwise there would be consequences "in a zeptosecond", which she called the shortest measurement of time ever recorded in a laboratory. Those are things said in a room this page did not sit in, and they are reported here as NPR reported them.

Four other accounts appeared in search results, from the Washington Post, The Hill, DC News Now and Law&Crime. None served an automated fetch, so this page did not read them and does not characterize them. That is a statement about the checker, not about the reporting.

**Wednesday, December 31.** Phang's response to the Rule 12(c) motion for judgment on the pleadings.

Pending, any day: the golf ruling, which the court said would come today; an order on the Kennedy Center sur-reply; and a ruling on the request to hear the Center's executive director live on November 19.

The thing to hold on to when reading next week's version of this page: four courts are asking variations of one question. Judge Reyes wants to know who wrote the submission and what the government intends to do at the seawall. Judge Sullivan wants the notes behind the report and a list of the records on which the agency relied. Judge Cooper will decide on Thursday whether he needs to know what the President's advisers said before he can rule. And the D.C. Circuit has been asked to hear the appeal sooner.

In each, the government's answer has been that the question need not be answered. This week, two judges said it did. A third will say on Thursday whether he agrees.
