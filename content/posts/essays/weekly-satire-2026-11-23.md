I said the last apology would be the last one. I meant it, which is the trouble with a promise made in the middle of a story: the next chapter arrives, and the man who promised is standing there holding it.

I am sorry for connecting the dots.

On Friday the President held his first news conference since he returned from Asia and was asked about the week. United Airlines had "resolved a technology issue that delayed flight departures" on Monday. The pilots' union said the airline could not "provide outbound flights with final weights." Visa, Mastercard and JPMorgan Chase were among the institutions to report issues, "with all three pointing to hackers as the cause for customer data leaks." A Microsoft outage "left millions of users without email access." The National Security Agency's classified communications network was degraded for three days; the workforce was told of "delayed openings," and some facilities went to "Code Orange." On Friday the agency said it could "confirm that we are now operating under normal conditions."

I read the week as one story. The President read it as a list. "They're all separate events," he said. "They're not like, Chase went down and this one went down."

He gave a cause as well, and it is the plainest sentence anyone in the government offered all week: "We have thousands of companies that are upgrading right now, and when they upgrade, bad things happen." He added that "some of that gets a little bit out of control, but they're all separate events."

The director of national intelligence, Jay Clayton, said the same thing in the language of the trade. The patching that followed maintenance on PHP, "a ubiquitous programming language," had "caused some of these systems to not come back up."

This is not a cover story. It is a maintenance schedule. Every large system in the country is being edited at once, and an edit is an outage with a purpose. The week was the purpose arriving on time.

Where I went wrong was in the next step. Having been told that the events were separate, I assumed that someone had checked. The national cyber director is the President's principal adviser on national cybersecurity policy and strategy. On Thursday the White House confirmed that the director, Sean Cairncross, was leaving. Axios, citing two White House officials, reported that the chief of staff, Stephen Miller, had pushed for his departure. On Friday the President was asked why. "Because I didn't think that person was good enough," he said.

That is the apology undone, and it takes one sentence. The sentence is not an explanation of the week. It is an explanation of the man. A week in which banks reported leaks of customer data and the National Security Agency's classified network was disrupted is the week a cyber director is most useful, and it is the week this one left. The reason given is not that he failed at anything. It is that he was not good enough, which is a judgment about a person, delivered in place of a judgment about the events.

The briefing had a second half. The administration has asked Congress for $50 billion to replace the air traffic control system, which the President called "ancient" and "obsolete." He said he told the transportation secretary not to spend money "to patch up all over the place" until the overhaul money arrived. In the private sector, the upgrade is what breaks things. In the public sector, the failure to upgrade is what breaks things. Both claims were made on Friday, in the same room.

So the government's account of the outages is maintenance, its remedy for the outages is more maintenance, and the official removed in the middle of it was the one whose title was cyber director. A list does not need a director. A pattern does.

I am sorry for connecting the dots. The White House says there were none. On Thursday it confirmed that the cyber director was leaving. On Friday the President explained why: "Because I didn't think that person was good enough."
