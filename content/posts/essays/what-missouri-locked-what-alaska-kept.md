On November 3, voters in two states were asked what they thought of their own election machinery, and both answered in the same direction.

In Missouri, a congressional map the legislature drew in 2025 to gain a seat was rejected 61.8 percent to 38.2, with all 3,214 precincts reporting in the count the state published, which remains unofficial until certification.[^1] In Alaska, a repeal of ranked-choice voting, the open primary, and the state's donor-disclosure rules was losing 52.8 percent to 47.2 with most of the count in.[^2]

Both results get filed under "democracy" and left there. The more useful reading is narrower and harder. Missouri's voters did not only kill a map. On the same ballot they entrenched, at 59.9 percent, the instrument that killed it.[^3] Alaska's voters did not only keep a voting method. They kept it by a wider margin than the last time they were asked, against a campaign that had the governor, the state's congressional delegation, and a $1.5 million check from Elon Musk.[^4]

One of those outcomes is structural. The other is a habit, and habits can be repealed. And the ceiling that stopped me in September, when I withdrew the statewide ranked-ballot remedy I had wanted to propose for Missouri, has not moved at all: federal law still forbids what the problem calls for.[^5]

## The map lost by twenty-three points

Start with what Missouri actually voted on, because the ballot language inverted the usual polarity. Proposition A was a veto referendum, a citizen petition against House Bill 1, the map the legislature passed in a 2025 special session at President Trump's urging. A "yes" vote upheld the new map; a "no" vote kept the districts Missouri has used since 2022.[^1]

The fight had already been litigated to the edge of the calendar. Secretary of State Denny Hoskins refused in August to certify the referendum, arguing the petition was unconstitutional; the Missouri Supreme Court overruled him in both that case and a companion petition, ordered both measures onto the ballot, and later found him in contempt when he tried to use the new map anyway. He backed down, and the justices did not have him arrested.[^6]

The September 3 opinion did two things at once. It held that "the referendum petition was legal, sufficient, and timely," and it enjoined the secretary from using the 2025 plan "for the November 2026 general election or at any other time thereafter unless and until" voters approved it, in which case it would "take effect 30 days thereafter."[^7] So the November election ran on the 2022 lines either way, and a yes vote would have changed nothing about the districts Missouri used this year; it would only have restored the gerrymander for the next cycle.

That is why the margin is worth stating plainly. Roughly three of every five Missourians who voted on the question rejected the map their legislature drew, knowing the rejection cost them nothing in November.

## The larger vote was the one about the rules

Proposition A was the loud measure. Amendment 6 was the consequential one, and it passed by more than nineteen points.

What it does is narrow and specific. It makes the initiative and referendum powers "fundamental rights," subject to strict scrutiny, and it forbids the legislature from weakening them by raising signature thresholds, shortening the gathering window, narrowing subject matter, or requiring a supermajority to pass an initiative.[^3] More to the point, it prohibits the legislature from changing any citizen-initiated law or amendment, or from passing, proposing, or referring a law "similar in effect to a law rejected by referendum petition," unless 80 percent of both chambers vote to refer the change to the voters, who then decide. The protection reaches back to measures enacted or rejected after January 1, 2010.[^3]

Read that second clause next to the first result. Missouri's voters rejected House Bill 1 by referendum in November, and by the same vote they made it materially harder for the same legislature to try again.

This is not an isolated spasm. In August, Missourians rejected a Republican-backed proposal to make citizen initiatives harder to pass by more than four to one.[^6] November's amendment went further in the opposite direction, and Travis Crum, a law professor at Washington University in St. Louis, told ProPublica he knew of no other state with a threshold quite like the 80 percent rule.[^6] Hoskins, one of the amendment's most prominent opponents, put his objection in blunter terms before the election: if it passed, "it will basically cut our legislature off at the knees as far as being able to pass laws to reduce crime and cut taxes."[^6]

That is the honest way to describe it. The voters decided that when the legislature disagrees with them, the legislature should usually lose.

## Alaska kept the system, by more than last time

Alaska is the only state besides Maine where ranked-choice voting runs statewide, and it runs the full package: an open primary in which all candidates appear on one ballot, the top four advancing, and a general election in which voters rank them.[^8]

The state adopted that system in 2020 and used it first in 2022. In 2024, opponents got a repeal onto the ballot and lost by 743 votes out of roughly 321,000 cast, a margin of about two-tenths of one percent.[^9] This year the repeal came back with heavier backing. Governor Mike Dunleavy, Representative Nick Begich, and President Trump all called for the system's end, and the repeal committee's principal funder, the Aurora Action Network, received $1.5 million from Elon Musk. The opposition raised more than $10.4 million, largely from out-of-state donors, against roughly $2.6 million for the repeal side.[^9]

The repeal lost by more. With 84 percent of the expected vote counted on November 12, Decision Desk HQ's tally stood at 128,575 against 114,884, which is 52.8 to 47.2.[^2] The "no" side began election night ahead and widened its lead as late ballots arrived, the reverse of 2024, when the reform side had to come from behind to survive.[^10]

Ballot Measure 2 was broader than its headline. It would have repealed the open primary and the ranked general election together, and it would have removed the disclosure rules requiring public reporting of large contributions, which is the part of the 2020 package that has attracted the most money and the most animosity.[^8] On the same ballot, Alaskans passed Measure 3 by about 69 percent, a citizenship requirement for voting that changes nothing in practice, since citizenship was already required.[^11]

There is a reading in which that pair is the story of the year. The reform that costs something survived; the reform that costs nothing won in a landslide.

## The count is not finished, and that is the honest state of it

Alaska does not certify until November 25, and none of these numbers is final. The Division of Elections lists the results as unofficial, and its ranked-choice tabulations for the contests that need them do not begin until November 18.[^12]

That last detail matters more than it looks. Alaska's own Senate race has no first-round majority: Mary Peltola led Dan Sullivan 49.0 percent to 47.2 in first choices, with the rest scattered across minor candidates.[^13] The seat will be decided by the ranked-choice tabulation on November 18, using the method the voters just chose to keep. Whatever one thinks of the system, its output will be visible within a week, in a race that decides control of the Senate.

## What the city record shows

The Alaska result is one data point. The fuller record runs the other way from the way the reform's critics describe it, and the pattern inside that record is consistent.

In 2026, two states, two counties, and twelve cities used ranked-choice voting, and more than four million people had the chance to rank candidates.[^14] On the same day, Irvine, California approved the method with 52 percent and Longmont, Colorado approved it with 56 percent; a Honolulu charter measure to adopt it fell short.[^15] FairVote's count of city ballot measures puts the record at 36 wins in the last 38.[^15]

The failures cluster in one place: statewide adoption. In 2024, Colorado's Proposition 131 lost, Idaho's Proposition 1 lost, Oregon's Measure 117 lost, and Nevada's Measure 3 lost on its second required passage.[^15] The adoptions that succeed are local. The repeals that fail are everywhere the method has actually been used, from Minnetonka in 2023 to Alaska twice.

That is a specific kind of evidence, and it is worth naming what it is. Voters who have used a ranked ballot tend not to want it taken away. Voters who have not used one are being asked to buy a method they have never tried, at the scale of a state, usually against a well-funded "too complicated" campaign. Neither pattern says the method is good. Together they say something narrower and more useful about where the appetite is real: in the places where the cost has already been paid.

## The ceiling

Now the part that has not changed since September, and the reason this piece is not a call to adopt ranked-choice voting everywhere.

In September, writing about Missouri's lost primary, I proposed the cleanest remedy I could imagine: put every primary winner on every ballot and let the state's voters rank them, so that a district whose nominee no longer matches its lines could still be represented by someone the whole electorate had a hand in choosing. I withdrew it after checking the law, for four reasons, and two of them were fatal by themselves.[^5]

The first is 2 U.S.C. § 2c, passed in 1967, which requires a state with more than one representative to establish districts and provides that "Representatives shall be elected only from districts so established, no district to elect more than one Representative."[^16] A statewide pool of every nominee, ranked to fill eight seats, is an at-large multi-member election, and federal law forbids it. The second is Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, which since Thornburg v. Gingles has treated at-large elections that submerge a concentrated minority electorate as dilution.[^5] A statewide ranked pool would sweep St. Louis and Kansas City into a statewide plurality. No court would order that as a remedy for a gerrymander.

The other two reasons were the calendar and the fact that no map produces a nominee for a district that did not exist when the primary ran. Neither has improved.

What Alaska and Maine demonstrate is the ceiling's shape rather than its absence. Ranked-choice voting works for federal elections in the United States, and it works at scale, so long as it operates inside single-member districts: Alaska ranks four candidates for one House seat, and Maine ranks candidates for one Senate seat. That is legal, and it produces majority winners instead of plurality winners. What remains forbidden is the thing I wanted: proportional representation of a whole state in its House delegation. The method can change how a district picks its member. It cannot change the fact that the district picks one.

That distinction matters for anyone who reads November 3 as a mandate for structural reform. The achievable version of this argument is modest and real: a majority requirement instead of a plurality, in races that are now decided by whichever side's vote splits less. The version that would actually repair a stolen district is unavailable, and no election result changes that.

## What the two results tell us

Put the two states side by side and the shared premise is not about maps or methods. It is about who is allowed to decide.

Missouri's voters used a veto to kill a map, then wrote the veto into the constitution with an 80 percent wall behind it. Alaska's voters used a repeal-proof majority to keep a system they have now run through two election cycles, and they did it while the count that will decide their Senate seat under that system was still running. In both places the question was not whether the machinery is perfect. It was who holds the handle.

Missouri spent the last year learning how much the handle matters. A secretary of state who controls the timing of a petition controls whether the people's veto arrives in time to be a veto at all.[^5] The remedy that essay landed on was procedural: make the suspension automatic when the signatures land, so that no official's calendar can convert a valid petition into a moot one. Amendment 6 does not do that. It does something adjacent and larger, which is to make the voters' verdicts harder to overturn after the fact.

Alaska's lesson is narrower and, for the reform movement, less comfortable. A voting method survives where people have used it, and it keeps losing where they have not. That is not an argument for patience. It is an argument about sequence: the case for ranked ballots is made in a city election that works, not in a statewide campaign ad that says the other side is rigging something.

Both states, in other words, voted for the same thing. Not ranked-choice voting, and not against gerrymandering as such. They voted to keep the deciding.

That is the part worth carrying out of November 3. The machinery is not self-executing and never was. It runs when someone turns the handle, and the only durable fix is to make sure the people hold it, whether the ballot they hold is a veto, an initiative, or a ranking.

Think clearly. Live intentionally. Love deeply.
