# The 2026 Senate Map: A Race-by-Race Guide

**The setup:** Republicans currently hold the Senate 53–47. Thirty-five seats are on the November 2026 ballot — 33 regular contests plus two special elections to fill vacancies. Democrats need a net gain of four seats to take the majority; Republicans can afford to lose only two and still hold it outright (a loss of three produces a 50–50 tie, broken by the vice president).

That math looks forbidding for Democrats on paper. But this cycle has been anything but predictable — a sitting senator has died in office, an incumbent lost his own primary for the first time in fourteen years, a Democratic nominee collapsed under scandal and was replaced eleven weeks before the general, and at least two nominally safe Republican governors' handpicked appointees are running as first-time statewide candidates. What follows is a state-by-state guide to all thirty-five races, grouped from most to least competitive, with a projected winner for each and the reasoning behind it — drawn from Cook Political Report, Sabato's Crystal Ball, Inside Elections, and primary reporting current as of early August 2026.

A caveat worth stating plainly: several of these primaries hadn't happened yet as of this writing, and ratings on the closest races move week to week. Where a race is genuinely too close to call, I've said so rather than forcing a pick.

---

## I. The Toss-Ups

These five races are where the majority will actually be decided.

### Alaska

Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan faces former U.S. Representative Mary Peltola, the first Alaska Native elected to Congress, who lost her House seat in 2024 but remains broadly popular and is out-fundraising expectations. Alaska's unusual top-four nonpartisan primary and ranked-choice general election add real volatility that traditional models struggle to price in. Cook Political Report and Sabato's Crystal Ball both moved this from "Lean Republican" to **Toss-up** over the summer; Inside Elections is more bullish on Sullivan, rating it Lean R — a genuine three-way disagreement among forecasters.

**Projection:** Too close to call. Sullivan has incumbency and a red-leaning state; Peltola has crossover appeal and momentum. Neither can be called with confidence.

### Maine

Susan Collins is seeking a historic sixth term in a state Kamala Harris carried by seven points in 2024 — but Collins has outrun Maine's partisan lean for decades. Democrats' original nominee, Graham Platner, won his primary in June and then imploded in July after a former partner accused him of rape (an allegation he denies) and past social-media posts about sexual assault surfaced; Senators Ruben Gallego and Elizabeth Warren pulled their endorsements, and Platner withdrew.[^1] Maine Democrats hastily nominated former state Senate President Troy Jackson as his replacement on July 25 — barely three months before the election. Cook and Sabato both rate this a **Toss-up**.

**Projection:** No confident pick. Collins's personal brand is a real asset, but a hostile national environment for Republicans and a viable, if late-installed, Democratic nominee make this one of the two or three most competitive races in the country.

### Michigan

Gary Peters is retiring, and Republicans see this open seat — in a state Donald Trump narrowly carried in 2024 — as their best pickup opportunity against Democratic-held turf. The Democratic primary was a close, expensive fight between Representative Haley Stevens (the establishment favorite, backed by Chuck Schumer) and progressive Abdul El-Sayed, after state Senator Mallory McMorrow dropped out in early July. Former Representative Mike Rogers is the consolidated Republican nominee. Notably, polling has shown Rogers running closer against El-Sayed than against Stevens — meaning which Democrat wins the primary could materially change the general election. Both Cook and Sabato rate it a **Toss-up**.

**Projection:** Too close to call, and contingent on an outcome that wasn't yet settled at publication.

### Ohio

This is the marquee race of the cycle. Jon Husted, appointed by Governor DeWine to fill the seat JD Vance vacated to become vice president, has never won statewide office in his own right. He faces Sherrod Brown — the three-term former senator who lost his own seat in 2024 but remains a top-tier recruit with statewide name recognition. Cook Political Report moved the race from "Likely Republican" to "Lean Republican" once Brown entered; Sabato's Crystal Ball went further, calling it a **Toss-up** in June. Polling has been within a point or two in either direction depending on the pollster.

**Projection:** Genuine toss-up. Husted holds a slight structural edge as the incumbent in a state that's trended red, but no forecaster is calling this safe.

### Texas

Texas hasn't elected a Democratic senator since 1988, but the Republican Party nominated Attorney General Ken Paxton — impeached in 2023, the subject of a securities-fraud case resolved in 2024, and facing a messy divorce and a controversial plea deal — while Democrats nominated James Talarico, a 37-year-old state representative and seminary student who out-raised Paxton roughly five-to-one ($40.3 million to $7.6 million as of July) and leads him on both favorability and the economy in public polling. Cook Political Report rates it **Lean Republican**, reflecting Texas's underlying partisan lean, but multiple public polls have shown a genuine single-digit race. *[I wrote a full compare-and-contrast of this race — the candidates, the money, and why it's suddenly competitive — in a [separate piece]({{< ref "texas-senate-2026-the-preachers-son-vs-the-indicted-attorney-general" >}}).]*

**Projection:** Leans Republican on the state's fundamentals, but this is the closest Texas has come to flipping a Senate seat in a generation, and it would not be a shock if Talarico won.

## II. Leaning Democratic

### Georgia

Arguably the second-most-important race in the country. Jon Ossoff is one of only two Democratic incumbents defending a seat in a state Trump carried in 2024, and Republicans nominated Representative Mike Collins after a bruising runoff against former UT football coach Derek Dooley. Collins's combative online persona and a lagging fundraising operation (roughly $2 million raised in the second quarter, against Ossoff's $42 million cash on hand) have forecasters increasingly confident in the incumbent — Sabato's Crystal Ball upgraded the race from "Lean Democratic" to **Likely Democratic** in late July, citing the financial disparity and June–July polling showing Ossoff with a double-digit lead in some surveys.

**Projection:** Jon Ossoff (D), though Georgia's underlying competitiveness means this stays short of "safe."

### Minnesota

Tina Smith's retirement turned a reliably blue seat (Minnesota hasn't elected a Republican senator since 2002) into a race worth watching, mostly because of a bitter, expensive Democratic primary between Lieutenant Governor Peggy Flanagan and Representative Angie Craig. On the Republican side, 2024 nominee Royce White has struggled to consolidate a fractured state party. Cook and Inside Elections both rate it **Lean Democratic** — a shift from "Safe D" purely because of the retirement and the national environment, not because Minnesota itself has moved.

**Projection:** The Democratic primary winner is favored in November, but not by the wide margin the state's history would normally suggest.

### New Hampshire

New England's only genuine swing state got more interesting when former Senator John E. Sununu — who lost this exact seat to retiring incumbent Jeanne Shaheen in 2008 — entered the race trying to reclaim it, running against former Massachusetts Senator Scott Brown for the GOP nod. Representative Chris Pappas is the clear Democratic favorite. Cook Political Report explicitly reaffirmed a **Lean Democratic** rating even after Sununu's entry, though some reporting suggests the race has tightened since.

**Projection:** Leans toward Chris Pappas (D), but a Sununu win would not be a surprise.

### North Carolina

Thom Tillis's retirement — following public friction with Trump — immediately made this one of Democrats' top pickup targets. Former Governor Roy Cooper, who won statewide races even as Trump carried North Carolina, is running against RNC Chairman Michael Whatley. Early general-election polling has shown Cooper with a mid-to-high single-digit lead. Ratings have diverged: Cook Political Report has it at **Lean Democratic** as of July, while Inside Elections still calls it a Toss-up; both agree it has moved away from the dead-even rating all three forecasters shared back in March.

**Projection:** Leans toward Roy Cooper (D) given his crossover appeal and current polling, but North Carolina's history of nail-biter statewide races argues for caution.

## III. Leaning Republican

### Iowa

One of the cycle's real surprises. Iowa hasn't sent a Democrat to the Senate since 2008 and Trump carried it by 14 points in 2024, yet Democratic nominee Josh Turek — a Paralympic gold medalist and state representative — has run a stronger-than-expected campaign against Republican Representative Ashley Hinson amid voter frustration over the cost of living. July polling has been genuinely mixed: one survey had Turek up 4, another had Hinson up 2. Both Cook and Sabato moved the race from "Likely Republican" to **Lean Republican** in June.

**Projection:** Leans Republican, but not safely — this is not the call it would have been a year ago.

### Montana

Steve Daines pulled off an unusual bit of political maneuvering, withdrawing minutes before the March filing deadline in a move widely read as an effort to prevent top-tier Democratic recruits from jumping into the race against him. His handpicked successor, U.S. Attorney Kurt Alme, won the primary comfortably, but the general election is now a messy three-way contest — Democrat Alani Bankhead has refused to drop out despite pressure to clear the field for independent Seth Bodnar, and recent polling shows the anti-Republican vote splitting between them while Alme leads with roughly 45%. Cook rates it **Lean Republican**; Sabato calls it Likely Republican.

**Projection:** Kurt Alme (R), favored largely because of vote-splitting, though not by the margin a deep-red state would normally produce.

### Nebraska

Pete Ricketts is seeking a full term after winning a 2022 special election, and normally this would be a non-contest. But independent Dan Osborn — a former union leader who ran a surprisingly strong 2024 race against Deb Fischer — is running again, and the Democratic nominee has reportedly pledged to step aside to consolidate the anti-Ricketts vote behind him. Cook Political Report has moved the race from "Solid Republican" to **Likely Republican**; Inside Elections rates it Lean Republican. Some polling has shown the race within a single point.

**Projection:** Pete Ricketts (R), narrowly favored as the incumbent in a heavily Republican state, but Osborn represents a live threat.

## IV. Races to Watch Despite the Rating

### South Carolina

The most extraordinary story of the cycle. Lindsey Graham won his June primary and then died on July 11 of an aortic dissection at 71, hours after returning from a trip to Ukraine.[^2] Governor Henry McMaster appointed Graham's sister, Darline Graham, as interim senator — the first woman to represent South Carolina in the Senate — and she is now running in a special Republican primary on August 11 against a crowded field that includes Representatives Russell Fry and Ralph Norman and former Governor Mark Sanford. Democrats already have their nominee locked in: pediatrician Annie Andrews, who won her primary in June. Cook Political Report still calls the seat **Solid Republican**; Inside Elections has moved it to Likely Republican, explicitly citing the uncertainty created by Graham's death.

**Projection:** Likely a Republican — probably Darline Graham or Russell Fry — but genuinely less certain than a typical South Carolina Senate race given the unresolved primary.

### Florida

Ashley Moody, appointed to fill Marco Rubio's seat after he became Secretary of State, faces Alex Vindman, the retired Army officer and former National Security Council staffer who became a prominent figure during Trump's first impeachment. Vindman has raised a striking $16.7 million and is framing the race as a referendum on Trump, but polling has consistently shown Moody leading by high single digits. Sabato's Crystal Ball downgraded the race from "Safe Republican" to **Likely Republican** after Vindman's fundraising surge.

**Projection:** Ashley Moody (R) is favored — this isn't a toss-up, but it's the closest of the "safe" Republican-held seats and worth watching if Vindman's fundraising keeps translating into movement.

## V. Safe Democratic Seats

**Colorado** — John Hickenlooper defeated a progressive primary challenge from state Senator Julie Gonzales and faces Republican state Senator Mark Baisley, who was unable to attract serious national GOP investment. **Solid Democratic**; projected winner: Hickenlooper.

**Delaware** — Chris Coons faces only token primary opposition in a small, reliably blue state with a thin Republican bench. **Solid Democratic**; projected winner: Coons.

**Illinois** — Dick Durbin's retirement after 44 years produced a genuinely crowded primary, won by Lieutenant Governor Juliana Stratton over two sitting House members. The general election itself isn't competitive — Illinois hasn't elected a Republican senator since 2010. **Solid Democratic**; projected winner: Stratton, who would be the first Black woman to represent Illinois in the Senate.

**Massachusetts** — The real contest here is the Democratic primary, an intraparty generational fight between 80-year-old incumbent Ed Markey and Representative Seth Moulton. Whoever wins is a heavy favorite in November — Massachusetts hasn't elected a Republican senator since 1972. **Solid Democratic**.

**New Jersey** — Cory Booker, seeking a third full term, faces Republican Justin Murphy, who emerged from a thin GOP field. New Jersey hasn't elected a Republican senator since 1972. **Solid Democratic**; projected winner: Booker.

**New Mexico** — Ben Ray Luján drew no conventional Republican challenger at all; Larry Marker won the GOP nomination as a write-in candidate. **Safe Democratic**; projected winner: Luján, essentially uncontested.

**Oregon** — Jeff Merkley, seeking a fourth term, faces state Senator David Brock Smith in a state Harris carried by 14 points. **Solid Democratic**; projected winner: Merkley.

**Rhode Island** — Jack Reed, first elected in 1996, remains enormously popular (52% to 34% over his closest Republican rival in an April poll) in a state that has been exclusively Democratic in Senate races since 2007. **Solid/Safe Democratic**; projected winner: Reed.

**Virginia** — Mark Warner is unopposed in his own primary and faces a Republican field with no prior statewide experience, running in a state where Democrat Abigail Spanberger just won the 2025 governor's race by 15 points. **Solid Democratic**; projected winner: Warner.

## VI. Safe Republican Seats

**Alabama** — Tommy Tuberville left to run for governor; Representative Barry Moore won a Trump-endorsed runoff for the open seat. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Moore.

**Arkansas** — Tom Cotton faces no serious threat; Democratic nominee Hallie Shoffner, a sixth-generation farmer, is a credible recruit in an uncompetitive state. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Cotton.

**Idaho** — Jim Risch faces no realistic threat despite a late-cycle switch on the Democratic side, where the primary winner withdrew in favor of an independent, Todd Achilles. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Risch.

**Kansas** — Roger Marshall faces only token primary opposition; the far more interesting contest is a crowded Democratic primary. No forecaster rates the general competitive regardless of who emerges. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Marshall.

**Kentucky** — Mitch McConnell's retirement after 40 years closes a chapter of institutional Republican leadership, but the seat isn't competitive. Representative Andy Barr won the Trump-backed nomination; Charles Booker, running for a third time, gives Democrats a familiar but long-shot nominee. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Barr.

**Louisiana** — The headline is entirely about the primary: Bill Cassidy became the first sitting senator in fourteen years to lose his own primary outright, a defeat widely read as payback for his 2021 vote to convict Trump at his second impeachment trial. Representative Julia Letlow won the runoff and enters the general as a heavy favorite. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Letlow.

**Mississippi** — Cindy Hyde-Smith faces Democrat Scott Colom, a credible district attorney, but Mississippi hasn't elected a Democratic senator since 1982. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Hyde-Smith.

**Oklahoma** — Markwayne Mullin resigned to join the Trump cabinet; Governor Kevin Stitt appointed Williams Companies CEO Alan Armstrong as a placeholder who isn't running for the full term. Representative Kevin Hern is the Republican nominee and heavy favorite. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Hern.

**South Dakota** — Mike Rounds faces a fractured opposition split between a Democrat and a 2022 independent nominee. **Solid/Safe Republican**; projected winner: Rounds.

**Tennessee** — Bill Hagerty is unchallenged in his own primary and faces a fragmented Democratic field led by 2020 nominee Marquita Bradshaw. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Hagerty.

**West Virginia** — Shelley Moore Capito, seeking a third term in a state Trump carried by roughly 40 points, faces Democrat Rachel Fetty Anderson, who emerged from a contested primary but starts far behind. **Solid Republican**; projected winner: Capito.

**Wyoming** — Cynthia Lummis's retirement drew brief national attention, but Representative Harriet Hageman — endorsed by Trump, Senator John Barrasso, and Lummis herself — makes this the most lopsided open seat of the cycle in the reddest state in the country. **Solid/Safe Republican**; projected winner: Hageman.

---

## Bottom Line

Strip away the drama — the death, the primary upset, the scandal-forced substitution, the surprise retirements — and the map still comes down to five races: Alaska, Maine, Michigan, Ohio, and Texas. Democrats need all four of the traditional battlegrounds plus a genuine reach into Texas, or some combination that adds up to a net of four, to retake the majority. Republicans need only hold serve in two or three of those five to keep control.

The honest answer is that nobody knows yet. What's notable about this cycle isn't any single race — it's how many races that were not supposed to be competitive have become so anyway: Iowa and Nebraska because of unexpectedly strong Democratic and independent challengers, Montana because an incumbent gamed his own filing deadline, South Carolina because of a tragedy nobody could have modeled, and Louisiana and Texas because state Republican parties chose loyalty to Trump over electability. Whatever the outcome in November, 2026 will be remembered as a cycle where the fundamentals mattered less than usual — and where a remarkable number of safe seats stopped being safe.
