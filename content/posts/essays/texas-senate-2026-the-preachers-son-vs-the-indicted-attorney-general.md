# Texas Senate 2026: The Preacher's Son vs. The Indicted Attorney General

**Question:** How did a race in Texas — a state that hasn't elected a Democratic senator since 1988 — become a genuine toss-up?

**Answer:** Because the Republican Party, in its wisdom, nominated a man who has been impeached, indicted, and investigated more times than some organized crime figures, and because the Democratic Party, for once, found a candidate who can talk about Jesus and insulin prices in the same breath without sounding like he's focus-grouping his soul.

---

## I. The Contenders

### James Talarico (D)

James Dell Talarico is 37 years old, a former middle school English teacher from San Antonio, a current member of the Texas House of Representatives, and a Presbyterian seminarian pursuing a Master of Divinity at Austin Presbyterian Theological Seminary.[^1] He was born in Round Rock, Texas, in 1989, graduated from UT Austin with a degree in government, earned a Master's in Education Policy from Harvard, and then did what very few Harvard graduates do: he went back to Texas to teach sixth-grade language arts at Rhodes Middle School in one of San Antonio's poorest zip codes.[^2]

In 2018, at age 29, he flipped a Republican-held state House district — one Donald Trump had carried by 9 points just two years earlier — and became the youngest member of the Texas Legislature.[^3] In a Republican-majority chamber, he passed 16 bills, eight focused on education, childcare, or youth workforce development. He authored legislation to cap insulin costs, led the fight against school vouchers, and was one of the Democrats who broke quorum in 2021 to protest Republican voting restrictions, fleeing to Washington, D.C., and drawing the ire of Attorney General Ken Paxton, who sued to have the Democrats' seats declared vacant.[^4]

Talarico is the only member of the Texas Legislature who has never taken corporate PAC money.[^5] He has described his political philosophy in explicitly Christian terms — "Jesus is the reason I'm in politics" — and delivered a widely circulated sermon against Christian nationalism in 2023.[^6] He is pro-choice, supports LGBTQ+ rights, backs cannabis legalization, and takes a critical view of Israel's conduct in Gaza — positions that have drawn criticism from some Texas Jewish voters but that he has defended as consistent with his faith.[^7]

He won the Democratic Senate primary on March 3, 2026, defeating U.S. Representative Jasmine Crockett and Ahmad Hassan, a lesser-known candidate.[^8] He has raised $40.3 million as of July 2026, with a donor base that has gone national.[^9]

### Ken Paxton (R)

Warren Kenneth Paxton Jr. is 63 years old, a lawyer who has spent his entire adult life in Texas politics, and the current Attorney General of Texas. He was born in Minot, North Dakota, in 1962, earned a B.A. and J.D. from Baylor University, served in the Texas House from 2003 to 2013, the Texas Senate from 2013 to 2015, and has been Attorney General since January 2015.[^10]

Paxton's tenure as Attorney General has been defined by two things: aggressive litigation against Democratic administrations and an endless cascade of personal legal troubles.

In May 2023, the Texas House of Representatives voted 121–23 to impeach Paxton on 16 articles, including allegations that he gave preferential treatment to a political donor who had bribed him, misapplied public resources, made false statements against whistleblowers, obstructed justice in the securities fraud trial against him, and made false statements regarding his financial interests.[^11] The Texas Senate acquitted him in September 2023 on largely party-line votes, and he resumed office.[^12]

The securities fraud indictment, dating to 2015, was resolved in March 2024, when Paxton agreed to community service and restitution and the charges were dropped.[^13] His wife, Angela Paxton, filed for divorce in July 2025, alleging adultery. His office entered a plea deal in May 2026 with a man charged with sexual abuse of a minor, drawing criticism from both parties.[^14]

Paxton has sued the Biden administration 106 times, opposed the bipartisan gun safety bill after the Uvalde massacre, and led efforts to block emergency abortions for women facing medical crises.[^15] He defeated incumbent Senator John Cornyn in the Republican primary runoff on May 26, 2026, running to Cornyn's right and casting the senior senator as insufficiently loyal to Donald Trump.[^16] He has raised $7.6 million as of July 2026 — roughly one-fifth of Talarico's total.[^17]

## II. The Contrast: Two Versions of Texas

### The Biographical Contrast

Talarico's origin story is almost comically on-the-nose for a Democratic candidate in 2026: a preacher's daughter from Laredo who fled an abusive marriage, packed her infant son into a Ford Escort, and worked her way up from a hotel room to a small apartment in East Austin.[^18] Talarico teaches in a poor neighborhood, attends seminary, and quotes the Sermon on the Mount on the campaign trail.

Paxton's origin story is more conventional for a Texas Republican: lawyer, legislator, statewide official. But it is shadowed by a decade of scandal — an indictment that predates his current office, an impeachment that shook the Texas Capitol, a divorce that aired marital infidelity, and a plea deal that protected an alleged child abuser.

### The Philosophical Contrast

Talarico is a progressive Christian who frames policy through the lens of Matthew 25 — "whatever you did for one of the least of these, you did for me." He supports abortion rights, LGBTQ+ equality, and immigration reform, arguing that these positions are not in tension with his faith but demanded by it. He has called Christian nationalism "a heresy" and warned that "when faith becomes a weapon, it ceases to be faith at all."[^19]

Paxton is a Christian nationalist in practice if not in name. He has used the power of the Attorney General's office to advance religious conservative causes: suing to block federal protections for transgender students, opposing the Biden administration's efforts to protect emergency abortion care, and aligning himself with the most aggressive wings of the Texas Republican Party. He does not quote scripture on the campaign trail. He files lawsuits.

### The Generational Contrast

Talarico is 37. He was born the year the Berlin Wall fell, grew up during 9/11 and the Iraq War, came of age during the Great Recession, and entered politics during the Trump era. His frame of reference is different from Paxton's in ways that matter: he understands social media natively, speaks to young voters in their own vernacular, and has built a national following through podcasts (including a widely viewed appearance on *The Joe Rogan Experience*) and viral moments (including a Stephen Colbert interview that CBS scrapped under pressure from the Trump FCC and that subsequently raised $2.5 million in 24 hours when posted to YouTube).[^20]

Paxton is 63. His political career began before Talarico was born. He speaks the language of litigation and conservative talk radio. He has built his following through Fox News appearances and direct-mail appeals to the Republican base. He is not a viral candidate. He is a known quantity — and for many Texans, that quantity is negative.

## III. The Race: Why It's Competitive

Texas hasn't elected a Democratic senator since Lloyd Bentsen in 1988. The state has trended slightly bluer in recent cycles — Beto O'Rourke came within 2.6 points of Ted Cruz in 2018, and Joe Biden lost Texas by only 5.6 points in 2020 — but it remains a Republican stronghold at the statewide level.[^21] So why is this race a toss-up?

**First: Paxton's negatives are astronomical.** The July 2026 Texas Public Opinion Research poll found Talarico with a +12 net favorability rating and Paxton with a -13 rating — the worst of any figure tested.[^22] Nearly every Texas voter knows who Paxton is, and a significant portion of them do not like what they see. The impeachment, the indictment, the divorce, the plea deal — these are not obscure scandals. They are the defining features of Paxton's public persona.

**Second: Talarico is winning the economic argument.** In a state where 44% of voters rank affordability as their top issue, Talarico leads Paxton by 7 points on who "better understands the economic challenges facing working Texans" and by 9 points on who is more trusted to "lower costs and improve Texans' financial situations."[^23] Talarico's insulin cap legislation, his opposition to school vouchers, and his relentless focus on housing and childcare costs have given him credibility on the issue that usually saves Republicans in Texas.

**Third: The coalition is shifting.** Talarico leads among Black voters 71% to 8% and among Latino voters 66% to 20%.[^24] He is winning 7% of self-described Republicans and dominating among independents (43% to 23%). He even leads among voters without a college degree — a demographic that has been Republicans' firewall in Texas.[^25]

**Fourth: Money.** Talarico has raised $40.3 million to Paxton's $7.6 million.[^26] In a state as large as Texas, with its expensive media markets, that disparity matters. Talarico can afford to define himself before Paxton can define him — and, more importantly, he can afford to remind voters of Paxton's scandals.

---

## IV. The Stakes

This race is about more than one Senate seat. If Talarico wins, he becomes the first Democratic senator from Texas in 38 years, the youngest member of the Senate, and a national figure overnight. He also gives Democrats a plausible path to a Senate majority that does not require winning Maine or North Carolina — a path that runs through the Sun Belt rather than the Rust Belt.

If Paxton wins, he becomes one of the most conservative members of the Senate, a reliable vote for Trump-aligned priorities, and a case study in Republican voter tolerance for scandal. He also validates the Texas Republican Party's decision to primary John Cornyn — a decision that looked risky in May 2026 and could look catastrophic in November if Talarico prevails.

---

## V. Bottom Line

The 2026 Texas Senate race is a collision between two models of politics: the seminarian who believes that faith demands justice for the poor, and the litigator who believes that power is its own justification. It is a race between a man whose worst scandal is that he once said something "cringey" about gender and religion — and a man whose career is a catalogue of indictments, impeachments, and investigations.

Texas Republicans had a choice. They could have renominated John Cornyn, a conventional conservative with a conventional conservative's baggage. Instead, they chose Ken Paxton — a man who has spent a decade proving that the rule of law is for other people. Whether that choice costs them a Senate seat in one of the reddest states in the union is the question the next hundred days will answer.
