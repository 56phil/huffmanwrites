A tribune of the praetorian guard arrived at a country house four miles from Rome in the evening, surrounded the building with soldiers, and told the man inside that the emperor required his death. The man was at dinner with his wife and two friends. He did not argue, and he did not run. He asked for tablets so that he could write his will, and when the officer refused him even that, he turned to his friends and told them he had nothing left to leave them except the pattern of his life. ([Tacitus, *Annals* 15.61–62](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Annals_(Tacitus)/Book_15), trans. Church and Brodribb, 1876)

That man was Lucius Annaeus Seneca, sixty-something years old, the most famous writer in the empire and a former tutor to the emperor who had just ordered him killed. What follows is how he got there, and why the letters he wrote in his last three years still read like they were posted last week.

## A family of words

Seneca was born at Corduba, in southern Spain, in the last years before the Christian era begins. The sources disagree about the exact year, placing it between four and one BCE, and the disagreement is a useful reminder that even the best-documented private life of the early empire is full of gaps. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/))

His family was prosperous, Italian in origin, and entirely devoted to language. His father, Seneca the Elder, was a celebrated teacher of rhetoric who wrote a surviving collection of declamations, the practice speeches that Roman boys drilled as a path to public life. His mother, Helvia, outlived him and received from her exiled son one of the most remarkable letters of consolation in Latin. Seneca had two brothers, and the household in Rome was the kind that measured a boy by how well he could argue. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/))

He learned both halves of what the city had to teach: rhetoric, which was the road to a career, and philosophy, which was a way of living that he came to treat as the more serious subject. The philosophy stuck to him the way a trade sticks to a man who has chosen it rather than inherited it.

## The breath

The most concrete fact about Seneca's body is that he could not reliably breathe.

In one of the letters he describes the condition with a physician's plainness and a patient's dread. He calls it by its Greek name only because Latin lacked a good one, and then says what it is: shortness of breath. The attacks, he writes, come like a squall and end within the hour, and what makes them terrible is that they never quite finish:

> I have passed through all the ills and dangers of the flesh; but nothing seems to me more troublesome than this.

Seneca, [*Moral Letters to Lucilius* 54](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_54) (trans. Richard Mott Gummere, 1917)

Modern readers call it asthma. He lived with it for decades, in a period with no inhalers, no steroids, and no explanation of what was happening inside his chest. The illness shaped his life in practical ways. He travelled to Egypt in his twenties to stay with an aunt, partly for his health, and did not return to Rome until 31. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/))

It also shaped his philosophy, though not in the way a reader might expect. Seneca did not write a theory of illness. He wrote about what a man does in the hour when his body refuses to obey him. His answer was to practise the argument in advance, so that when the body failed, the mind had something to say:

> Death is non-existence, and I know already what that means.

Seneca, [*Moral Letters to Lucilius* 54](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_54) (trans. Richard Mott Gummere, 1917)

This is the Stoic method at its most concrete. The Stoics held that only virtue is good and that health, like wealth, is a preferred indifferent: worth having, not worth a life built around. A man who has spent forty years struggling for air has an unusually practical reason to test that claim.

## The career, and the sea crossing

Back in Rome, Seneca became a successful advocate and then a quaestor, and his speaking drew the kind of attention that is dangerous under a suspicious emperor. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/)) The charge against him, in 41, was adultery with a sister of the emperor Caligula. He was convicted in the Senate and sent to Corsica.

Exile in the ancient world was not a holiday. It was the removal of a citizen from the city that made him a citizen, and it could last forever. Seneca spent eight years on the island. He kept writing, and the works from those years show a man working out what to do with a life that has been taken away from him. The *Consolation to Helvia* is addressed to his mother and argues, with real force, that the loss of country is not the loss of everything. The *Consolation to Polybius* is addressed to a powerful freedman of the emperor Claudius, and it is a much weaker performance: flattery dressed as philosophy, written by a man who wanted to come home. Both letters are Seneca, and the difference between them is the honest record of what exile does to a person.

He was recalled in 49, and the reason was political. Agrippina, the emperor's niece and the mother of a twelve-year-old boy named Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus, wanted a tutor for her son, and Seneca was famous, available, and likely to be grateful. He was made praetor, married Pompeia Paulina, and took up the education of the future Nero. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/))

## The tutor

Claudius died in 54, and Nero took the throne at seventeen. Seneca and the praetorian prefect Sextus Afranius Burrus became, in effect, the government. The early years of the reign are the ones historians treat most kindly: the young emperor was managed, the Senate was consulted, and Seneca wrote the speeches. He also wrote *On Mercy*, an essay addressed directly to Nero in 55 or 56 which argues that a ruler's power is most secure when it is restrained. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)) It is a strange document to read now, knowing the end of the story, because it is sincere.

The arrangement held for about five years. Then Nero had his mother killed, and Seneca, who had by then spent a decade inside the palace, wrote the letter to the Senate that defended the murder. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/)) Whatever he believed privately, he put his prose at the service of the killing, and Romans noticed. Burrus died in 62, probably poisoned, and with him went the last brake on the emperor. Seneca asked permission to retire, was refused at first, and eventually withdrew from public life. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/))

The three years that followed are the ones that matter most. He was in his sixties, out of power, and free.

## The letters

In retirement Seneca wrote to a friend named Lucilius, a procurator in Sicily, and the result is 124 letters in twenty books that are among the strangest and most useful documents to survive from antiquity. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/)) They are not a treatise. They are a correspondence that reads as if written one day at a time, which is exactly the point: philosophy as a daily practice rather than a system delivered once and filed.

The first letter is about time, and it opens with an instruction that could be posted above a modern desk:

> gather and save your time, which till lately has been forced from you, or filched away, or has merely slipped from your hands

Seneca, [*Moral Letters to Lucilius* 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_1) (trans. Richard Mott Gummere, 1917)

The Stoics had a word for the failure this describes. It is not laziness; it is the ordinary leakage of attention, the way a life is spent without any single decision to spend it. Seneca returns to the theme constantly because he does not believe the reader has absorbed it. A man in his sixties writing to a friend in middle age has a particular authority on the subject, and he knows it.

He writes about old age with the same unsentimental eye:

> Wherever I turn, I see evidences of my advancing years.

Seneca, [*Moral Letters to Lucilius* 12](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_12) (trans. Richard Mott Gummere, 1917)

The context is a visit to a country house that is falling down, and the letter turns the crumbling plaster into a lesson about his own body. What follows is not despair. It is the argument that a life is measured by how well it is used rather than by how long it lasts.

Then there is the letter that ought to be read aloud in every generation that congratulates itself on its moral progress. Seneca is writing about the treatment of slaves, and he takes the standard defence of his own class apart in stages. “They are slaves,” people declare. His answer runs into four words:

> Nay, rather they are men.

Seneca, [*Moral Letters to Lucilius* 47](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_47) (trans. Richard Mott Gummere, 1917)

He goes on to say that a slave is a fellow slave, that Fortune has the same rights over everyone, and that a man who thinks it degrading to dine with his own household has misunderstood what dignity is. Rome did not abolish slavery for another four centuries, and Seneca did not propose its abolition. What he did was strip the institution of its moral excuse for a reader who had never thought to ask.

And there is the letter where the whole enterprise is stated plainly. Written to Lucilius on the death of a mutual acquaintance, it refuses to dress the fact up:

> Every day and every hour reveal to us what a nothing we are

Seneca, [*Moral Letters to Lucilius* 101](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_101) (trans. Richard Mott Gummere, 1925)

Seneca's own answer to that sentence was not gloom. It was arithmetic. If the day is not guaranteed, then the day is the unit of account, and the only sane response is to use the one in front of you.

He also wrote about the physical world in these years. The *Natural Questions* works through clouds, rain, lightning, wind, comets, earthquakes, the waters of the earth, and the flooding of the Nile, and it is the least read of his major works, which is a pity. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)) It is a book by a man who wanted to understand what the sky was doing, in an age with no instruments to tell him, and some of its questions are still the right ones even where the answers are wrong.

## The death

In 65, a conspiracy against Nero was uncovered. Its leader was Gaius Calpurnius Piso, and the plot was wide enough that the interrogations produced names faster than the emperor could act on them. Seneca was named, probably because a man of his reputation was an obvious figurehead for anyone imagining a different government, and possibly with more evidence than that. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/); [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Seneca/))

Nero sent the order. What followed is one of the most detailed death scenes in ancient literature, and Tacitus reports it with the precision of a medical observer, because the details are medical:

> Seneca, as his aged frame, attenuated by frugal diet, allowed the blood to escape but slowly, severed also the veins of his legs and knees.

Tacitus, [*Annals* 15.63](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Annals_(Tacitus)/Book_15) (trans. Church and Brodribb, 1876)

That sentence contains a physiological fact that deserves a pause. Seneca was old, thin, and had spent decades eating sparingly; his blood pressure and blood volume were low, so a cut artery in the arm drained him too slowly to kill him quickly. He cut the veins behind his knees to speed it up, and it still was not enough. He dictated to his secretaries while he bled, and Tacitus says the words were published and so need no paraphrase.

His wife, Pompeia Paulina, opened her own veins at the same moment. Nero, not wishing to add a second killing to the story, ordered her saved; the slaves bound her arms, and she lived a few more years with the pallor of a woman who had lost a great deal of blood. Seneca, still dying, asked a friend with medical skill for the poison that Athens used on condemned men, drank it, and it had no effect: his limbs were already cold, and cold tissue absorbs nothing. He was carried into a bath of hot water and died of the steam. ([Tacitus, *Annals* 15.63–64](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Annals_(Tacitus)/Book_15), trans. Church and Brodribb, 1876)

The body that had given him trouble since childhood took almost as long to die as the philosophy had taken to build. He was cremated without funeral rites, as he had instructed in a codicil to his will.

## The honest limits

There are two charges against Seneca that will not go away, and both have something in them.

The first is hypocrisy. He preached detachment from wealth while being one of the richest men in Rome, and he lent money to provincials at interest while writing that money is an indifferent. His answer, in *On the Happy Life*, is that the wise man owns wealth without being owned by it, which is a real distinction and also the defence that every comfortable person uses. A reader should hold both facts at once: the distinction is genuine, and the man making it was living in a palace.

The second is complicity. He served Nero for more than a decade, wrote the speech that excused the murder of the emperor's mother, and was still close enough to power in 65 for the conspirators to want his name. The Stoic claim is that a good man can work inside a bad system and reduce the harm. Whether Seneca did, or merely enjoyed the benefits, is a question his own letters cannot settle.

What the letters can settle is smaller and more useful. They are not a memoir, and the Stanford Encyclopedia is careful to say so: Seneca constructs a literary persona and invites readers to examine their own lives rather than his. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)) He is not confessing. He is demonstrating a method, in public, on a friend, and the method is the same one he used on himself in an asthma attack.

## How to read him now

Start with the letters, not the essays. The essays are polished performances for a Roman audience; the letters are working notes, and their roughness is the reason they still land. Richard Mott Gummere's Loeb translation, free on Wikisource, is the one quoted here. It keeps the compressed, slightly impatient rhythm of the Latin, and it is archaic enough to remind you that you are reading a man from another century.

Read one letter a day rather than forty in a sitting. The letters were written to be read that way, one arrival at a time, and the effect accumulates in a manner a binge destroys. Begin with the first book, which is about time, and stay with it even when the subject seems obvious. The obvious subjects are where Seneca does his best work, because he refuses to let them stay obvious.

And keep the body in view. The most underrated fact about Seneca is that his philosophy came out of a chest that would not open properly, in a city where a cough could kill you and a rival could have you exiled for a sentence. He was not writing from a garden about how pleasant moderation is. He was writing from the middle of a life that had already taken away his country, his health, and eventually his voice in public affairs, and he kept asking the same question to the end: what is still yours?

The answer he gave, in a letter written within three years of his death, is the one worth carrying out of all this. Not much is yours. Your judgment is, and the hour in front of you is, and the way you treat the people who cannot hurt you. Everything else is water running through a marble clock.
