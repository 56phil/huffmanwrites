For much of the post–Cold War era, the Arctic was held up as the one place great-power rivals still cooperated — search and rescue, fisheries, scientific monitoring, the kind of work that doesn't function unless everyone at the table shows up. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine broke that. In March 2022, the seven other Arctic Council members — the U.S., Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, and Sweden — suspended their participation rather than sit across from Moscow.[^1] Russia has spent the years since trying to build a substitute reality: hosting its own forums, leaning harder on a partnership with China, and treating Arctic diplomacy less as shared stewardship than as a stage for proving it isn't isolated.

The Arctic Council itself never fully reopened. Working-level technical projects resumed in 2023 with Russia's muted participation, but a third of the council's roughly 130 projects stalled, and new work simply stopped getting approved.[^2] In February 2024, Russia announced it was halting its annual dues to the council altogether — not a formal withdrawal, which Moscow has consistently ruled out, but a pointed signal that it no longer sees much value in an institution it can't fully use.[^3] In the meantime, Russia has kept its own venue running: the biennial "Arctic: Territory of Dialogue" forum, most recently held in Murmansk in March 2025 under the banner "To Live in the North," with the next edition scheduled for St. Petersburg in 2026.[^4] It's a real conference with real attendees. It is not a substitute for the Arctic Council, and Moscow knows it — which is precisely why the forum's messaging leans so heavily on framing Russia as a "responsible Arctic power" continuing to develop the region on its own terms, whether or not the rest of the Council agrees.

The more consequential pivot has been toward China, and this is where the official narrative — Russia and China as a clean, complementary Arctic partnership — runs into the actual numbers. Beijing, which has no Arctic territory of its own, has spent nearly a decade describing itself as a "near-Arctic state" with legitimate interests in the region's shipping lanes and resources.[^5] China has bought real volumes of sanctioned Russian Arctic LNG, including cargoes from the Arctic LNG 2 project that Western sanctions were specifically designed to strangle.[^6] But the same reporting that documents those purchases also shows China quietly declining to sell Russia the one thing its Arctic ambitions actually depend on: the propulsion and ice-class hull technology needed to build the tanker fleet that would make the Northern Sea Route commercially viable at scale. Russia's own Central Marine Research and Design Institute has acknowledged that Chinese equipment for large ice-class vessels isn't coming "unless pressure from sanctions eases."[^7] The practical result: Northern Sea Route cargo volumes, which Vladimir Putin once targeted at 80 million tons by 2024, actually fell to 37.02 million tons in 2025 — down from the year before — with nearly a third of the vessels using the route now flagged as part of a sanctions-evading "shadow fleet."[^8] China is happy to buy discounted Russian gas. It is considerably less willing to hand Moscow the industrial base for a shipping route that would compete with China's own maritime interests. That is not a partnership of equals; it's a transaction, priced accordingly.

Russia's other channel back into multilateral Arctic cooperation, the Arctic Coast Guard Forum, tells a similarly qualified story. Russia remains a formal member, but the forum's multilateral exercises involving Russian participation have been on hold since 2022, same as the Council's technical work.[^9] What has continued is narrower and more local: bilateral search-and-rescue and oil-spill-response cooperation between Norway and Russia in the Barents Sea, sustained at an operational level even as the larger multilateral architecture stays frozen.[^10] That's a genuine, if modest, channel — not evidence that Russia has kept "one foot in cooperative institutions," but evidence that a single neighboring country has judged narrow practical cooperation worth preserving despite everything else.

The symbolism, meanwhile, hasn't changed at all, because it was never really about institutions. In August 2007, the Arktika 2007 expedition sent two manned submersibles, Mir-1 and Mir-2, more than four thousand meters down to the seabed beneath the North Pole and planted a titanium Russian flag there, alongside soil samples meant to support Russia's claim to an extended continental shelf under the U.N. Convention on the Law of the Sea.[^11] Canada's foreign minister at the time dismissed it as a stunt from another century — "you can't go around the world and just plant flags" — and legally, he was right: the flag changed nothing. But the gesture wasn't really a legal argument. It was a rehearsal for exactly the posture Russia has taken ever since: symbolic assertion first, institutional legitimacy claimed after the fact, and continued UNCLOS shelf submissions used less to win a specific ruling than to keep restating, indefinitely, that Russia intends to be treated as the Arctic's largest and most permanent power.[^12]

That's the accurate version of Russia's Arctic gambit, and it's a smaller story than the official one. Moscow has not built a workable alternative to the institution it lost. It has built a stage, a genuinely useful but narrowly transactional relationship with a partner who won't hand over the technology that would matter most, and a set of symbols that assert far more than the underlying position can currently support. The Arctic hasn't become the theater of managed confrontation Russian messaging likes to describe. It's become a place where Russia is discovering, slowly and at real cost, how much of its former standing there depended on cooperation it can no longer buy back on its own terms.
