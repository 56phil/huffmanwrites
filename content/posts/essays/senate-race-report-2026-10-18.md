**Question:** Sixteen days before Election Day, Democratic Senate candidates raised four times what Republicans raised, nine of the week's eleven rating changes moved their way, and the president's approval fell to a record low, so why does control of the Senate still come down to five states that could go either way?

**Answer:** Because a lead is not a majority, and this week made the difference between them visible. The third-quarter filings, due October 15, showed Democratic candidates raising $253 million to the Republicans' $56 million across the fifteen races NBC News rates as competitive, and winning the money race in fourteen of them.[^1] Sabato's Crystal Ball moved five Senate ratings, four of them toward the Democrats, and its tally now stands at 49 seats at least leaning Democratic, 46 at least leaning Republican, and five toss-ups: Kansas, Iowa, Maine, Ohio and Texas.[^2] That arithmetic is the week's most important number. A week ago Republicans needed three of four toss-ups to hold a 50-50 chamber with the vice president's tiebreaker, and Democrats needed two of four. This week Republicans need four of five, and Democrats still need two.[^2] The path got cheaper for one party and narrower for the other in the same seven days.

It is still a path, not a result, and the markets priced it that way. Kalshi's own desk had Democratic control at 62 percent on October 15, up a point from October 9, while pricing Kansas at 65 percent for Roger Marshall, Iowa at 58 percent for Ashley Hinson and Nebraska at 75 percent for Pete Ricketts.[^3] The exchanges are not disputing the forecasters about the direction of the country. They are pricing the specific Republican-held states where a good Democratic year has not yet become a Democratic win.

---

## What Changed This Week

**The money arrived, and it arrived on one side.** The third-quarter reports filed this week put Democratic Senate candidates at $253 million raised from July through September against $56 million for Republicans, with Democrats holding $87 million in cash to the Republicans' $56 million.[^1] The advantage ran through the map rather than around it: James Talarico raised $60.2 million in Texas against Ken Paxton's $10.2 million, Jon Ossoff raised $33.1 million in Georgia against Mike Collins's $2.8 million, Adam Hamilton raised $9.3 million in Kansas, about $8.5 million more than Roger Marshall, and Josh Turek raised $13.9 million in Iowa against Ashley Hinson's $3.3 million.[^1][^4][^8] Minnesota was the lone competitive race in which the Republican outraised the Democrat.[^1] The caveat is cash rather than receipts, and it is real: Hinson ended the quarter with nearly $7 million banked against Turek's $3.6 million, because Turek spent nearly everything he raised.[^8]

**The raters moved again, and for the first time in weeks two of them moved toward the Republicans.** Inside Elections revised five Senate ratings on October 15, every one toward the Democrats: Florida from solid to likely Republican, Texas from tilt Republican to toss-up, South Carolina from likely to lean Republican, New Hampshire from toss-up to tilt Democratic, and Kansas from likely to tilt Republican.[^41] Sabato's Crystal Ball moved five: Kansas from leans Republican to toss-up, Mississippi from safe to likely Republican, Nebraska from likely to leans Republican, New Hampshire from leans to likely Democratic, and Minnesota from likely to leans Democratic.[^2] RealClearPolitics moved Nebraska from toss-up to lean Republican in its October 12 update.[^41] Counted across the three, that is eleven changes, nine toward the Democrats and two toward the Republicans. Cook, Decision Desk HQ and Silver did not change a Senate rating this week.[^41]

Sabato's reasoning on Kansas was blunt: "a full-on Republican rescue operation appears underway for Marshall."[^2] On New Hampshire it wrote that "it would be a big surprise to us at this point if Pappas lost," and on Minnesota it said that while "Democrats remain favorites to hold this seat," the analysts had "nonetheless felt underwhelmed by the numbers Flanagan has posted."[^2] The Minnesota move is the one to keep an eye on, because it is the first time this month a forecaster has marked a Democratic-held seat down while the national environment was improving for the party.

**Kansas had the week that a margin seat has.** Hamilton reported raising $9.3 million for the quarter, about $8.5 million more than Marshall, while the Senate Leadership Fund has made an eight-figure investment in defending the seat and WinSenate, a super PAC affiliated with Senate Majority PAC, booked $3.76 million in broadcast advertising as part of a promised $5 million.[^2][^4][^7] Hamilton also called for a Senate ethics inquiry into reporting, published by Kansas Reflector and MS NOW, that Marshall and his chief of staff used congressional staff and taxpayer resources for campaign travel in 2020; the reporting quotes chief of staff Brent Robertson saying, "Primary purpose is what we say it is."[^5] Marshall's staff did not respond to renewed questions.[^5] Former governor Sam Brownback endorsed Marshall, while six of the eight living Kansas governors back Hamilton.[^43] The race is now a toss-up in Sabato's column and a tilt Republican in Inside Elections', and Kalshi still prices Marshall at 65 percent.[^2][^3]

**Three debates and an empty chair.** Alaska's final debate turned personal, with Dan Sullivan saying, "Twenty-five staffers quit on Mary Peltola because she abused them physically and emotionally," and Peltola answering, "I don't think this election is about me on my worst day."[^10] Maine's fourth debate was interrupted by a protester shouting about data centers; Collins called him "obviously one of Troy's supporters," and Jackson replied, "Oh, shut up. I don't even know that kid."[^12] Minnesota's only debate put the state's fraud scandals, the Iran war and Trump's endorsement between Peggy Flanagan and Michele Tafoya.[^13] Texas had no debate at all: Talarico took questions alone at a CNN and Jubilee town hall while Paxton stayed away, and the moderator noted that "He has yet to respond, but that invitation still stands."[^14]

**The national environment got worse for the party in power.** The ABC News and Washington Post poll with Ipsos, in the field October 9 to 13, put the president's approval at 33 percent against 66 percent disapproval, a record low, and had Democrats ahead on the generic ballot 52 to 44 among likely voters.[^18] The Reuters and Ipsos large-sample survey, in the field September 30 to October 5, put cost of living first among the issues at 52 percent and the president at 32 percent approval against 65 percent disapproval.[^19] Oil finished the week at $109.86 a barrel for Brent and $101.41 for West Texas Intermediate, with diesel rising faster than gasoline because of the war and strikes on Russian refineries.[^20] Every one of those numbers is a headwind for the incumbent party, and none of them decides a Senate race by itself.

**New Hampshire picked up a Republican endorsement for its Democrat.** Don Bolduc, the Republican nominee for Senate in 2022, endorsed Chris Pappas on October 15 and joined a "Country Before Party" committee of Republicans and independents backing him, saying Pappas had shown "unwavering dedication to our veterans and his efforts to foster bipartisan cooperation."[^22] The spending in the state still favors the Republicans, $41 million to $31 million, and John Sununu is running on the family name rather than on the president: "Lots of people would like to make this about the president," he said at an AARP forum. "The president's going to be out of office in two years. This is about what's next."[^23]

**North Carolina stopped pretending to be close.** A WRAL and WSOC poll conducted by SurveyUSA, in the field October 7 to 11, put Roy Cooper ahead of Michael Whatley 54 to 40 among likely voters, with the president's approval in the state at 37 percent against 60 percent.[^21] Whatley has halted television advertising, and a major national conservative group has withdrawn millions from the state.[^21] Catawba College political scientist Michael Bitzer supplied the week's cleanest summary: "The common expectation that a lot of us had was this lead by Cooper would shrink."[^21]

## The Defense

Michigan remains the most competitive Democratic seat in the country, and the polling has settled into a narrow, stable lead. Six polls from late September and early October put Abdul El-Sayed ahead of Mike Rogers by between one and seven points; InsiderAdvantage had it 48 to 46, YouGov 49 to 48 and Wedgewood 53 to 47.[^35] Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and JD Vance both campaigned in Michigan this week, she in Iron Mountain and Marquette, he in Monroe, which is what a race both parties believe they can win looks like in its final fortnight.[^35] Kalshi prices El-Sayed at 68 percent.[^3]

Georgia is no longer being treated as a contest, but it produced the week's sharpest debate. Ossoff said, "I have zero interest in running for president," and "I'm not running for president. I'm running for re-election to the Senate," then asked Collins whether he considered himself a white nationalist; Collins answered, "I condemn any sort of hate speech," and later, "I'm a little tired of this, Jon."[^26] Collins again declined to say that Joe Biden won in 2020, offering instead, "You know, Joe Biden went into office, and boy, Georgians paid for it."[^26] The president campaigned for Collins in Dalton on October 16, calling Ossoff a "weak, arrogant, hate-filled, radical-left senator" and repeating that "I won the election in Georgia," while Ossoff answered that he was "surprised Georgia Republicans want the president here."[^45] Ossoff leads the polling average by nine points, 52.5 to 43.2, and the money gap is wider still.[^44][^1] Kalshi prices Ossoff at 95 percent.[^3]

Minnesota is the defense seat that will not settle, and this week it moved in the wrong direction for the Democrats. Rasmussen, in the field October 5 to 7 with 856 likely voters, had Flanagan ahead 48 to 45, and Sabato moved the race from likely to leans Democratic, citing its own doubt about the size of the lead.[^40][^2] The only debate, on October 16, was Tafoya's best hour of the campaign: she accused Flanagan of changing positions "when it becomes politically expedient," while Flanagan called her a potential "rubber stamp" and said, "Donald Trump doesn't just endorse anybody."[^13] Tafoya also called for the war to end, saying, "The prices are too high. This war does need to end, and it needs to end quickly," a position she did not hold when the war began.[^13] Kalshi prices Flanagan at 87 percent, which is a comfortable number for a race that has stopped feeling comfortable.[^3]

New Hampshire is the seat that kept moving toward the Democrats and got closer anyway. Rasmussen, in the field October 2 to 4, had Pappas ahead 49 to 44, down from an eleven-point lead in early September.[^24] The Bolduc endorsement is the week's meaningful development because it gives Pappas the permission structure to run as the bipartisan choice in a state where the Sununu name still carries weight.[^22] Inside Elections moved the race to tilt Democratic, Sabato to likely Democratic, and Kalshi prices Pappas at 89 percent.[^41][^2][^3]

The other nine Democratic seats, Colorado, Delaware, Illinois, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, Rhode Island and Virginia, are not in play.

## The Offense, Tiered

North Carolina remains the anchor of every path, and it is now a race that the Republican side has largely stopped funding. Cooper led 54 to 40 in the WRAL and WSOC survey, and Whatley has halted television advertising.[^21] A major national conservative group's retreat from the state is the tell: a party that believed it could hold North Carolina would not leave its nominee off the air with sixteen days to go.[^21] Kalshi prices Cooper at 95 percent.[^3]

Ohio and Texas are the two coin flips, and Ohio's late surprise is data centers. More than $14 million has been spent on data center advertising in the Brown and Husted race, six times as much as in any other Senate race, and an NRSC memo described the issue as an "anchor hanging around Husted's neck."[^27] Husted's answer is that his legislation would "force data centers to pay for their own power"; Brown's is that "It really is part of the rigged system."[^27] Brown leads the polling average by four points, 47.9 to 43.7, and Kalshi prices him at 60 percent.[^28][^3]

Texas is the race where the money has become the story. Elon Musk gave $158 million to the Senate Leadership Fund in September, and the fund transferred $158 million to Texas PAC, the super PAC supporting Paxton; Talarico told a San Marcos rally, "You are my slingshot," and "We are going up against our own Goliaths."[^16] More than $440 million has been spent between the parties, with Republicans at $323 million and Democrats at $116 million.[^36] The president preemptively assigned blame for a loss, writing that "If Ken Paxton loses, it will be because of WEAK and INEFFECTIVE Political Hacks like John Cornyn and Karl Rove."[^17] Paxton, asked why he keeps refusing debates, said the campaigns "can never quite work out the details because he always wants something we can't do"; Talarico replied, "Here are the details: literally any time, any place, any network."[^15] Talarico leads the polling average by three points, 48.5 to 45.5, and Kalshi prices him at 62 percent.[^29][^3]

Maine is a seat where the polling and the price sit close together, and the reason is a question about turnout. Suffolk University, in the field October 8 to 11 with 500 likely voters, had Jackson ahead 49 to 47, with 4 percent undecided, while Collins led independents 47 to 44.[^11] Suffolk's David Paleologos flagged the crossover that keeps the race close: about 12 percent of Maine voters who disapprove of Trump said they were voting for Collins, "a crossover that is unusual," he said, adding that he "would have expected 1% or 2% of those unhappy with Trump might be voting for Collins, not 12%."[^11] The fourth and final debate on October 15 was disrupted by a protester, and the exchange that followed was the least senatorial moment of the campaign.[^12] Kalshi prices Jackson at 57 percent, and the memory of 2020, when the polls underestimated Collins, is the reason the price is not higher.[^3][^12]

Alaska is the week's genuine toss-up, and the final debate showed why. Peltola said of the war, "There does not appear to be a peace deal on the horizon," while Sullivan said, "the largest state sponsor of terrorism should not have nuclear weapons," and then, "But it needs to end soon. The energy costs from this war are too high."[^10] Sullivan went after Peltola's lobbying and her staff turnover; Peltola answered that Sullivan had been in Washington twelve years and "has gotten richer every single one of those years."[^10] The last New York Times and Siena survey had Peltola ahead 50 to 43, and an Alaska Survey Research poll had her ahead 51 to 49 after ranked-choice allocation.[^37][^38] Two men named Dan Sullivan are on the ballot, the incumbent and a retired schoolteacher whose disqualification was overturned by the state Supreme Court, and the second Sullivan is the reason the ranked-choice count is not a formality.[^39] Kalshi prices the Democratic Party at 75 percent.[^3]

Iowa's polls split in both directions again, which is now the defining fact of the race. Marist, in the field October 8 to 11 with 1,100 registered voters, had Turek ahead 48 to 45 among likely voters, with independents breaking for him 51 to 31 and 59 percent of likely voters nonetheless expecting Hinson to win.[^25] Turek raised $13.9 million to Hinson's $3.3 million but spent nearly all of it, leaving him with $3.6 million against her $7 million, and outside spending in the race has passed $100 million.[^8] Cook rates it a toss-up, Inside Elections tilt Republican, and Kalshi prices Hinson at 58 percent, which is the market saying that the money is real, but her cash advantage and the state's Republican lean are more real.[^41][^3]

Nebraska remains the wildcard and the cheapest fourth seat on the board. YouGov, in the field September 24 to October 3 with 1,809 likely voters, had Osborn ahead 48 to 47, while a co/efficient survey had Ricketts ahead 48 to 43 and SurveyUSA had Osborn ahead 46 to 42; none of the three had a margin larger than five.[^6] Osborn continues to say, "I will not caucus with either party," and "I'll fight for Nebraskans, no matter what the party bosses say," and outside groups have spent $12.8 million supporting him through October 13, with the Senate Leadership Fund committing another $8 million for Ricketts.[^6] Sabato moved the race to leans Republican, which is the right read of a tied poll in a state the president carried by 20 points.[^2] Kalshi prices Ricketts at 75 percent.[^3]

Kansas is the margin seat, and it is now a two-party argument for the first time. Hamilton's $9.3 million quarter, the Senate Leadership Fund's eight-figure defense, WinSenate's $3.76 million booking and the ethics inquiry have turned a curiosity into a race for which both parties are paying.[^2][^4][^7][^5] The polling is close enough that Sabato moved it to toss-up and Inside Elections moved it only to tilt Republican, but Kalshi still prices Marshall at 65 percent.[^2][^3] The honest caution is unchanged: Kansas produced competitive September polls in 2014 and 2020 and elected Republicans by double digits in both Novembers, and a state that has not elected a Democratic senator since 1932 does not flip because one quarter's fundraising report was good.[^9][^4]

Florida is not a race again, but it is closer than a safe state should be. An MDW and EDGE Communications survey, in the field October 4 to 10 with 2,351 likely voters, put Ashley Moody ahead of Angie Nixon 47.6 to 42.4, with 10 percent undecided.[^30] That is a five-point race in a state the president carried comfortably, and it is the reason Inside Elections moved Florida from solid to likely Republican rather than leaving it in the safe column.[^41]

Louisiana and Mississippi remain long shots with real activity and no new polling. In Louisiana, the only two public surveys were paid for by Jamie Davis's campaign and showed Julia Letlow ahead by four in each, while Poliwave's average put her ahead 47.1 to 43.1 and its projection had her ahead by 14.6; Letlow dismissed the latest survey as "a bogus poll," and she has not agreed to the one televised debate Davis accepted.[^33] In Mississippi, Barack Obama narrated a campaign advertisement for Scott Colom, saying, "Mississippi, it's time for a change and that's why you need to send Scott Colom to the U.S. Senate," the first time Obama has endorsed a Mississippi Senate candidate since Mike Espy in 2018.[^32] Sabato moved Mississippi from safe to likely Republican, which is a warning rather than a prediction.[^2]

Montana and South Carolina are Republican-favored, with South Carolina the less comfortable of the two. Montana held its first all-candidate debate on October 11, with Kurt Alme, Seth Bodnar, Alani Bankhead and Kyle Austin on the same stage; Alme said, "I will never support a cut in benefits for seniors," and Bodnar dismissed the committee-assignment objection as "a red herring."[^34] Recent polling shows Alme with a comfortable lead in the four-way race, and the independent Bodnar is the reason the seat is not entirely safe.[^34] In South Carolina, Darline Graham and Annie Andrews met in the only scheduled debate on October 11, and a Trafalgar poll released that day had Graham ahead 49 to 45, narrower than the president's 18-point win in the state two years ago; Inside Elections responded by moving the race to lean Republican.[^31][^41]

The nine safe Republican seats, Alabama, Arkansas, Idaho, Kentucky, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Tennessee, West Virginia and Wyoming, are not in play.

## The Math

The forecasters and the markets are now telling nearly the same story, which is itself a change from last week. Sabato counts 49 seats at least leaning Democratic, 46 at least leaning Republican and five toss-ups, and the requirement that follows is stark: Republicans need four of the five toss-ups for a 50-50 chamber with the vice president's tiebreaker, while Democrats need two of them for 51 seats.[^2] Cook's most recent published assessment, on October 6, projected a Democratic gain of two to six seats, which is consistent with a map on which the Democrats are favorites in North Carolina, Ohio, Texas and Maine and competitive in Alaska, Iowa, Kansas and Nebraska.[^42] Kalshi prices Democratic control at 62 percent, up from 61 percent on October 9 and 53 percent on September 14, the first day the market moved ahead of the Republicans.[^3]

The paths have not changed in their order, only in their price. The market path is North Carolina, Ohio and Texas, plus one of Maine, Alaska, Iowa or Nebraska. The consensus path is North Carolina, Ohio, Maine and Alaska or Iowa, which requires winning the top four and assumes the defense holds everywhere. The nightmare is any loss among Michigan, Minnesota or New Hampshire, because every defensive loss raises the requirement to five flips, and five flips means winning Kansas or Nebraska or Florida on top of everything else.

The Kansas connection is the whole of the argument, and this week it became a two-party argument for the first time. A month ago Kansas was a curiosity, a week ago it earned a row in the table below, and this week Sabato moved it to toss-up, Inside Elections moved it to tilt Republican, Hamilton out-raised Marshall by $8.5 million, and the Republican Senate fund made an eight-figure investment in a state it used to take for granted.[^2][^41][^4] What has not happened is the other half: the Democratic Senate fund is now spending, but the market still prices Marshall at 65 percent.[^7][^3] If the Democrats sweep North Carolina, Ohio and Texas, Kansas is not needed. If any one of those slips, Kansas becomes the margin seat, and a margin seat that the market still prices Republican is a margin seat that stays where it is.

The columns are refreshed on different schedules and the differences are real, so the dates are given per column rather than averaged: Cook October 6; Inside Elections October 15; Sabato October 14; RealClearPolitics October 12; Decision Desk HQ October 15; Silver Bulletin October 15.[^41] South Carolina entered the table because Inside Elections moved it to lean Republican, and Kansas and Minnesota moved within the table because Sabato revised them.[^2][^41]

A word on what the columns mean. **PVI** is the Cook Partisan Voting Index, and it is the only backward-looking number here. It measures how far a state leans from the nation as a whole, computed from the 2020 and 2024 presidential two-party vote shares weighted toward 2024, and Cook says of it that the race ratings reflect an outlook for which party will win the next election, while the PVI takes a longer view. It earns its place as contrast, which is what makes "Tossup in an R+6 state" legible, and not as a forecast. Lean is a strong prior across the whole map: Pew found 158 of 176 Senate elections since 2012, 89.8 percent, went the way of the state's presidential carry. But applied as the sole predictor to the competitive races this series tracks, lean calls five correctly and misses four, Georgia, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas, every one of which it would call Republican and every one of which the forecasters now favor for the Democrat. That is not a contradiction but a selection effect: a race earns its way onto this table precisely by being a case where the baseline does not settle it. The rule that follows is simple, and it is the reason the PVI column sits first and means least: never argue from PVI against the forecasters, because where the two disagree, the forecasters are the point of the exercise.

There are two traps in reading that column, and both make it look inconsistent with numbers quoted elsewhere. The first is that PVI is denominated in **vote share for one party, not margin**, which makes it roughly half the size of a margin-style lean: Cook calls Texas R+6 while Silver's partisan lean calls the same state R+11, and both are right, because the denominators differ. Never place a PVI figure beside a margin figure without saying which is which. The second is that the scale flattens **elasticity**, how hard a state swings when the nation swings, and elasticity is orthogonal to lean, correlating with it at only r = +0.12 across these races. New Hampshire, at 1.15, and Maine, at 1.13, are among the most elastic states in the country, with many cross-pressured moderates who swing hard; Georgia, at 0.90, is among the most inelastic, with a bifurcated electorate and few persuadable voters, which is why it can be rated comfortably Democratic while leaning Republican on the baseline. Texas, at 1.03, is dead average. A state can be strongly partisan and highly elastic at once, which is why a lean column can never stand in for a competitiveness column.

**IE** is Inside Elections, Nathan Gonzales's nonpartisan newsletter, one forecaster among several. The rating scale runs, strongest to weakest, solid, likely, lean, tilt, and toss-up, and **Tilt** is Inside Elections' own rating: Cook, Sabato and Decision Desk HQ do not use it, so a Tilt appearing only in the IE column is not a disagreement but a finer scale. Decision Desk HQ publishes win probabilities that map onto the same ladder: above 95 percent is solid, 80 to 95 likely, 65 to 80 lean, and 35 to 65 a toss-up for either party. **(flip)** marks a rating that forecasts a change of party from the current holder.

**A note on the sources.** One column in that table is arithmetic and six are not. PVI alone can be reproduced from certified FEC totals, which we have done, matching Cook's published figures; everything else is somebody's assessment. Cook, Inside Elections, Sabato and RealClearPolitics are analyst judgment, which means their ratings cannot be independently reproduced from any underlying data, because the rating is the data. That is expert opinion in a table rather than a measurement, and it is not a scandal: election analysis is not a solved problem. RealClearPolitics is worse than unaudited, because it blocks automated reading, so we cannot say whether that column is independent judgment or a composite of the others; treat it as unlisted rather than as a seventh opinion. Decision Desk HQ and Silver are published models, and Decision Desk HQ is the most independent column here, using neither expert ratings nor Cook's PVI and deriving its prior from the most recent presidential margin adjusted for the national swing. Silver's FLIPR is not independent in the same way, because in its default build it feeds the Cook, Inside Elections and Sabato ratings in as an input at roughly one-sixth weight, so where his column agrees with theirs the agreement is partly built in and is not corroboration. Silver has acknowledged the recursion risk himself, observing that if expert raters calibrate against his forecast, the whole process becomes somewhat recursive. All six read the same polls and talk to the same operatives, which means six agreeing columns are not six confirmations; the signal is where they disagree, and this week that is Kansas, Minnesota, Nebraska and Texas. Freshness differs per column and is stated above rather than averaged; Inside Elections' column is now a week fresher than Cook's, which is the reverse of the usual pattern and the reason its five moves lead this report.

## The Close

Sixteen days is enough time for one more turn of the wheel, and this week supplied the clearest picture yet of the direction in which the wheel is turning. The money says the Democratic coalition is engaged: four dollars raised for every Republican dollar in the competitive races, and fourteen of fifteen races won on the ledger. The raters say the map has moved: nine of eleven changes toward the Democrats, and a Republican path that now runs through four toss-ups rather than three. The markets say the last mile is uphill: Kansas still 65 percent Republican, Iowa still 58 percent Republican, Nebraska still 75 percent Republican, even as the national price for Democratic control rises.

All three can be true at once, and the way they fit together is the story of the cycle. A party can win the money and the argument and still lose a seat because the state's baseline is heavy, the incumbent's cash is banked, and the voters who decide it are not the voters who donate. That is why Kansas matters more than its one seat: it is the place where the new Democratic money meets the old Republican map, and sixteen days is just enough time to learn which one is stronger.
