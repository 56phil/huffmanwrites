# A Repair Plan

*Working draft. Prepared 2026-09-24. Not for publication.*

*Written for the 48th President, inaugurated January 20, 2027, on the assumption that the person holding the office wants to repair what the 47th broke and is willing to spend their own power to do it. The plan is written so that it works for any such president regardless of party. The variable that matters is not the label; it is whether the president will accept limits on their own office. A repair that depends on the repairer's virtue is not a repair.*

---

## Premise: three instruments, three speeds

The first thing a repair administration must internalize is that "damage" is not one thing. It was inflicted through instruments with different durability, and it can only be undone through matching instruments. Conflating them is how repair administrations waste their first year.

**Tier 1: Executive action.** Executive orders, presidential memoranda, proclamations, emergency declarations. Reversible by the next president's signature within days. This is the fastest tier and the least valuable, because anything undone this way can be redone by the following president. Treat these as clearing operations, not repairs.

**Tier 2: Agency rulemaking.** Rules published in the Federal Register under statutory authority. Not reversible by order. Undoing a final rule generally requires a new notice-and-comment rulemaking, which takes months and is vulnerable to challenge if the agency cannot articulate a reasoned basis for the change. Schedule Policy/Career lives here, and this is the trap most people miss: the executive order that populated it is Tier 1, but the OPM rule that created it is Tier 2, and the MSPB rule that removed the appeal rights is Tier 2 as well.

**Tier 3: Statute and structure.** What Congress enacts, and what the courts construe. This is the only tier that binds a future president. Every repair effort in American history that lasted was made here; nearly every one that failed was attempted in Tier 1.

The historical record on this is unusually clear, and the design lessons are transferable:

- **Specificity beats generality.** Whether a statutory limit binds the executive depends on which of the three *Youngstown* zones Congress's action occupies. A vague or silent statute is read as acquiescence, and acquiescence can harden into constitutional "gloss" over time ([CRS R48524](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48524.html)). A repair statute must prohibit the specific act, in terms, or it will be absorbed.
- **Never let the president hold the only termination lever.** The National Emergencies Act requires renewal by presidential notice, and since the 1985 amendment replaced a concurrent resolution with a joint resolution, termination requires the president's signature. The result: "To date, no national emergency declared under the NEA has been terminated without presidential consent" ([CRS R45618](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45618.html)).
- **Avoid sunsets you cannot control.** The independent counsel statute lapsed in 1999, not because it failed in court but because its recurring reauthorization gave opponents a veto point every few years ([CRS R44857](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R44857.html)).
- **Prefer mechanisms with an external decisionmaker** (a court, an inspector general, a mandatory report) over mechanisms that depend on political will at a single moment.

The plan below is organized by tier, not by topic, because that is the order in which the work can actually be done.

## Phase 0: The things that expire

There is one item on this list that has a hard deadline before most of the rest can even be attempted, and it should be the first thing the new president's lawyers look at on January 20.

### The UNFCCC withdrawal, and the thirty-eight days

On February 27, 2026, the United States notified the depositary of its withdrawal from the 1992 UN Framework Convention on Climate Change. That withdrawal **takes legal effect on February 27, 2027**, one year after notification ([depositary notification C.N.102.2026](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/CN/2026/CN.102.2026-Eng.pdf)).

The new president is inaugurated January 20, 2027. That leaves **thirty-eight days** between inauguration and the moment the withdrawal lands.

Here I have to be careful, because the obvious legal argument is weaker than it looks and I initially got it wrong. Article 68 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties provides that "A notification or instrument provided for in article 65 or 67 may be revoked at any time before it takes effect," and Article 67(2) covers acts carried out "pursuant to the provisions of the treaty," which is what a notice under UNFCCC Article 25 is ([VCLT, Arts. 67–68](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/conventions/1_1_1969.pdf)). **But the United States is not a party to the Vienna Convention.** It signed in 1970 and never ratified; the UN depositary record shows a signature date of 24 April 1970 and no ratification, accession, or succession date ([UN Treaty Collection, chap. XXIII.1](https://treaties.un.org/pages/ViewDetailsIII.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=XXIII-1&chapter=23&Temp=mtdsg3&clang=_en)). So Article 68 does not bind the United States as treaty law.

That does not end the question, but it relocates it. The revocation analysis turns on three things, in order of strength:

1. **The UNFCCC's own text and the depositary's practice.** The Convention's Article 25 sets a one-year clock, and nothing in it addresses revocation. Whether the depositary would accept a revocation before the effective date is a question of depositary practice, not of a treaty the U.S. is party to. This must be answered by the depositary, not assumed.
2. **Customary international law.** Much of the VCLT is treated as declaratory of custom, and Article 68's rule (that a notice may be revoked before it takes effect) reflects the broader principle that a state may change its position until its act is complete. I have not verified that the United States accepts Article 68 specifically as customary law binding on it, and the plan should not assert it without that verification.
3. **Unilateral policy.** A new administration is free to announce that it does not regard the notice as representing U.S. policy, and to resume participation and reporting. That is worth doing regardless of the legal question.

The honest framing for the action item is therefore: **transmit the revocation, request the depositary's position, and get a formal legal opinion, all before February 27.** The reason to move fast is asymmetric cost, not legal certainty. If the revocation is accepted, the U.S. never leaves. If it is contested, the U.S. has lost nothing by trying and the fallback sequence below applies. Waiting, by contrast, forfeits the option entirely.

The stakes are structural, not symbolic. **Article 28(3) of the Paris Agreement provides that any party withdrawing from the Convention "shall be considered as also having withdrawn from this Agreement."** Paris re-entry requires being a UNFCCC party, because Paris is only open to "States... that are Parties to the Convention" (Art. 20(1)). Letting the UNFCCC withdrawal complete makes Paris re-entry *harder*, not merely later.

### The sequence if the window is missed

If February 27 passes, the pathway lengthens but does not close:

1. **Rejoin the UNFCCC.** Deposit an instrument of ratification, acceptance, approval, or accession. Under Article 23(2), the Convention enters into force for that state **ninety days** after deposit ([UNFCCC text](https://unfccc.int/resource/docs/convkp/conveng.pdf)).
2. **Then rejoin Paris.** Deposit an instrument of acceptance. Under Article 21(3), Paris enters into force for that state **thirty days** after deposit.

So the full re-entry path is roughly 120 days *after* a decision that can only be made once the UNFCCC gap is closed. Missing the February window does not forfeit the outcome; it adds four months and a dependency chain.

There is a domestic-law question underneath this that the plan must flag rather than resolve. Whether a president may unilaterally withdraw from a Senate-ratified treaty is contested, and the contest carries into re-entry: if the 1992 Senate resolution of advice and consent is treated as exhausted, re-entry may require a new two-thirds vote. Two experts who have worked this question closely argue the original resolution "remains in effect (unless repealed by the Senate) and provides the legal authority for rejoining by a future President," and list alternatives including a second round of advice and consent or congressional approval ([Just Security, Jan. 14, 2026](https://www.justsecurity.org/128687/implications-us-withdrawal-unfccc/)). The opposing view, that a president "would need to obtain a new approval," would require two-thirds of the Senate, which the same analysis calls "difficult to obtain in the contemporary political climate" ([The Guardian, Jan. 12, 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jan/12/trump-un-climate-treaty-unfccc)).

**Action:** On day one, direct the State Department and the White House Counsel to prepare a revocation of the February 2026 UNFCCC notification, transmit it before February 27, 2027, and request the depositary's position on its effect. Simultaneously request a formal opinion on the surviving-consent question, because the answer determines whether the Senate must be involved and therefore how early the Senate must be engaged.

### UNESCO, and the items already complete

**UNESCO.** The U.S. notified withdrawal effective December 31, 2026, which falls *before* the inauguration ([State Dept., July 22, 2025](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesperson/2025/07/the-united-states-withdraws-from-the-united-nations-educational-scientific-and-cultural-organization-unesco)). This one is unrecoverable by revocation and must be rejoined. The good news is that the mechanism is the simplest on the list: "a state that has withdrawn from the Organization shall simply deposit a new instrument of acceptance in order to resume membership" (UNESCO Constitution, Art. XV(2); I reached that text through a reader proxy after the direct page timed out repeatedly, so a reader should confirm it at source rather than take the link as evidence). No negotiation, no admission vote. Note also that arrears carry: "No such withdrawal shall affect the financial obligations owed to the Organization on the date the withdrawal takes effect." Budget for them.

**Paris.** Effective January 27, 2026, already complete ([C.N.71.2025](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/CN/2025/CN.71.2025-Eng.pdf)). Re-entry follows the UNFCCC sequencing above.

**WHO.** The U.S. asserts it "formally exited from WHO on January 22, 2026" ([CDC republishing HHS](https://www.cdc.gov/global-health/resources/us-withdrawal-who.html)). The legal position is messier than the assertion, and the mess is worth understanding because it may be an advantage. The WHO Constitution contains **no withdrawal clause**; the U.S. right to leave rests entirely on a reservation Congress attached at acceptance in 1948, which conditioned the right on one year's notice **and** full payment of U.S. financial obligations for the Organization's current fiscal year ([WHO EB158/45, Jan. 20, 2026](https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/EB158/B158_45-en.pdf)). The WHO Secretariat's own report notes that "there has therefore never been an effective withdrawal from the Organization," and leaves open "whether the language... constitutes a condition precedent."

The Director-General stated in April 2026 that the withdrawal was conditioned on two things, and that the second was unmet: "One is notification one year in advance, which is actually met. And the second is paying the arrears, so we hope they will do that but we haven't received anything yet" ([AFP via France 24](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20260429-departing-us-still-owes-money-says-who-chief)). And the 2026 World Health Assembly decided that if the United States is still in arrears at the opening of WHA80 in 2027, "their voting privileges shall be suspended as from the said opening" ([WHA79.2](https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/WHA79/A79_R2-en.pdf)).

Read that carefully, because it cuts both ways. If the withdrawal was never legally effective, the U.S. remains a member and can resume participation by paying its arrears. If it was effective, re-admission requires an application approved by a simple majority of the Health Assembly and then deposit of a formal instrument of acceptance ([WHO Constitution, Arts. 6 and 79(b)](https://apps.who.int/gb/bd/pdf_files/BD_49th-en.pdf)). Either way the arrears must be paid, and paying them is also what restores voting rights. **Action:** request an opinion on whether the U.S. remains a member, then pay the arrears and resume participation by whichever route the opinion identifies. The WHO has publicly invited this: "We hope that in the future, the United States will return to active participation in WHO" ([WHO statement, Jan. 24, 2026](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-01-2026-who-statement-on-notification-of-withdrawal-of-the-united-states)).

**The 66-body memorandum.** The January 7, 2026 presidential memorandum directed agencies to withdraw from 35 non-UN and 31 UN organizations ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/01/withdrawing-the-united-states-from-international-organizations-conventions-and-treaties-that-are-contrary-to-the-interests-of-the-united-states/)). For UN entities it defined withdrawal functionally: "withdrawal means ceasing participation in or funding to those entities to the extent permitted by law." That definition is the vulnerability. Most of these are directives rather than deposited treaty notices, and directives are Tier 1. **Action:** inventory which bodies received actual depositary notices and which only received a directive. Revoke the directive; resume participation and funding in the bodies where a directive was the whole of the act; treat the remainder case by case.

**New START.** Expired February 5, 2026, by its own terms, with no successor ([ICAN](https://www.icanw.org/new_start_expiration)). This is not a withdrawal to reverse; it is a negotiation to open. The Secretary-General's framing is the right one: "For the first time in more than half a century, we face a world without any binding limits on the strategic nuclear arsenals" ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/02/1166892)). A successor instrument requires Senate advice and consent. Start the negotiation early, because it is the longest-lead item on this list.

## Phase 1: Unilateral reversal, and its limits

These are the acts the president can take alone. They should be done quickly and understood as clearing, not as repair.

**Terminate the emergency declarations.** The 2025 declarations are the clearest abuse of the NEA's design. They come in bundles: a single continuation notice signed January 12, 2026 renewed five emergencies at once, including the southern border declaration (Proc. 10886) and four related orders ([91 FR 1665](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/14/2026-00731/continuation-of-the-national-emergencies-with-respect-to-the-southern-border-of-the-united-states)). The energy emergency (EO 14156) was likewise continued ([91 FR 1667](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/14/2026-00732/continuation-of-the-national-emergency-with-respect-to-energy)). Both next come up January 20, 2027.

There is an instructive detail in how the administration handled its own tariff emergencies. When it ended certain tariff actions in February 2026, it expressly preserved the emergencies: the declarations "remain in effect and shall not be affected by this order" ([EO 14389, 91 FR 9437](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/25/2026-03832/ending-certain-tariff-actions)). The emergencies were kept alive after their purpose ended. That is the pattern to reverse, and it is also the argument for the statutory fix in Phase 2: this is what the design permits.

**Note the D.C. item that already resolved itself, and the one that did not.** EO 14333 (August 11, 2025) federalized the Metropolitan Police under §740 of the Home Rule Act. That authority is capped at thirty days absent a joint resolution, and it lapsed September 10, 2025 when Congress did not extend it ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/trump-washington-dc-guard-police-crime-cd2bc19a0c6b7e4bf3a2e1da6c57ce6e); D.C. Code [§1-207.40](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-207.40)). The statute worked. But the National Guard deployment rested on separate orders and continued: the Secretary of the Army extended it to November 30, 2025, then Defense to February 28, 2026 ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/national-guard-trump-deployment-washington-cbae3a840cec29b0d361413fbe162e14)). A federal district court found the deployment exceeded statutory authority and enjoined it, then the D.C. Circuit stayed the injunction pending appeal, reasoning the president "possesses a unique power within the District" ([District of Columbia v. Trump](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10759905/dc-v-donald-trump/)). The appeal's disposition was not located, so the current status is open. **Action:** withdraw the deployment orders as a matter of policy, and do not rely on the litigation outcome.

**Rescind the Schedule Policy/Career orders, and understand what that does not accomplish.** The executive orders are Tier 1 and can be revoked. But the operative machinery is Tier 2. The OPM final rule ([2026-02375](https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2026-02375.json), effective March 9, 2026) created the schedule and made the positions "at-will... excepted from adverse action procedures or appeals." The MSPB then removed its own jurisdiction by rule: its "Appellate Jurisdiction Update" ([2026-03594](https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2026-03594.json)) "amending its regulations to remove certain appeal rights... This rule reflects the Office of Personnel Management's recission of these rights." EO 14410 placed roughly 8,000 positions into the schedule ([Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/workforce/2026/06/trump-moves-about-8000-federal-positions-to-schedule-policy-career/)).

Revoking the EO does not repeal either rule. Both must be unwound by new rulemaking, and both must survive the reasoned-basis requirement. **Action:** initiate notice-and-comment to rescind both rules, and pair it with the statutory fix in Phase 2, because rulemaking alone leaves the same structure available to the next administration.

**Restore inspectors general, and then protect them.** IG independence rests on a real statutory foundation: the IG Act created offices that report both to the agency head and to Congress, and the statute provides that neither the agency head nor the next-ranking officer "shall prevent or prohibit the Inspector General from initiating, carrying out, or completing any audit or investigation" ([5 U.S.C. §403](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/403)). Removals require that the President communicate the reasons in writing to both Houses of Congress not later than 30 days before the removal. **Action:** fill every vacancy with a career-qualified appointment, and publish the substantive rationale for every removal the administration makes, because the reporting requirement is the mechanism and it only works if it is used.

**Immigration: the largest unilateral reversal available, and it splits by instrument.** The Alien Enemies Act proclamation (Proc. 10903, March 14, 2025) is Tier 1 and can be revoked by proclamation, which removes the legal basis for the removals conducted under it ([Proclamation 10903](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/03/20/2025-04865/invocation-of-the-alien-enemies-act-regarding-the-invasion-of-the-united-states-by-tren-de-aragua)). The Temporary Protected Status terminations are Tier 2: the Haiti termination, for instance, took effect February 3, 2026 by Federal Register notice, and reversing it requires a new designation ([Federal Register notice terminating Haiti's designation](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/11/28/2025-21379/termination-of-the-designation-of-haiti-for-temporary-protected-status)). The asylum changes are Tier 2 and harder: the DHS rule permitting referrals without interview (effective July 28, 2026) and the "Security Bars" rule (effective December 31, 2025) both require notice-and-comment to unwind ([2026-15190](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/28/2026-15190/affirmative-asylum-referrals-without-interview); [2025-23970](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/30/2025-23970/security-bars-and-processing-confirmation-of-effective-date-partial-withdrawal)). The travel-ban proclamations are Tier 1. The 287(g) expansion and the detention buildout are administrative and contractual, not proclamation-based, and reversing the scale of the latter is a budget question, not a legal one. **Action:** revoke the proclamations immediately; begin rulemaking on the asylum rules; and treat the TPS restorations as a case-by-case sequence, because each is a separate designation with its own statutory clock.

**Health: reverse the rules, and respect the litigation that is already working.** The January 5, 2026 childhood immunization schedule and the reconstituted ACIP's votes after June 11, 2025 are already stayed by a district court in *American Academy of Pediatrics v. Kennedy*, which postponed the schedule's effective date, the appointments of 13 ACIP members, and all committee votes after that date ([CRS IN12684](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12684)). The correct posture is to stop defending the changes and let the stay do its work, then reconstitute the committee through the ordinary process. The mifepristone REMS review is a Tier 2 proceeding and can be withdrawn. **Action:** withdraw the government's defense of the stayed schedule, withdraw the REMS review, and nominate a full committee whose members can be confirmed on professional qualifications rather than reviewed for their conclusions.

## Phase 2: The statutory repair

This is the only phase that matters in the long run. Everything above can be undone by the next president in a week. Nothing below can.

### The National Emergencies Act

The design flaw is documented and specific. The NEA requires an annual continuation notice, and termination requires a joint resolution, which means the president's signature. In practice: no emergency has ever been terminated without presidential consent, and the average emergency lasts more than nine years ([CRS R45618](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45618.html)). As of September 2026 there were 43 continuation notices published in the prior twelve months, covering 45 distinct subjects.

The reform that addresses this is already drafted, and it is bipartisan. The **ARTICLE ONE Act** was reintroduced August 20, 2026 by Reps. Roy and Cohen ([H.R. 10132](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/10132)). Its operative mechanism inverts the default:

> "A declaration of a national emergency shall remain in effect for a period of 30 calendar days from the issuance of the proclamation... and shall terminate when such period expires unless there is enacted into law a joint resolution of approval" ([bill text](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr10132/BILLS-119hr10132ih.htm)).

Renewal follows the same logic: the president publishes a renewal order *and* Congress must enact a joint resolution of approval. And it closes the obvious workaround: if approval is not enacted within 30 days, "the President may not, during the remainder of the term of office of that President, declare a subsequent national emergency under subsection (a) with respect to the same circumstances." The bill also uses expedited Senate procedures, discharging a committee after ten calendar days and waiving points of order, which is the part that makes the mechanism real rather than nominal.

An alternative with a different design is the **National Emergencies Reform Act** ([H.R. 3908](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/3908)), which also requires approval, adds a five-year automatic termination, and requires disclosure of Presidential Emergency Action Documents: "each of the approximately 56 documents described as presidential emergency action documents in the budget justification materials for the Office of Legal Counsel."

Either vehicle works. The essential features are: **affirmative congressional approval to sustain an emergency, expedited procedures so approval is possible, a bar on re-declaring the same emergency, and application to existing emergencies.** The last one matters most; a reform that grandfathers the current 45 subjects leaves the problem intact.

### The civil service

The **Saving the Civil Service Act** ([S.134](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s134is/html/BILLS-119s134is.htm) / [H.R. 492](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr492ih/pdf/BILLS-119hr492ih.pdf)) is the direct answer. Its approach is to freeze the boundaries of the excepted service to what they were before the abuse began:

> "A position in the competitive service may not be excepted from the competitive service unless that position is placed—(1) in any of schedules A through E, as described in section 6.2 of title 5, Code of Federal Regulations, as in effect on September 30, 2020..."

It adds a numeric cap and an employee-consent requirement: during any four-year term an agency may not transfer more than one percent of its employees or five employees, whichever is greater, and "an employee who occupies a position in the competitive service may not be transferred to the excepted service without the prior written consent of the employee."

The reference to a fixed prior date is the design feature worth preserving. It does not attempt to define the boundary prospectively, which would invite re-litigation; it pins it to a state of the world that already existed.

### The MSPB, and the removal-power problem

The Board's own data shows the damage: 9,457 open cases as of July 1, 2026, with an average of 165.91 open cases per administrative judge against a historical norm of "25-30 open cases" ([MSPB](https://www.mspb.gov/Number_of_Open_Cases_ORO.pdf)); 249 petitions for review awaiting Board action, up from zero in October 2024 ([MSPB](https://www.mspb.gov/Pending%20PFR%20Data_9.1.2026.pdf)). The Board lost quorum from April 10 to October 28, 2025 and could not issue final decisions in that window ([MSPB quorum FAQ](https://www.mspb.gov/FAQs%20Absence%20of%20Board%20Quorum%20and%20Board%20Status%208-28-28.pdf)).

The **FAST Justice Act** ([H.R. 5724](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr5724ih/html/BILLS-119hr5724ih.htm)) addresses the backlog by giving employees an exit: if the Board "has not taken an action with respect to the appeal that is subject to judicial review" within 120 days, the employee may file a civil action, and the Board stays its own appeal. The **Protecting Our Democracy Act** ([S. 2838](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2838/text)) goes further, adding a 180-day route to de novo district court review with a jury right, and expanding whistleblower protections.

But the plan must be honest about a constraint that no statute in this list can overcome. The for-cause removal protection for MSPB members was struck down by the D.C. Circuit: "Congress may not restrict the President's ability to remove MSPB members" ([*Harris v. Bessent*](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/12/25-5037_25-5057.pdf)), cert denied. Then the Supreme Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor* in *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 29, 2026), holding that officials within the president's "general administrative control" must be removable at will ([opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_qn12.pdf)). The MSPB has stopped describing itself as independent: "The word 'independent' is being removed from public-facing agency descriptions in light of recent legal developments and the Supreme Court's decision in Slaughter" ([Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/management/2026/07/after-slaughter-decision-mspb-no-longer-calling-itself-independent/)).

This changes what a repair can be. **Insulation by removal protection is no longer available for officers of this kind.** The repair must therefore be designed around appointment and process rather than tenure: fixed terms with staggered expiration, bipartisan balance requirements, mandatory reporting, and, most importantly, an external route to court when the agency does not act. That is precisely what the FAST Justice Act and the S. 2838 180-day provision do, and it is why they matter more than they appear to. The D.C. Circuit found the post-1999 regulatory special-counsel arrangement weaker than the statutory independent counsel precisely because "the Attorney General generally appoints, supervises, and may remove special counsels" ([CRS R44857](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R44857.html)). After *Slaughter*, any new insulation scheme must survive a formalist Article II challenge, not merely the functional reasoning of *Morrison*.

### What to ask Congress for, in one sentence each

1. **ARTICLE ONE Act or H.R. 3908**, applied to existing emergencies as well as future ones.
2. **Saving the Civil Service Act**, with the September 30, 2020 boundary date and the consent requirement.
3. **FAST Justice Act**, plus the S. 2838 judicial-review provision, as the substitute for removal protection.
4. **IG Act amendments** requiring personal presidential notice and reasons, with a private right of action for retaliatory removal.
5. **Impoundment Control Act enforcement with teeth.** The Comptroller General issued a series of decisions finding ICA violations, including DOT withholding NEVI funds ([B-337137](https://www.gao.gov/products/b-337137)), IMLS ([B-337375](https://www.gao.gov/products/b-337375)), HHS on Head Start ([B-337202](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/880226.pdf)), DOE ([B-337208](https://www.gao.gov/products/b-337208)), NIH ([B-337203](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/880607.pdf)), and FEMA ([B-337204.1](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/881507.pdf)). GAO's framing of the principle is the one to legislate: "The Constitution grants the President no unilateral authority to withhold funds from obligation." The obstacle is remedial rather than doctrinal: the D.C. Circuit held that grantees "lack a cause of action to press their claims" under the ICA because "the ICA precludes such review" ([*Global Health Council*](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10662777/global-health-council-v-donald-j-trump-amended-opinion/)). Congress should create the cause of action it withheld in 1974, and should close the "pocket rescission" route that GAO identified as also inconsistent with the Act: the ICA "does not authorize the withholding of budget authority through its date of expiration."

## Phase 3: Litigation posture

A repair administration holds a lever that requires no legislation: it decides what the government argues in court. Several of the most consequential pending cases turn on positions the executive has taken, and those positions can be withdrawn.

**Schedule Policy/Career.** Four cases are pending with no merits ruling: *PEER v. Trump* (D. Md., Judge Xinis), *NTEU v. Trump* (D.D.C.), *Government Accountability Project v. OPM*, and *NTEU v. OPM* (FOIA) ([PEER docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69586644/public-employees-for-environmental-responsibility-v-trump/); [NTEU docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69560537/national-treasury-employees-union-v-trump/)). The government's defense is a legal position, not a fact. A new administration can decline to defend the schedule and concede the relief plaintiffs seek, which would resolve the matter faster than rulemaking.

**Impoundment.** The same is true of the withholding cases. The administration told a district court it "continue[s] to intend to allow a substantial portion of those funds to expire" ([*Global Health Council*](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10665613/global-health-council-v-donald-j-trump/)). Releasing the funds moots the dispute and is the correct remedy regardless of litigation.

**Emergency powers.** Withdraw the substantive defenses that assume broad emergency authority, and let the statutory fix in Phase 2 do the durable work.

---

## Phase 4: Capacity

Reversing policies does not restore the ability to make policy. Four years of attrition has to be rebuilt, and the rebuild is slower than the damage because it requires hiring, clearances, and institutional memory.

- **Rehire where the law allows.** OPM's own data shows a net loss of 271,363 federal employees since January 20, 2025, with 139,963 separations through the Deferred Resignation Program ([OPM Federal Workforce Data](https://data.opm.gov/explore-data/analytics/workforce-changes)). Note the data's own caveat: the July 2026 figures are incomplete because a Department of War processing failure left roughly 6,211 records outstanding, so treat these as floors rather than finals.
- **Restore scientific capacity.** Science agencies lost about 20 percent of staff, with 5,844 NIH grants and 1,996 NSF grants cancelled or suspended ([*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/immersive/d41586-026-00088-9/index.html)). NSF obligations are running at $6.3 billion against $8.1 billion the prior year ([*Inside Higher Ed*](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/government/science-research-policy/2026/09/21/nsf-over-billion-behind-grants-ies-says-will)). Rebuilding means restoring the grant pipeline, not just the headcount.
- **Restore the research offices in the agencies.** EPA dismantled the Office of Research and Development and staffing fell to about 12,700 ([Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/management/2026/05/epa-producing-less-scientific-research-after-20-staffing-cut-data-shows/)).
- **Rebuild data integrity.** The apportionment website was removed and only restored after litigation ([GAO letter to OMB](https://www.gao.gov/products/e12062)). Statutory reporting obligations are the repair here, and they should be written with automatic publication requirements so the next removal is a contempt, not a policy choice.
- **Restore the records.** The Presidential Records Act already provides that the United States "shall reserve and retain complete ownership, possession, and control of Presidential records" ([44 U.S.C. §2202](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/44/2202)). The failure has been access, not ownership: "successive presidential Administrations have interpreted its meaning differently" ([CRS R40238](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R40238.html)). Amend it to place access disputes before a neutral decisionmaker rather than leaving interpretation to each administration.

## What 1978 got right, and what 1999 got wrong

The repair administrations worth studying are the post-Watergate ones, because they faced a broken presidency and produced structures that mostly held. Four of them still work, and it is worth being precise about why.

The **Inspector General Act of 1978** worked because it created units that report to Congress as well as to the agency, gave them a statutory purpose ("to prevent and detect fraud and abuse"), and made removal costly by requiring published reasons. Its independence has survived because it does not depend on tenure; it depends on disclosure.

**FISA (1978)** worked because it routed the executive's most sensitive authority through an external decisionmaker, the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court. The statute's protective core survived decades of amendment, and its most contested expansions were eventually permitted to lapse. The lesson: judicial authorization is durable in a way political oversight is not.

The **Presidential Records Act (1978)** has held on ownership and failed on access, which is the precise lesson for Phase 4.

The **National Emergencies Act of 1976** is the cautionary case, and it is worth studying because it was designed thoughtfully and still failed. It terminated four outstanding emergencies and forced the dormant return of delegated powers. But the renewal mechanism required presidential notice, and the 1985 amendment after *INS v. Chadha* replaced a concurrent resolution with a joint resolution, adding the president's signature to the termination path. Every element of the design was sound except one: the exit was in the wrong hands.

The **independent counsel statute** is the failure to learn from. It lapsed in 1999, not by court order but by exhaustion; the investigations of Iran-Contra and Whitewater made reauthorization politically impossible, and because the statute expired on a five-year cycle it needed reauthorization again and again, each round a veto point. Its replacement is regulatory and weaker by design. The lesson is not that insulation is futile. It is that **a repair which must be renewed has an expiration date, and its opponents know when it is.**

---

## What this plan cannot do

Three honest limits, because a plan that claims more than it can deliver is the thing it is trying to repair.

**It cannot restore what the Supreme Court has removed.** After *Slaughter*, for-cause removal protection is unavailable for a large class of officers. Any repair that assumed otherwise is written against a constitution that no longer exists. The workarounds (staggered terms, bipartisan balance, mandatory process, and above all a private right to go to court when an agency refuses to act) are weaker than what was lost. They should be adopted anyway, and they should not be described as equivalent.

**It cannot legislate norms.** The most important damage of the last several years is not in the statute book. It is the normalization of the idea that a president may withhold appropriated funds, ignore a court's question, or treat an agency as personal property. Statutes can make specific conduct costly. They cannot make it shameful again. That work belongs to a different set of institutions.

**It cannot be self-enforcing.** Every recommendation above is a Tier 3 fix adopted by an administration that could, if it chose, ignore all of them. The reason to write them specifically, to pin them to fixed dates, to give them expedited procedures, and to create causes of action is that the next president may be no better than this one. A repair is only a repair if it is designed for the person who does not want to follow it.

That is the standard by which the 48th President's work should be judged, and it is the standard the 1976 Act failed and the 1978 Act met.
